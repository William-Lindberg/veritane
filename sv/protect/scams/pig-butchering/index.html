Scam patterns

“Pig butchering”: long-con investment scams

A stranger builds a friendship or romance over weeks or months, then introduces a “profitable” trading platform; the target deposits more and more until withdrawals are blocked.

Updated 6 November 2026

Key points

  • The name comes from the scammers' own slang: the target is “fattened” with attention and fake profits before the money is taken.
  • First contact is often a “wrong number” text or a message on social media, a dating app or a game.
  • Small early profits, and sometimes a small withdrawal, build trust before larger deposits.
  • The trading platform or app is fake; the balance on screen is invented.
  • Requests to pay a “tax” or “fee” before a withdrawal lead to more losses, not to a payout.
  • “Recovery” firms that appear afterwards are, in documented cases, run by the same people.

Warning signs

  • A friendly message from a stranger who seems to have contacted the wrong number and keeps chatting.
  • A move from the original app to a private messaging app.
  • A new friend who is wealthy, successful and keen to teach you to trade.
  • A trading app or website you have not heard of, recommended only by that person.
  • A balance that grows steadily, followed by a block on withdrawals until a payment is made.
  • Pressure to borrow, use savings or pension money to “not miss the window”.

How it works, step by step

  1. The opening. A message arrives, apparently by mistake, or a stranger starts a conversation on social media, a dating app or a game.
  2. The relationship. Weeks of friendly or romantic messages follow. Scammers try to move the chat to a private messaging app (Scamwatch).
  3. The introduction. The new friend mentions their success with crypto or currency trading and offers to show how it works. Scamwatch calls this approach, building a relationship before asking someone to invest in a fake opportunity, a “romance baiting” scam (Scamwatch).
  4. The first profits. The target opens an account on a recommended platform. Small deposits show quick gains, and a small withdrawal may be allowed (Scamwatch).
  5. The bigger bets. Encouraged by the “profits”, the target deposits savings or borrowed money.
  6. The block. A larger withdrawal is refused until a “tax”, “fee” or “deposit” is paid. Each payment leads to another request.
  7. The second wave. After contact stops, someone offers to recover the money for a fee.

Why it is hard to see from inside

The scam does not start with money. By the time the platform appears, the target trusts the person and has seen what looks like real profit. Scammers can spend a long time building that trust (Scamwatch). Victims are not at fault: these operations are organised, scripted and practised.

Safety facts

  • Money sent to someone you have never met in person is at very high risk of loss; a request for it is the standard final step of long-con scams. (Scamwatch)
  • An online contact you have never met who starts talking to you about investing is a warning sign of an investment scam. (Scamwatch)
  • Do not pay a “tax” or “fee” to release a withdrawal; when scammers allow small withdrawals and then block larger ones, the money is lost. (Scamwatch)
  • Recovery services that contact you after a loss are, in documented cases, run by the same people behind the original scam. (FCA)

Where to report

The general route is: your bank or the exchange used to send money, then the police or national fraud service. Examples:

  • United States: the FBI’s Internet Crime Complaint Center (ic3.gov) and the Federal Trade Commission (reportfraud.ftc.gov).
  • United Kingdom: Report Fraud in England and Wales or Police Scotland in Scotland (GOV.UK).
  • Sweden: the police on 114 14 (Polisen).
  • Australia: Scamwatch (scamwatch.gov.au).

What people commonly do next

  • People who realise they are in this scam commonly stop sending money and contact their bank or exchange straight away.
  • Keeping screenshots of the chats, the platform, wallet addresses and payment receipts helps the police and the bank.
  • Losses are reported to the police or national fraud service; reports help investigators link cases.
  • Talking to someone trusted, or a victim-support service, helps; scammers rely on isolation and shame.
