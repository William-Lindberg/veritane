Consumer rights

Making a complaint and escalating it

The usual ladder for a consumer problem: the company's own complaints process, then a free ombudsman or dispute body, a regulator, and, as a last step, a small-claims court.

Updated 6 November 2026

Key points

  • Almost every route starts with a complaint to the company itself, and dispute bodies generally check that this happened.
  • A written complaint that says what went wrong, what you want and by when is easier to follow up than a phone call.
  • Firms in regulated sectors, such as finance, often have a fixed time to respond.
  • Independent ombudsman and dispute-resolution schemes are free or low cost for consumers in many countries; their names vary.
  • Small-claims court procedures are built for individuals and often do not need a lawyer.

Not legal advice

This page describes the general shape of complaint routes and gives examples. It is not legal advice. Time limits and the right body depend on the country and the sector.

The ladder

  1. The company’s complaints process. Most problems are settled here. The complaint names the product, the date, what went wrong and what would put it right (a repair, a refund, a correction, an apology).
  2. A final answer or a deadline. The company replies with a decision, or the time it is allowed to take runs out.
  3. An independent body. An ombudsman, an alternative dispute resolution (ADR) body or a consumer centre looks at the case. These are free or low cost for consumers in many countries.
  4. A regulator. Regulators use complaints to spot patterns and act against firms; many of them do not resolve individual cases.
  5. Court. Small-claims procedures are designed for individuals and modest amounts.

Writing it down

A dispute is easier to win with documents. The Financial Literacy Framework lists the papers worth keeping: signed agreements and terms, written messages with the company, proof of payments (statements and receipts) and any refusal letters (Financial Literacy Framework). Ombudsman services make the same point: a note of calls, emails and events supports the complaint and helps them work faster (Financial Ombudsman Service).

Varies by country: examples

  • European Union: contacting the trader directly is a necessary first step before using an out-of-court ADR body. Each EU country has ADR bodies that can propose or impose a solution at low cost, with an outcome within 90 days; whether the trader has to take part depends on national law (Your Europe). For a trader in another EU country, Norway or Iceland, the European Consumer Centre in your own country can help (European Commission). Cross-border claims of up to €5,000 can use the European Small Claims Procedure without a lawyer (Your Europe).
  • United Kingdom: a financial business generally has up to eight weeks to consider a complaint, or 15 business days (up to 35 business days in exceptional cases) for complaints about payment services, fraud and scams; after its final response, the consumer has six months to take the complaint to the Financial Ombudsman Service (Financial Ombudsman Service). Ombudsman schemes are independent, free and impartial, and do not investigate a case once court action has started (Citizens Advice). Money claims can be made online at a county court, which may offer free mediation (GOV.UK).
  • United States: USAGov explains how to complain to a company and which federal or state agency can help when that fails (USAGov). Complaints about banking and credit products can go to the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau when contacting the bank directly has not helped (USAGov), and each state has a consumer protection office that handles complaints against businesses (USAGov).

What people commonly do next

  • People with a complaint commonly write to the company's complaints address, stating the facts, the remedy they want and a reasonable deadline.
  • A log of dates, names, reference numbers and copies of every message is kept from the start.
  • If the company does not resolve it, or does not reply in time, the complaint goes to the relevant ombudsman or dispute body.
  • Court is the last step, and many courts offer or require mediation first.
