Consumer rights

Cooling-off periods

Time-limited rights to cancel certain contracts without giving a reason: purchases made at a distance or at the door, and some credit agreements.

Updated 6 November 2026

Key points

  • A cooling-off period lets a buyer cancel within a set time without giving a reason and without the item being faulty.
  • It applies mainly where buyers could not inspect the product or were approached without asking: online, by phone, at the doorstep.
  • Typical windows are 14 days in the EU and the UK for distance and doorstep sales; some countries allow longer for doorstep selling.
  • Many credit agreements carry their own right to withdraw.
  • There are listed exceptions, such as perishable goods, made-to-order items, dated bookings and services already fully provided at the buyer's request.

Not legal advice

This page explains general principles and gives examples. It is not legal advice. Cooling-off rules depend on the country, the type of contract and how it was made; a national consumer authority can say how they apply.

Why cooling-off periods exist

Some sales happen in conditions where a careful decision is hard: the buyer cannot see or try the product, or a seller arrives unannounced and asks for a decision on the spot. A cooling-off period gives time to think after the contract is made. It is different from a right based on a fault: the product can be perfect and the buyer can still cancel.

The principles that hold broadly

  • It is time-limited. The clock starts on a defined day (delivery for goods, the contract date for services) and runs for a fixed number of days.
  • No reason is needed. The buyer does not have to explain.
  • It is exercised by telling the seller. A clear statement, ideally in writing, is what counts; simply not using the product is generally not enough.
  • There are exceptions. Items that perish, are made to order, are unsealed digital media or are booked for a specific date are commonly excluded.
  • Sellers have duties too. Sellers are generally required to tell buyers about the right and the exceptions before the sale.

Varies by country: examples

  • European Union: goods or services bought online, by phone or at the doorstep can be cancelled within 14 days without justification; for goods, from the date of delivery, and for services, from the day the contract was agreed. Some EU countries give 30 days for contracts made during unsolicited doorstep selling. Exceptions include travel and event bookings for specific dates, perishable goods, made-to-order goods, unsealed software or media, and services fully provided after the buyer agreed to start immediately (Your Europe).
  • European Union, credit: a consumer credit agreement can be withdrawn from within 14 calendar days of signing, without explanation; the borrower repays the money borrowed plus interest for the days it was held (Your Europe).
  • United Kingdom: items bought without being seen in person carry a 14-day cooling-off period that starts the day after delivery; after telling the seller, the buyer has another 14 days to send the item back (Citizens Advice).
  • United States: cooling-off rights are narrower and depend on the type of sale and the state. One federal example is the right to cancel certain home-secured loans, such as a home-equity loan on a main home, within three business days under the Truth in Lending Act (Regulation Z, 12 CFR 1026.23).

Purchases from private individuals, for example on marketplaces, are generally not covered by cooling-off rights (Your Europe).

What people commonly do next

  • People who cancel within a cooling-off period commonly do it in writing (email, letter or the seller's form) and keep a copy with the date.
  • Goods are returned within the time the rules allow after cancelling; the terms say who pays the return postage.
  • If a refund does not arrive, the card provider or a consumer advice service is the usual next step.
