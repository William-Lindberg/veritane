Report it

The route people commonly take, in this order:

  1. The bank or card provider, on the number printed on the card or on the bank’s own website, as soon as money or account details have been shared.
  2. The police.
  3. The national fraud or consumer body.

Examples of national reporting services:

  • Australia: Scamwatch: report a scam
  • United Kingdom: GOV.UK: report suspicious emails, websites and phishing
  • United States: Federal Trade Commission: ReportFraud

Your numbers

Think of one message, call or offer. Answer each question; “Unsure” is fine. Nothing you choose is stored or sent.

1. Did the contact start without you asking for it (a call, text, email, message or advert)?
2. Is there a deadline or a reason to act straight away?
3. Were you asked to keep it secret from family, friends or your bank?
4. Were you asked for a password, a one-time code or a crypto seed phrase?
5. Were you asked to move money to another account “to keep it safe”?
6. Is payment requested by gift card, crypto, wire transfer or cash handed to a courier?
7. Is a return guaranteed, or described as having no risk?
8. Is there a fee to pay first, to release money, a prize, a job or a loan?
9. Were you asked to install an app or allow remote access to a computer or phone?
10. Does the other person refuse a video call, a meeting or an office address that can be checked?
11. Does the email address, phone number or website differ from the official one?
12. Is there pressure not to talk to anyone else about it?

Antaganden och begränsningar

  • The 12 questions describe patterns that regulators and police list as common in fraud. A situation can be a scam and match none of them.
  • Answers stay on this page: nothing is stored, sent or put in the link.
  • The count is a description of patterns, not a verdict on any person or company.
