Productivity and Potential Growth

Output per hour, why it matters for wages and public finances, and how potential growth is estimated.

Professional·5 min (Simple) · 10 min (Detailed)·Updated 6 November 2026

Read first: BNP: vad det mäter och vad det missar, Labour Market Indicators in Depth

Key points

  • Labour productivity is output per hour worked (or per worker); multifactor productivity also accounts for capital and is measured as a residual.
  • Over long periods, living standards follow productivity; over shorter periods, wages can move apart from it.
  • Productivity is hard to measure in services and the public sector, where output has no simple market price.
  • Potential growth is an estimate of the pace the economy can sustain without rising or falling inflation pressure; it is revised often, and the output gap depends on it.
  • Comparing productivity across countries needs data on hours worked and purchasing power parities.

Simple: plain words. Detailed: exact figures, codes and methods.

In short: productivity is how much is produced for each hour of work. It is the main source of rising living standards over decades, and one of the hardest numbers in economics to measure well.

Simple version

Same hours, more output

ProductivityProductivityOutput per hour or per worker, the main long-run driver of wages and living standards.Read more about Productivity compares what comes out with what goes in. The most common measure is labour productivity: output per hour worked.

Example

A bakery makes 400 loaves in 100 hours of work (example): 4 loaves per hour. The next year a new oven lets the same 100 hours produce 420 loaves: 4.2 loaves per hour. Productivity rose 5% (4.2 ÷ 4 − 1). Nobody worked longer; the hours produced more.

Across a whole economy, “output” is real GDP and “hours” is the total hours everyone worked.

Where productivity growth comes from

Output per hour rises for several reasons. Workers have more or newer equipment. They gain skills and experience. Firms find new ways to organise work, or new technology arrives. Workers move from less productive firms or industries to more productive ones. In practice these happen together, which is one reason the causes of faster or slower productivity growth are debated.

Why it matters

  • Wages. Over long periods, pay can only rise faster than prices if each hour of work produces more. Over a few years, though, wages and productivity can move apart.
  • Public finances. Government budget forecasts depend on how fast the economy can grow. Faster productivity growth means more tax revenue for the same tax rates. That is why productivity assumptions appear in government budget forecasts.
  • Living standards. Countries with higher output per hour tend to have higher incomes, although how that income is shared is a separate question.

Potential growth and the output gap

Economists also estimate potential output: the level of output an economy could sustain without pushing inflation up or down. It depends on how many people can work, how many hours, how much equipment there is and how productively it is used.

The difference between actual and potential output is the output gapOutput gapThe difference between what an economy is producing and what it could produce at full capacity without pushing up inflation.Read more about Output gap.

Example

Actual output is 1,980 and estimated potential is 2,000 (example, ¤ billion). The output gap is (1,980 − 2,000) ÷ 2,000 = −1.0%: the economy is producing a little below what it could sustain. A positive gap would mean output above its sustainable level.

Three reasons the number is uncertain

  1. Some output has no clear price. How much “output” does a hospital, a school or a free online service produce? Statistics offices use careful methods, but the answers are estimates.
  2. Quality changes. A phone that costs the same as last year’s but does more is more output. Measuring that is hard.
  3. Potential cannot be seen. It is estimated from models, and estimates are revised, sometimes by a lot, as new data arrive.

Detailed version

Definitions

  • Labour productivity = real output ÷ labour input, where labour input is hours worked (preferred) or persons employed (Eurostat). Per-hour and per-worker measures diverge when average hours change, for example with more part-time work.
  • Multifactor (total factor) productivity, MFP or TFP, is output growth not explained by measured growth in inputs. It is calculated, not observed (ONS).

Growth accounting

With a Cobb–Douglas production function and capital share α:

g_Y = α·g_K + (1 − α)·g_L + g_A

Example (example values): GDP growth 2.0%, capital services growth 3.0%, hours growth 1.0%, α = 0.35.

g_A = 2.0 − 0.35×3.0 − 0.65×1.0 = 0.30 percentage points
labour productivity growth = 1.02 / 1.01 − 1 ≈ 0.99%

Labour productivity growth here comes from capital deepening (more capital per hour) plus the TFP residual. National statistics offices publish versions with quality-adjusted labour input (accounting for skills and experience) and capital services weighted by asset type (ONS).

Measurement problems

  • Non-market output. Public services are often measured by inputs or by activity counts with quality adjustment, which makes productivity in those sectors partly an assumption of the method.
  • Quality and new goods. Price indices that miss quality improvements overstate inflation and understate real output.
  • Intangibles. Software, data, research and organisational capital are only partly capitalised in national accounts, so both output and capital may be mismeasured.
  • Cross-country comparisons need purchasing power parities rather than market exchange rates (Eurostat) and comparable hours data, which are less reliable than output data. The World Bank publishes GDP per person employed at constant PPP for broad comparisons (World Bank); per-hour comparisons are available mainly for higher-income economies.

Potential output

Two main estimation approaches are used. A production-function approach estimates trend labour, capital and TFP and combines them; the European Commission’s estimates, published in its AMECO database, follow this route (European Commission). Statistical filters (such as the Hodrick–Prescott filter) separate trend from cycle in output itself; they are simple but unreliable at the end of the sample, which is where policy needs them. Real-time output-gap estimates are revised substantially, and the sign of the gap has changed after revision in some episodes. Policy rules that use the gap inherit this uncertainty.

Why people disagree

Economists disagree about why productivity growth slowed in many advanced economies from the mid-2000s. One position holds that recent technologies, though visible, are less transformative for measured output than earlier waves such as electrification. A second holds that new technologies have spread slowly from leading firms to the rest, so the gains are concentrated. A third emphasises low investment and low demand after the 2008–09 recession. A fourth argues that part of the slowdown is mismeasurement of digital and free services; several studies conclude this explains only a small share. The positions are not mutually exclusive, and the relative weights remain contested.
