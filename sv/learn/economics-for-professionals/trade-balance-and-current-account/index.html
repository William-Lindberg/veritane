Trade Balance, Current Account and the Balance of Payments

The accounts that track a country's dealings with the rest of the world, and how to read a deficit or surplus without a value judgement.

Professional·6 min (Simple) · 11 min (Detailed)·Updated 6 November 2026

Read first: Trade, Tariffs and Globalisation, Exchange Rates

Key points

  • The trade balance is exports minus imports of goods, and of services when stated.
  • The current account adds income flows and transfers, including money that migrants send home.
  • By construction, a current-account deficit is matched by net inflows in the financial account: it is financed by the rest of the world.
  • A deficit or a surplus is not good or bad on its own; the questions are what finances it, what it pays for and how long it lasts.
  • Monthly trade data are volatile and revised; annual figures as a share of GDP are the comparable unit.

Simple: plain words. Detailed: exact figures, codes and methods.

In short: the balance of payments is a country’s account of every transaction with the rest of the world. A deficit in one part is, by the rules of the accounts, matched by a surplus in another.

Simple version

The trade balance

A country sells goods and services abroad (exports) and buys them from abroad (imports). The trade balanceTrade balanceExports minus imports of goods, and of services when stated, over a period.Read more about Trade balance is exports minus imports. If it is positive, there is a trade surplus; if negative, a trade deficit. News reports often mean goods only, so the label matters.

The current account

The current account is wider. It adds two more kinds of flow:

  • Income: wages earned abroad, interest and dividends received from foreign investments, minus the same paid out to foreigners.
  • Transfers: money sent with nothing bought in return, such as aid, and money that migrants send to their families at home (a remittanceRemittanceMoney sent by a migrant worker to family or others in another country.Read more about Remittance).

A three-line balance of payments

Example

A country (example, in ¤ billion) exports 500 and imports 550, so its trade balance is −50. It receives net income from abroad of +10 and net transfers of +15. Its current account is −50 + 10 + 15 = −25.

With GDP of 2,000, that is −1.25% of GDP. Because the accounts must balance, the country also recorded net financial inflows of about 25: foreigners bought more of its assets, or lent it more, than it bought or lent abroad.

The full balance of paymentsBalance of paymentsThe full account of a country's transactions with the rest of the world: trade, income, transfers and financial flows.Read more about Balance of payments has three main parts: the current account, a small capital account, and the financial account, which records purchases and sales of assets across borders.

The household analogy, and where it breaks

A household that spends more than it earns has to borrow or sell something. A country with a current-account deficit is similar: it is financed by money from abroad. The analogy breaks in three places:

  1. A country’s deficit is the total of millions of private decisions by firms and households, not one budget.
  2. The money can come as investment in factories or shares, not only as loans.
  3. Borrowing to build something that raises future income is different from borrowing to consume.

Not good or bad on its own

A surplus can reflect a highly competitive export sector, or low spending at home. A deficit can reflect an attractive place to invest, or spending that relies on short-term borrowing. Two examples with opposite signs (example): an economy with high household saving and large export industries may run a surplus for many years; a fast-growing economy that imports machinery to build new factories may run a deficit while foreign investors fund the expansion. The useful questions are what finances the balance, what it pays for and how long it can last.

Detailed version

Structure (BPM6)

The IMF’s Balance of Payments and International Investment Position Manual (sixth edition, BPM6) sets the international standard (Eurostat; ONS):

Account Main items
Current account Goods; services; primary income (compensation of employees, investment income); secondary income (transfers, including personal remittances)
Capital account Capital transfers; acquisition and disposal of non-produced, non-financial assets
Financial account Direct investment; portfolio investment; financial derivatives; other investment; reserve assets

The identity is:

current account + capital account − net financial account + net errors and omissions = 0

where a positive net financial account means net acquisition of foreign assets (net lending abroad). Errors and omissions absorb measurement gaps; persistently large values are a data-quality signal.

The saving–investment identity

From the national accounts, the current account equals national saving minus domestic investment:

CA = S − I

In the example, saving of 400 and investment of 425 (¤ billion) give CA = −25. The identity explains why trade policy alone has limited effect on the current account unless saving or investment changes, an argument common in the literature. It is an identity, not a theory of causation: it does not say whether saving or investment moved first.

Measurement issues

  • Services and digital trade are harder to measure than goods crossing a border; they rely on surveys and administrative data and are revised more.
  • Basis differences. Customs-basis trade figures (for example monthly goods data from the US Census Bureau) are adjusted to a balance-of-payments basis in the international accounts (BEA); the two differ in timing, coverage and valuation.
  • Global asymmetries. World exports and world imports should match but do not, so the sum of all countries’ current accounts is not zero in published data.
  • Volatility. Monthly trade balances move with shipment timing and commodity prices. Annual current-account balances as a share of GDP are the standard comparable unit, available for most countries through the World Bank (World Bank). Remittances are a large share of GDP in some economies (World Bank).

Prices and the exchange rate

Why people disagree

Economists disagree about persistent global imbalances. One view sees large surpluses as the result of high saving in some economies (sometimes called the “global saving glut” view), which lowered world interest rates and was absorbed by deficit countries. Another view emphasises policy choices in surplus countries, such as exchange-rate management or suppressed domestic demand, and argues these impose adjustment costs on others. A third strand focuses on gross capital flows and the financial system rather than net balances, arguing that net figures hide the exposures that matter. Each position draws on the same accounts; they differ on causation.
