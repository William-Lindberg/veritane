Leverage, Derivatives and Margin

Products that multiply gains and losses, and why regulators restrict them for retail users.

Intermediate·6 min (Simple) · 11 min (Detailed)·Updated 6 November 2026

Read first: How Stock Markets Work

Key points

  • Leverage means having exposure to more than the money you put in; losses can be larger than your stake.
  • Derivatives (options, futures, CFDs, swaps) take their value from something else and are used both to reduce risk and to bet on prices.
  • A margin call asks for more money when prices move against a position, and can force a sale at a low price.
  • European regulators reported that 74–89% of retail CFD accounts lose money, and restricted how these products are sold.
  • Complexity is a risk in itself: it is harder to know what you own and what it costs.

Simple: plain words. Detailed: exact figures, codes and methods.

In short: leverage lets a small amount of money control a large position, which multiplies gains and losses by the same factor.

Simple version

What leverage means

LeverageLeverageUsing borrowed money to increase exposure, which magnifies both gains and losses.Read more about Leverage means having exposure to more than the money you put in. A house bought with a large loan is leveraged: if its price changes by 10%, the owner’s share of the house changes by much more than 10%.

The same works in markets. Some products let ¤1,000 control a position worth ¤10,000. That is called 10 to 1 (10:1) leverage.

The 10x example, both ways

Example

You put in ¤1,000 and control ¤10,000 of shares at 10:1 leverage (example).

Price change Gain or loss on ¤10,000 Your ¤1,000 becomes Change on your money
+5% +¤500 ¤1,500 +50%
−5% −¤500 ¤500 −50%
−10% −¤1,000 ¤0 −100%
−15% −¤1,500 −¤500 (you owe) −150%

A 10% fall in the price wipes out the whole stake. A larger fall can leave a debt, unless the product limits losses to the money in the account.

Margin and the margin call

When you borrow to invest, the money you put up is called margin. The lender sets a minimum: your own share of the position must stay above a set level.

If prices fall, your share shrinks. The lender then makes a margin callMargin callA demand to add money when a leveraged position has lost value, or have it closed.Read more about Margin call: add money, or the position is sold. Margin calls happen after prices have fallen. Many people receive them at the same time, so forced selling can push prices lower still. The result can be a sale near the low point, with no chance to wait for a recovery.

What derivatives are

A derivativeDerivativeA contract whose value depends on something else, such as a share, an index, an interest rate or a commodity.Read more about Derivative is a contract whose value depends on something else: a share, an index, a currency, a commodity or an interest rate.

  • An optionOptionA contract giving the right, but not the obligation, to buy or sell something at a set price by a set date.Read more about Option gives the right, but not the duty, to buy or sell at a set price before a set date.
  • A future is an agreement to buy or sell at a set price on a set future date.
  • A contract for difference (CFD) pays the change in an asset’s price between opening and closing the contract, without owning the asset.
  • A swap exchanges one stream of payments for another, such as fixed for floating interest.

Businesses use derivatives for hedgingHedgingTaking a position that reduces the risk of another one, like insurance for an investment.Read more about Hedging: an airline can fix its fuel price for next year, or an exporter can fix an exchange rate. Others use them to bet on prices. The same contract can do either job.

What the warnings on trading apps mean

In many countries, providers must show a warning such as “X% of retail investor accounts lose money when trading CFDs with this provider”. The figure is that provider’s own data. European regulators found that across countries 74–89% of retail CFD accounts lost money (ESMA, 2018).

Varies by country

Rules for retail leveraged products differ. In the European Union, ESMA set leverage limits for retail CFDs, a rule to close positions when margin falls to 50% of the requirement, and protection against losing more than the account balance (ESMA). The UK made similar rules permanent (FCA). Australia’s securities regulator has used product intervention orders for CFDs (ASIC, REP 724) and binary options (ASIC, REP 736). In some countries retail CFDs are not offered at all.

Detailed version

Leverage arithmetic

With leverage L (position ÷ own funds), the return on own funds is approximately:

r_equity ≈ L × r_asset − (L − 1) × r_borrow − costs

A loss of 1 / L on the asset removes all equity. At 30:1 that is a 3.33% move; at 2:1 it is 50%.

Margin call threshold (worked example). ¤5,000 own money plus a ¤5,000 loan buys ¤10,000 of shares. The maintenance requirement is 30% equity. Equity = V − 5,000, so the call comes when (V − 5,000) / V = 0.30, giving V = ¤7,142.86: a fall of 28.57%.

Options basics

A call option’s payoff at expiry is max(S − K, 0); a put’s is max(K − S, 0), where S is the underlying price and K the strike. The buyer pays a premium and can lose at most that premium; the seller (writer) receives it and, for an uncovered call, faces losses with no fixed limit. Option prices rise with time to expiry and with expected volatility. US investor education from FINRA describes these structures and the risk involved in writing options (FINRA).

Futures and funding

Futures are standardised, exchange-traded and cleared through a central counterparty. Positions are marked to market daily: gains and losses are settled in cash every day through variation margin, on top of an initial margin deposit (CFTC Glossary). Daily settlement limits the build-up of unpaid losses but creates funding needs at short notice.

CFD structure

A CFD is a bilateral contract with the provider, not exchange-traded. The provider often acts as counterparty, so the client’s loss can be the provider’s gain unless the provider hedges. Costs include the spread, overnight financing on the leveraged amount and, for some, commission. ESMA’s 2018 measures for retail clients set initial margin limits from 30:1 for major currency pairs down to 2:1 for crypto assets, a 50% margin close-out rule per account, negative balance protection and a standard risk warning (ESMA, 2018).

Hedging and speculation

A hedge offsets an existing exposure: the combined position moves less than either part. Speculation creates exposure. Hedgers need counterparties willing to take the other side, and speculators supply part of that. A hedge also gives up gains: a producer that sells its output forward at a fixed price does not benefit if the market price rises.

Counterparty risk

Exchange-traded derivatives are guaranteed by a clearing house funded by members’ margin and default funds. Over-the-counter contracts depend on the other party paying; collateral agreements and, for many standard swaps, mandatory central clearing reduce that exposure.

Common misconceptions

  • “Stop-loss orders cap my loss.” A stop becomes a market order; in a price gap it fills below the trigger.
  • “Hedging is free.” It costs premiums, spreads or forgone gains.
  • “The 74–89% figure means those traders were unlucky.” It is an aggregate outcome across accounts, consistent with costs and leverage applied to short-term price changes.
