How to Read Company Accounts

The three main statements in an annual report, the figures on a company page, and what a few common ratios measure.

Intermediate·7 min (Simple) · 12 min (Detailed)·Updated 7 November 2026

Read first: How Stock Markets Work, Percentages, Averages and Charts, Step by Step

Key points

  • An annual report has three main statements: the income statement (a year of income and costs), the balance sheet (what is owned and owed on one day) and the cash flow statement (cash in and out during the year).
  • Profit and cash are different things: a company can report a profit and still have less cash at the end of the year, and the other way round.
  • A fiscal year is the company's own 12-month accounting year; it does not have to match the calendar year.
  • Later reports can restate earlier years. The latest filed figure replaces the first one, and both stay on file.
  • A ratio describes a relationship between two figures. Its level differs a lot between industries, so it is a starting point for questions rather than a verdict.

Simple: plain words. Detailed: exact figures, codes and methods.

In short: company accounts answer three questions. How much did the company earn and spend in the year? What did it own and owe at the end of the year? Where did its cash come from and go? Each question has its own statement.

Simple version

Where the figures come from

Companies whose shares trade on a stock exchange publish an annual report every year. In the United States it is filed with the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) as a form called the 10-K. Companies based abroad file a 20-F or a 40-F. The figures in it are checked by an outside auditor before they are published.

The company pages on Veritane take their figures straight from these filings and link every number to the filing it comes from.

The income statement: one year of income and costs

The income statementIncome statementThe financial statement showing revenue, costs and profit over a period; also called the profit and loss account.Read more about Income statement starts with revenue: the money from selling goods and services in the year. Costs are then taken away step by step:

  1. Revenue minus the costs of running the business gives operating income (profit from the main business).
  2. Operating income minus interest and tax gives net income: the profit that belongs to the shareholders. A negative figure is a net loss.

Example

Example Co sells goods worth ¤500 million in a year (example). Making the goods, paying staff and running offices cost ¤440 million, so operating income is ¤60 million. After ¤10 million of interest and ¤12 million of tax, net income is ¤38 million.

The balance sheet: one day’s snapshot

The balance sheetBalance sheetA snapshot of what is owned and what is owed at one point in time.Read more about Balance sheet lists what the company owns (its assets) and what it owes (its liabilities) on the last day of the year. The difference is shareholders’ equity: the part that belongs to the owners.

Assets = liabilities + equity. The two sides balance by definition, which is where the name comes from.

Items due within a year, or that turn into cash within a year, are called current: current assets (cash, money customers owe, stock waiting to be sold) and current liabilities (bills and short-term debts). Long-term debt is borrowing that is due after more than a year.

The cash flow statement: cash in and out

Profit is not the same as cash. A sale made on credit counts as revenue straight away, but the cash arrives later. A new factory is paid for at once, but its cost is spread over many years in the income statement.

The cash flow statementCash-flow statementThe financial statement that shows cash received and paid in a period, split into operating, investing and financing activities.Read more about Cash-flow statement shows the actual cash:

  • Cash from operations: cash that came in from running the business.
  • Capital expenditure (capex): cash spent on buildings, machines and other long-lived assets.
  • Cash from operations minus capex is often called free cash flowFree cash flowCash from operations minus capital spending: the cash a business generates after maintaining and expanding its assets.Read more about Free cash flow.

Fiscal years

A fiscal year is the company’s own accounting year. Many companies use the calendar year. Others end their year in March, June or September, and some end it on a set weekday, so a year can have 52 or 53 weeks. Retailers often end their year in late January and name it after the calendar year in which most of it fell. So “fiscal 2024” for one company and “fiscal 2024” for another can cover different months.

Restatements

A company sometimes changes figures for an earlier year in a later report. This is a restatement. It can correct a mistake, follow a new accounting rule or show a part of the business that was sold separately. The company pages show the latest filed figure and mark it when it differs from the first one reported.

Four common ratios

A ratio divides one figure by another, so companies of different sizes can be put side by side.

Ratio Formula What it describes
Profit margin Net income ÷ revenue How much of each unit of revenue was left as profit
Return on equity (ROE) Net income ÷ shareholders’ equity Profit compared with the owners’ part of the company
Debt-to-equity Long-term debt ÷ shareholders’ equity Borrowing compared with the owners’ part
Current ratio Current assets ÷ current liabilities What turns into cash within a year, compared with what is due within a year

Example

For Example Co: net income ¤38 million, revenue ¤500 million, equity ¤200 million, long-term debt ¤120 million (example). Profit margin = 38 ÷ 500 = 7.6%. ROE = 38 ÷ 200 = 19%. Debt-to-equity = 120 ÷ 200 = 0.6.

Typical ratios differ a lot between industries. A supermarket works with thin margins and sells a lot; a software company has high margins and few physical assets; a bank’s balance sheet is mostly borrowed money by design. A ratio is a question to look into, not a grade.

What the accounts do not tell you

  • The share price. Accounts describe the business, not what investors pay for it. Share prices come from the stock exchange.
  • The future. Accounts describe the past year.
  • Everything about the business. The notes and the text of the report explain the figures; the figures alone leave a lot out.

Detailed version

Accounting frameworks and tagging

U.S. companies report under US GAAP; most other listed companies report under IFRS. Since 2009–2011 the SEC has required filers to tag the figures in their reports with XBRL, a machine-readable label for each number. Every tag names a concept from a taxonomy (for example us-gaap:Revenues or ifrs-full:Revenue), a period (a start and end date for flows, one date for balances), a unit (a currency, shares) and the filing it appears in. The SEC publishes all tagged figures for each company as “company facts”.

Companies choose concepts themselves. The same idea can carry different tags: after the 2018 revenue standard (ASC 606) many U.S. filers moved from Revenues or SalesRevenueNet to RevenueFromContractWithCustomerExcludingAssessedTax. A consistent series therefore needs a list of concepts in order of preference, and the concept used for each year is part of the receipt.

Periods, comparatives and restatements

Each annual report contains the current year and comparative columns for earlier years (usually two years of income and cash flow, one earlier balance sheet). So one period appears in several filings. Using the latest filed value gives the figure on the company’s current basis, including:

  • Error corrections (a restatement in the strict sense, IAS 8 / ASC 250),
  • Retrospective changes in accounting policy,
  • Discontinued operations, where a business that was sold is taken out of earlier years’ continuing figures,
  • Reclassifications between lines.

The first reported value stays relevant: it is what was known at the time. Keeping both, with the filing dates, makes the change visible.

Flows, balances and fiscal calendars

Income statement and cash flow figures are flows over a period; balance sheet figures are stocks at a date. A ratio that divides a flow by a stock (ROE, return on assets) depends on which balance is used. Veritane uses the year-end balance, as the DuPont tool does; analysts often use the average of the opening and closing balance, which smooths the effect of large changes during the year.

Annual flows in XBRL are identified by duration: a 52/53-week year runs 364 or 371 days, so a window of about 350–380 days separates the year from quarters and transition periods. A fiscal year label follows the company’s convention (fy in the filing), which matters for January year-ends.

Reading the ratios with care

  • Profit margin varies with the business model and with one-off items (asset sales, write-downs, tax changes). Operating margin (operating income ÷ revenue) strips out interest and tax.
  • ROE rises with leverage: the DuPont identityReturn on equity (ROE)Profit as a share of shareholders' equity, which rises with leverage as well as with a more profitable business.Read more about Return on equity (ROE) splits it into margin × asset turnover × equity multiplier, which shows whether a change came from profitability, efficiency or borrowing. With negative equity (after large buybacks or losses) ROE is not meaningful.
  • Debt-to-equity has several definitions: long-term debt only, total debt including the current portion and leases, or net debt (debt minus cash). The formula has to be stated next to the number. Book equity can differ a lot from market value, which is why the WACC tool weights by market values.
  • Current ratio reflects the operating cycle: a business paid in cash by customers and on credit by suppliers can run below 1 for years.
  • Banks and insurers have balance sheets of a different kind. Revenue, current assets and debt-to-equity are not defined the same way, and regulators use capital ratios instead.

Currencies and comparability

Foreign private issuers file in their reporting currency (euro, yen and others). Ranking or adding figures across companies needs a currency conversion and a choice of rate (year-end or average). Veritane keeps each company’s own currency on its page and converts at the ECB reference rate for one purpose: choosing which companies get a page.
