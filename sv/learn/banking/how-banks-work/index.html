How Banks Work

What a bank does with your deposit, and why that shapes the fees and interest rates it offers.

Everyday·5 min (Simple) · 10 min (Detailed)·Updated 6 November 2026

Read first: What Money Is (and Isn't)

Key points

  • A deposit is a loan from you to the bank: the bank owes you the money and has to repay it when you ask.
  • Banks earn most of their income from the spread: the gap between the interest they charge on loans and the interest they pay on deposits.
  • Credit unions and cooperative banks are owned by their members; the names and rules for them vary by country.
  • Banks are regulated, and in most countries they have to hold minimum amounts of capital and easy-to-sell assets.
  • Banks are businesses: fees and rates reflect their costs and profit targets, and competition between banks is one of the things that keeps prices in check.

Simple: plain words. Detailed: exact figures, codes and methods.

In short: a bank borrows from savers, lends to borrowers and earns the difference. Your deposit is not kept in a box with your name on it; it is a promise by the bank to pay you back.

Simple version

The “vault” picture is not quite right

Many people picture a bank as a big safe. You hand over ¤500 (example), and the bank keeps those notes until you come back. That is not how it works.

When you pay money into an account, the bank owes you that amount. In law, your deposit is a loan from you to the bank. The bank can use the money, and it promises to pay it back when you ask. The number on your screen is that promise.

How a bank earns money: the spread

A bank pays you interest on some accounts. It also lends money to people and businesses, and charges them interest. The gap between the two rates is called the spread.

Example

A small bank (example) holds ¤10 million of deposits and pays 1% a year on them. That costs it ¤100,000 a year. It lends ¤8 million at 6% a year, which brings in ¤480,000. The gap is ¤380,000. Out of that, the bank pays staff, rent, technology and the cost of loans that are not repaid. What is left is profit.

Banks also earn money from fees, for example for overdrafts, card payments or sending money abroad. The lesson on fees looks at these in detail.

A bank does not keep all the money

A bank keeps some money ready for people who want to withdraw cash or make payments. It lends or invests the rest. This works because, on a normal day, only a small share of customers take their money out at once.

If many customers ask for their money at the same moment, the bank can run short of ready cash. This is called a bank runBank runMany depositors withdrawing their money at once because they worry the bank will fail, which can itself cause the failure.Read more about Bank run. Rules, deposit protection and central banks exist partly to stop that from happening. A later lesson explains deposit protection.

Who watches the banks

Banks are among the most closely watched businesses in any economy. A government agency, often linked to the central bankCentral bankThe institution that issues a country's or region's currency, sets the policy interest rate and often supervises banks.Read more about Central bank, gives banks a licence and checks that they follow the rules. Two of the main rules are:

  • Capital: the bank’s owners have to put in enough of their own money to absorb losses before depositors lose anything.
  • Liquidity: the bank has to hold enough cash and easy-to-sell assets to pay customers who withdraw money.

Why fees and rates differ between banks

Banks are businesses. Their fees and interest rates depend on their costs, their profit targets and what other banks offer. Where switching to another bank is easy, banks compete harder on price. That is why the same service can cost different amounts at different banks.

Detailed version

The balance sheet view

A bank’s customer deposits are liabilities (it owes them); its loans and securities are assets. The gap between assets and liabilities is equity, the owners’ stake. This is the accounting form of the key point that a deposit is a loan to the bank (Bank of England).

Money creation: two descriptions

The textbook fractional-reserve model says banks take in deposits, keep a fraction as reserves, and lend the rest, so that central bank money is “multiplied up”. The Bank of England describes the modern process differently: when a bank makes a loan, it credits the borrower’s account with a new deposit, creating money at that moment; lending is limited by profitability, borrower demand, regulation and the central bank’s interest rate, not by a fixed reserve ratio (Bank of England, 2014). Both descriptions agree that a bank holds only a fraction of its deposits as cash or reserves.

Net interest margin

The standard measure of the spread is the net interest margin (NIM):

NIM = (interest income − interest expense) ÷ average interest-earning assets

Worked example (illustrative): loans of ¤8 million at 6% earn ¤480,000; ¤2 million of reserves and government bonds at 3% earn ¤60,000; ¤9 million of deposits at 1% cost ¤90,000. NIM = (540,000 − 90,000) ÷ 10,000,000 = 4.5%. Analysts read NIM together with fee income, operating costs and loan losses, because a high margin can reflect high-risk lending.

Maturity transformation and runs

Banks fund long-term loans (a 25-year mortgage) with short-term liabilities (deposits withdrawable today). This maturity transformation is useful to the economy but leaves banks exposed to sudden withdrawals. The classic model by Diamond and Dybvig shows that a run can happen even at a solvent bank if each depositor expects others to withdraw first, and that deposit insurance can remove the reason to run (Diamond and Dybvig, 1983).

Capital and liquidity rules

International minimum standards are set by the Basel Committee on Banking Supervision and applied through national law (BIS, Basel Framework). In one paragraph: banks hold capital in proportion to risk-weighted assets (riskier assets need more capital), plus buffers that can be used in bad times; a leverage ratio sets a floor that ignores risk weights; the liquidity coverage ratio requires enough high-quality liquid assets to cover 30 days of stressed outflows; and the net stable funding ratio limits reliance on short-term funding. Supervisors add their own requirements on top (Bank of England).

Shadow banking

Some bank-like activity happens outside banks: money market funds, finance companies, securities lending and other forms of credit provided by non-banks. The Financial Stability Board calls this non-bank financial intermediation and monitors it because it can involve maturity transformation and leverage without the bank safety net (FSB).

Common misconceptions

  • “My money is kept separately for me.” A deposit is a claim on the bank, not property held in your name.
  • “Banks lend out savers’ money one-for-one.” Lending creates deposits; the constraints are capital, liquidity, demand and price.
  • “A profitable bank cannot fail.” A bank can be profitable and still run out of liquid funds.

Varies by country

Who licenses and supervises banks differs. In the euro area, the European Central Bank directly supervises the largest banks through the Single Supervisory Mechanism, with national authorities supervising smaller ones (ECB). In the United Kingdom, the Prudential Regulation Authority, part of the Bank of England, sets prudential rules (Bank of England). In the United States, several federal and state agencies share the job, depending on how a bank is chartered. The names for member-owned institutions also vary: credit unions, cooperative banks, building societies and savings banks are common terms.

Why people disagree

How much freedom should banks have to create money through lending? The mainstream view, reflected in the Basel standards, is that fractional banking is useful because it turns short-term savings into long-term credit, and that its dangers can be managed with capital rules, liquidity rules and deposit insurance (BIS). Supporters of “full-reserve” or “narrow” banking argue that deposits used for payments should be backed one-for-one by central bank money, so that bank runs could not happen; critics reply that this would reduce lending or push it into less regulated non-banks (FSB). The debate continues, including in work on digital central bank money.
