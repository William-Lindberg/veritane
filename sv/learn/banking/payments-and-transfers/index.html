Payments and Transfers

Cards, bank transfers, direct debits, instant payments and cash: how each one moves money and what can go wrong.

Everyday·6 min (Simple) · 11 min (Detailed)·Updated 6 November 2026

Read first: Types of Accounts

Key points

  • A debit card spends money you already have; a credit card borrows the card issuer's money until you repay it.
  • Some transfers arrive in seconds and others take days, depending on the payment system behind them.
  • Direct debits and standing orders are permissions, and the account holder can cancel them.
  • Payments in another currency usually add an exchange-rate margin and fees on top of the amount sent.
  • Rights to get money back, such as chargebacks and refunds, depend on how you paid.

Simple: plain words. Detailed: exact figures, codes and methods.

In short: every payment is a message that tells banks to move a balance from one account to another. The method you choose decides how fast it moves, what it costs and how easy it is to reverse.

Push and pull

A push payment is one you send: a transfer or a standing order. A pull payment is one a company collects from you with your permission: a direct debit or a card payment. With a pull payment, the other side starts the payment, so the permission matters. You can cancel a direct debit or a recurring card payment with your bank or the company; the rules on notice vary.

What “pending” means

When you pay by card, the shop’s bank asks your bank to approve the payment. Your bank puts a hold on that amount. The payment shows as “pending”. A day or a few days later, the payment is completed (settled), and the hold turns into a final payment. Hotels and fuel stations sometimes place a larger hold first and then settle the real amount. That is why your available balance can be lower than you expect for a few days.

Instant payments

Many countries now have systems that move money between banks in seconds, at any time of day. Examples include the euro area’s instant transfers, Brazil’s Pix and the United States’ FedNow service. Speed is convenient, but it also means that money sent to the wrong person, or to a fraudster, is gone in seconds. The lesson on digital banking looks at this.

Sending money abroad

A payment in another currency usually has two costs:

  • A fee, shown up front.
  • An exchange-rate margin: the provider uses a rate that is less favourable than the mid-market rate (the midpoint between buying and selling prices), and keeps the difference.

Example

You send ¤500 (example) abroad. The provider charges a ¤5 fee and converts the remaining ¤495 at 1.45 units of the other currency per ¤. The mid-market rate is 1.50. The recipient gets 495 × 1.45 = 717.75. At the mid-market rate with no fee, ¤500 would have been 750. The total cost is 32.25 in the recipient’s currency, or 4.3% of the amount.

Seeing both the fee and the rate together shows the full price of a transfer. The currency converter shows how a margin changes what arrives.

Detailed version

Rails: the systems underneath

  • Card networks: four-party schemes link cardholder, issuer, acquirer and merchant. Authorisation (the issuer approves and holds funds) is separate from clearing and settlement (the final exchange of funds between banks), which often happens one or more days later (Bank of England).
  • Batch credit transfers and direct debits: payments are collected and settled in cycles. In the euro area, SEPA schemes standardise credit transfers and direct debits across participating countries (ECB, SEPA).
  • Real-time (instant) systems: settle individual payments within seconds, around the clock. Examples: the Eurosystem’s TIPS (ECB), the Federal Reserve’s FedNow (Federal Reserve) and Pix, operated by Brazil’s central bank (Banco Central do Brasil). EU rules now require payment providers that offer euro credit transfers to offer instant ones, with a check that the payee’s name matches the account (Regulation (EU) 2024/886).
  • Correspondent banking: cross-border payments pass through chains of banks holding accounts with each other. Each link can add time, cost and opacity, which is why the G20 adopted targets to make cross-border payments cheaper, faster and more transparent (FSB).

Behind all of them, the final transfer between banks is settled in central bank money or through a settlement agent (Bank of England).

Pricing a cross-border payment

total cost = fee + amount converted × (mid rate − applied rate) ÷ mid rate   (in source currency)
cost %     = 1 − (amount received ÷ (amount sent × mid rate))

Using the Simple example: total cost = 5 + 495 × 0.05 ÷ 1.50 = ¤21.50, and cost % = 1 − 717.75 ÷ 750 = 4.3%.

Open banking

Open banking lets a customer authorise a licensed third party to read account data or start a payment directly from the account, through secure interfaces (APIs). In the EU this is set out in PSD2 (Directive (EU) 2015/2366); other jurisdictions use regulation, industry standards or market-led models (BIS). Account-to-account payments started this way avoid card interchange but do not carry card-scheme chargeback rights.

Common misconceptions

  • “Pending means the money has left.” It is held, not settled; the final amount can differ.
  • “Chargebacks are a legal right everywhere.” They are mostly scheme rules; legal rights vary.
  • “Zero fee means no cost.” The exchange-rate margin is a cost even when the fee is zero.

Varies by country

Payment systems and rights differ. In the euro area, SEPA standardises transfers and direct debits, and instant euro transfers are widely required (ECB). In Brazil, the central bank runs Pix for instant payments (Banco Central do Brasil). In the United Kingdom, credit card holders have an extra legal claim against the card provider for many purchases (CCA 1974 s75). In the United States, billing-error rules apply to credit cards (Regulation Z).

Why people disagree

How far should payments move away from cash? One view holds that digital and instant payments are cheaper, faster and easier to trace, and that falling cash use reflects what customers choose. Another view holds that cash protects privacy, works during power or network failures, and matters for people without bank accounts or digital skills, so access to it is a public concern. Central banks and governments are weighing these points in different ways, including through work on digital central bank money and rules on cash access (Bank of England).
