Paying Down Debt

The two main ways of ordering repayments across several debts, and how to compare them with your own numbers.

Everyday·6 min (Simple) · 10 min (Detailed)·Updated 6 November 2026

Read first: The Real Cost of Borrowing

Key points

  • The avalanche method (highest interest rate first) gives the lowest total interest; the snowball method (smallest balance first) clears whole debts sooner.
  • Both methods reach zero; the difference in cost is often smaller than the difference made by paying more each month, and the method a person keeps to is the one that works for them.
  • Consolidation can lower the interest rate, or it can simply stretch the debt over a longer time.
  • Both methods start the same way: the minimum on every debt, then any extra on one debt at a time.
  • Fees for moving or refinancing a debt are part of the comparison.

Simple: plain words. Detailed: exact figures, codes and methods.

In short: with several debts, the order of repayment changes how much interest you pay and how soon each debt disappears. The two best-known orders are the avalanche and the snowball.

Simple version

Three steps

Both methods follow the same three steps:

  1. List every debt: what is owed, the interest rate and the minimum payment.
  2. Pay the minimum on all of them, so none falls behind.
  3. Put any money left over on one debt until it is gone, then move that whole payment to the next one.

The only difference is the order in step 3.

  • AvalancheAvalanche methodRepaying debts by putting any extra money towards the debt with the highest interest rate first, which keeps total interest as low as possible.Read more about Avalanche method: the debt with the highest interest rate comes first.
  • SnowballSnowball methodRepaying debts smallest balance first to build momentum.Read more about Snowball method: the debt with the smallest balance comes first.

A worked example

Example

Three debts (example) and ¤400 a month to spend on them:

Debt Balance Rate Minimum
Store card ¤600 12% ¤25
Credit card ¤3,000 24% ¤90
Personal loan ¤5,000 8% ¤150

The minimums add up to ¤265, so ¤135 a month is extra.

Snowball Avalanche
First debt cleared Store card, month 4 Credit card, month 16
Second debt cleared Credit card, month 18 Store card, month 17
All clear month 25 month 25
Total interest ¤1,209.62 ¤1,136.11

The avalanche costs ¤73.51 less in interest. The snowball clears its first debt a whole year earlier.

Now compare paying only the minimums (¤265 a month). It takes about 42–43 months and costs about ¤2,520–¤2,540 in interest, whichever order is used. In this example, the extra ¤135 a month saves far more than the choice of method.

Why both methods have supporters

The avalanche is the cheaper choice by arithmetic: the highest rate is cut first, so less interest builds up. The snowball gives a quicker sense of progress, because a whole debt disappears early. Some people find that this makes them more likely to keep going. Both reach zero, and the debt payoff tool shows both orders for your own numbers.

Consolidation: one loan instead of many

Debt consolidationDebt consolidationCombining several debts into one loan, which can lower the rate, lengthen the term, or both.Read more about Debt consolidation means taking one new loan to pay off several old ones. It can help in two ways: one payment is easier to manage, and the new rate may be lower.

It can also cost more. If the new loan runs for longer, the monthly payment falls but the total interest can rise. Fees for the new loan, and charges for closing the old ones early, add to the cost. And if the old cards stay open and are used again, the debt can grow back.

Balance transfers

A balance transfer moves a card debt to a new card, often at 0% for a set time, for a fee.

Example

¤5,000 (example) on a card at 20% a year, repaid over 24 months, costs about ¤1,108 in interest. Moving it to a 0% card for 24 months with a 3% fee costs ¤150. If the whole amount is repaid within the 0% period, that is about ¤958 less. Whatever is still owed when the offer ends is charged at the card’s standard rate.

Detailed version

Why the avalanche minimises interest

Each month the borrower allocates a fixed surplus S above the minimums. Interest accrues at rate rᵢ on balance Bᵢ. Directing S to the debt with the highest rᵢ reduces total interest by the most per unit of currency in every period, so, for a fixed budget, no other ordering produces lower total interest (ties aside). The snowball’s extra cost is roughly the rate gap times the balance that remains on the higher-rate debt for longer. In the Simple example, the avalanche clears the 24% credit card in month 16 instead of month 18, which saves ¤73.51.

Behavioural evidence

Research on debt repayment suggests that the ordering people choose is not only about cost. Amar and co-authors found, in experiments, that people tend to pay off small debts first even when this raises the total interest paid, a pattern they call “debt account aversion” (Amar et al., 2011). Gal and McShane, studying data from a debt-settlement programme, found that closing individual accounts was associated with a higher chance of eliminating total debt (Gal and McShane, 2012). Together these results are often cited both for and against the snowball: it can raise persistence, and it can also raise cost.

Refinancing break-even

saving = interest on current path − (interest on new path + new fees + early-repayment charges)
break-even fee = interest on current path − interest on new path

In the balance-transfer example: 1,107.50 − (0 + 150) = ¤957.50. The comparison holds only if the balance is repaid on the assumed schedule; any remainder after a promotional period is priced at the standard rate. EU consumer credit law gives a right to repay early, with compensation to the lender capped (Directive 2008/48/EC).

Debt-to-income ratios

Debt-to-incomeDebt-to-income ratioMonthly debt payments divided by monthly income, used by lenders to judge affordability.Read more about Debt-to-income ratio (DTI) metrics express debt burdens relative to income:

payment DTI = monthly debt payments ÷ gross monthly income
stock DTI   = total debt outstanding ÷ gross annual income

Example (illustrative): payments of ¤900 on monthly income of ¤2,500 give a payment DTI of 36%. Lenders use DTI in affordability checks, and some macroprudential frameworks limit lending above set ratios. Definitions (gross or net income, which debts count) vary by lender and country.

Secured versus unsecured priority

Debts differ in consequences, not just rates. Missing payments on secured debt (such as a mortgage or car loan) can lead to losing the asset; arrears on rent, energy or tax can lead to eviction, disconnection or enforcement. Debt advisers therefore distinguish priority from non-priority debts, which can justify paying a lower-rate debt before a higher-rate one (Bank of England). The next lesson covers this.

Common misconceptions

  • “The snowball is irrational.” It costs more interest; whether it is worth that depends on its effect on persistence.
  • “Consolidation reduces debt.” It replaces debt; total cost depends on rate, term and fees.
  • “A 0% offer has no cost.” Transfer fees and the post-offer rate apply.

Why people disagree

Which ordering is preferable? Economists who focus on cost point out that, for a fixed budget, the avalanche gives the lowest total interest, so the snowball’s extra cost is a price paid for motivation. Behavioural researchers note that people tend to favour clearing small balances (Amar et al., 2011) and that closing accounts is associated with finishing repayment (Gal and McShane, 2012), so a method that someone keeps to can matter more than one that is cheaper on paper. Debt advisers often add a third consideration: priority debts first, whatever their rate.
