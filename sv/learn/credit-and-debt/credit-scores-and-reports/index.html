Credit Scores and Reports

What is on a credit report, how a score is built from it, and why both vary by country.

Everyday·6 min (Simple) · 11 min (Detailed)·Updated 6 November 2026

Read first: What Credit Is

Key points

  • A credit report is the data; a credit score is a number that summarises it.
  • Common factors in scores are payment history, how much is owed, how long credit has been held, recent applications and the mix of credit types.
  • In many countries you can see your own report and ask for errors to be corrected.
  • Scoring models and credit bureaus vary by country, and some countries have no consumer credit score at all.
  • Checking your own report does not lower your score.

Simple version

Report and score: two different things

A credit report is a file of facts. It is kept by a credit bureauCredit bureauA company or public registry that collects information about how people repay their debts.Read more about Credit bureau (a company that collects credit information) or, in some countries, by the central bank. A report typically shows:

  • your name, date of birth and addresses;
  • the loans, cards and some other accounts you hold, with their limits and balances;
  • whether payments were made on time;
  • serious events such as defaults or court judgements;
  • which lenders have looked at your file recently.

A credit score is a number calculated from the report. Different bureaus and lenders use different scoring systems, so the same person can have several different scores.

What tends to raise or lower a score

Scoring systems differ, but most look at similar things:

Factor What helps the score What lowers it
Payment history Paying on time, every month Late or missed payments, defaults
Amounts owed Using a small share of available limits Balances close to the limit
Length of history Accounts held for years Only very new accounts
New applications Few applications at a time Many applications in a short period
Credit mix Different types handled well (A small factor in most systems)

Example

Someone has two cards with limits of ¤2,000 and ¤3,000 (example), so ¤5,000 in total. They owe ¤1,500. Their utilisation (the share of the limit used) is 1,500 ÷ 5,000 = 30%. If they repay ¤1,000, utilisation falls to 500 ÷ 5,000 = 10%. Many scoring systems treat lower utilisation as a sign of lower risk.

Seeing your own report

In many countries the law gives people a right to see the information held about them, often for free. You can ask the bureau or registry for a copy. Looking at your own report is a “soft” check: it does not affect your score.

Fixing an error

Reports can contain mistakes: a payment marked late that was on time, an account that is not yours, or an old address. Where a right to correction exists, the usual route is:

  1. Contacting the bureau (and often the lender that supplied the data) in writing.
  2. Explaining what is wrong, with evidence such as a bank statement.
  3. The bureau investigating within a set time and correcting or confirming the entry.

An account you do not recognise can also be a sign of identity fraud.

No score at all

In some countries, there is no consumer score. Lenders look at a central-bank register of loans and defaults, or ask for payslips and bank statements instead. And people who are new to credit, or have just moved country, may have little or no file. That is a “thin file”, not a bad one.

Detailed version

How a scorecard is built

Most consumer scores are statistical models fitted to past borrowers: the outcome is whether an account went seriously delinquent (for example, 90 days past due) within a set window, typically 12–24 months. A common form is a logistic regression with grouped (“binned”) variables, scaled to points:

ln(odds of good) = a + Σ bᵢ·xᵢ
score = offset + factor × ln(odds)
factor = PDO ÷ ln 2         (PDO = points to double the odds)

Worked example (illustrative): with PDO = 20 and a score of 600 set at odds of 50 good to 1 bad, factor = 28.85 and offset = 600 − 28.85 × ln 50 = 487.12. A score of 620 then means odds of 100:1 (a bad rate of about 1.0%), and 580 means 25:1. Each band of 20 points doubles or halves the odds. The scale is a design choice, which is why scores from different systems are not comparable.

Hard and soft enquiries

A hard enquiry is recorded when a lender checks a file to decide on an application; it is visible to other lenders and can lower some scores for a period. A soft enquiry (checking your own file, quotation searches, existing-lender reviews) is visible only to the consumer and does not affect scores.

Negative-only versus full-file systems

  • Negative-only: the file holds defaults and arrears only. Lenders learn who has failed to pay, not who has paid well.
  • Full-file (positive and negative): the file also holds on-time payments, balances and limits. Australia moved towards comprehensive credit reporting, in which repayment history is shared (OAIC).

Full-file systems give lenders more information about people with limited histories; they also hold more personal data. The World Bank’s work on credit reporting discusses this balance (World Bank).

Data-retention periods

Retention is set by law and varies. In the United States, most adverse information may be reported for seven years and bankruptcies for ten (15 U.S.C. 1681c). Other countries set their own periods, often shorter for repayment history than for defaults.

Access and correction rights

US consumers are entitled to free reports from the nationwide bureaus through a single authorised site (AnnualCreditReport.com). In the EU, the GDPR gives rights of access and rectification, and rights concerning decisions based solely on automated processing (GDPR). The revised EU consumer credit directive adds rules on creditworthiness assessment, including limits on the data that can be used and a right to a human review of automated decisions (Directive (EU) 2023/2225).

Alternative data and explainability

Models that use bank transaction data, rent and utility payments, or machine-learning methods can score thin-file applicants. They raise questions of consent, accuracy, indirect discrimination and explainability: a declined applicant may be entitled to the main reasons for the decision. A later lesson on automated decisions covers this in more detail.

Common misconceptions

  • “There is one credit score.” There are many models and scales.
  • “Income is part of the score.” Usually not; income enters the lender’s separate affordability assessment.
  • “Checking my own report lowers my score.” Self-checks are soft enquiries.

Varies by country

Credit reporting differs widely. In the United States, several private bureaus hold full files and consumers can get free reports through one authorised site (AnnualCreditReport.com). In Australia, comprehensive credit reporting adds repayment history to the file, with rights to access and correction (OAIC). In the European Union, data-protection law gives rights to see and correct personal data (GDPR), while the type of system (private bureau, public registry or both) differs by member state. Some countries have mainly negative-only files or no consumer score.

Why people disagree

How much data should credit scores use? Supporters of wider data, such as rent, utility and bank-account information, argue that it lets lenders assess people with thin files, who are otherwise refused or charged more (World Bank). Critics argue that more data means more intrusion into private life, more chance of errors, and models that can be hard to explain or can disadvantage groups indirectly. EU law answers part of this by restricting some data and giving a right to human review (Directive (EU) 2023/2225); other countries strike the balance differently.
