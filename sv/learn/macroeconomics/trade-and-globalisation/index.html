Trade, Tariffs and Globalisation

Why countries trade, who gains and who loses, and what tariffs do to prices.

Intermediate·6 min (Simple) · 11 min (Detailed)·Updated 6 November 2026

Read first: BNP: vad det mäter och vad det missar

Key points

  • Comparative advantage: trade can raise total output even when one country is more productive at everything.
  • The gains from trade are spread widely; the losses are concentrated in particular industries and places, which shapes the politics of trade.
  • A tariff is a tax on imports collected at the border; much of it is usually passed on to buyers in the importing country.
  • A trade deficit or surplus reflects a country's saving and investment, not only how competitive it is.
  • Supply chains link prices across borders, so events far away can change prices in local shops.

In short: countries trade because each can specialise in what it gives up least to produce. Trade raises total output, but the gains and losses are not shared equally, and tariffs change who pays.

Simple version

Why trade at all?

A country could make everything it needs itself. But making everything means doing many things it is not especially good at. Trade lets each country focus on what it does with the smallest sacrifice, and buy the rest.

The key idea is comparative advantageComparative advantageThe idea that two trading partners can both gain when each produces what it gives up least to make.Read more about Comparative advantage. It is not about who is best at something. It is about who gives up the least to make it.

A two-country example

Example

Two countries each have 100 worker-days (example).

  • In country A, one worker-day makes 10 units of food or 5 units of clothing.
  • In country B, one worker-day makes 4 units of food or 4 units of clothing.

A is more productive at both. But look at what each gives up:

  • In A, 1 unit of clothing costs 2 units of food.
  • In B, 1 unit of clothing costs 1 unit of food.

B gives up less to make clothing. B has the comparative advantage in clothing; A has it in food.

Without trade, each splits its days in half. A makes 500 food and 250 clothing; B makes 200 food and 200 clothing. Total: 700 food and 450 clothing.

With specialisation, B makes only clothing: 400 units. A spends 10 days on clothing (50 units) and 90 days on food (900 units). Total: 900 food and 450 clothing.

The two countries together have the same clothing and 200 more units of food. Trade lets them share that extra output.

Who gains and who loses

Shoppers gain from lower prices and more choice, and exporters gain from bigger markets. These gains are spread across millions of people, a little each. The losses fall on the workers and firms that compete with imports, often in particular towns or regions, and they can be large for those people. This difference helps explain why trade is a political question.

Who pays a tariff?

A tariffTariffA tax on imported goods, collected at the border and often passed on to buyers.Read more about Tariff is a tax on imports. It is paid at the border by the business that imports the goods, not by the foreign country.

Example

An importer buys a product for ¤100 (example). A 10% tariff adds ¤10 at the border. The importer can pass the ¤10 on to customers, take a smaller profit, or ask the foreign seller to cut its price. Much of the cost usually ends up with buyers in the importing country, but the split varies.

The trade balance is not a scoreboard

A trade deficitTrade balanceExports minus imports of goods, and of services when stated, over a period.Read more about Trade balance means a country buys more from abroad than it sells. It is often described as “losing”. But a deficit mainly reflects that the country spends and invests more than it saves, and borrows or sells assets abroad to pay for it. A surplus means the reverse. Neither is good or bad in itself.

Supply chains in your basket

A phone, a shirt or a box of cereal often contains parts and ingredients from many countries. A drought, a port closure or a change in exchange rates far away can change local prices. Detailed version

Models of trade

  • Ricardian model: trade follows differences in relative labour productivity, as in the example above. Gains arise whenever opportunity costs differ.
  • Factor-endowment (Heckscher–Ohlin) model: countries export goods that use intensively the factors they have in abundance (labour, land, capital). A corollary (Stolper–Samuelson) is that trade raises the return to the abundant factor and lowers it for the scarce one, which predicts winners and losers within each country.
  • New trade theory: economies of scale and consumers’ taste for variety explain trade between similar countries in similar goods. Paul Krugman’s work on this received the 2008 Nobel Prize (Nobel Prize).

Tariff incidence

The share of a tariff borne by domestic buyers depends on the elasticities of supply and demand. A large importing country can push part of the cost onto foreign exporters; a small one generally cannot. Studies of the tariffs the United States introduced in 2018 found that the tariffs were passed through almost fully into the prices US importers paid (Amiti, Redding and Weinstein, 2019). Tariffs also shift demand to domestic producers, raise government revenue and can prompt retaliation.

The current account identity

From the national accounts:

current account = national saving − domestic investment
CA = S − I

The current account includes the trade balance in goods and services plus primary income (such as interest and dividends) and secondary income (such as remittances) (Eurostat). A current account deficit is matched by a financial account surplus: net borrowing from, or sales of assets to, the rest of the world (Eurostat). Policies that change saving or investment change the trade balance; a tariff alone may not, if saving and investment are unchanged.

Trade rules

The World Trade Organization provides rules agreed by its members, including non-discrimination (most-favoured-nation treatment and national treatment), bound tariff ceilings, and a dispute settlement process (WTO, WTO tariffs). Regional and bilateral trade agreements go further between their members. Exceptions exist for security, safeguards and trade remedies such as anti-dumping duties.

Evidence on trade and labour markets

Aggregate gains from trade are well established in theory and evidence. The distribution of effects has received more attention since the 2010s. Autor, Dorn and Hanson found that US local labour markets more exposed to rising imports from China saw larger falls in manufacturing employment and wages and larger increases in benefit receipt, with effects that persisted (Autor, Dorn and Hanson, 2013). The findings informed debate on how adjustment costs are shared.

Why people disagree

Economists broadly agree that trade raises total income, but disagree about policy. One view holds that open trade with compensation for those who lose (through retraining, transfers or regional policy) is the most efficient approach. Another view holds that compensation has rarely been delivered at the scale needed and that some protection or industrial policy is justified to preserve jobs, communities or strategic industries. A third view stresses national security and supply-chain resilience over cost. Evidence on job losses in exposed regions (Autor, Dorn and Hanson) and on tariff pass-through (Amiti, Redding and Weinstein) is cited on several sides of the debate.

Varies by country

Trade policy is set at different levels. The European Union sets one common external tariff for all its member states, so individual EU countries do not set their own tariffs. Members of the WTO bind their tariff rates at agreed ceilings (WTO). Many countries belong to regional agreements, for example in Southeast Asia, Africa and the Americas, that reduce tariffs between members.
