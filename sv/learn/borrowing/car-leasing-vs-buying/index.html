Car Leasing vs Buying

The four ways to pay for a car compared on total cost, ownership and flexibility.

Intermediate·5 min (Simple) · 9 min (Detailed)·Updated 6 November 2026

Read first: Personal and Car Loans

Key points

  • Cash, a loan, hire purchase and a lease end in different places: with the car, with a balloon decision, or with nothing to own.
  • Depreciation is the largest cost in the early years whichever route is used.
  • Lease payments cover depreciation plus interest plus the lessor's margin, with mileage and condition limits.
  • The cost of ending early differs sharply between contracts.
  • Insurance, tax and maintenance are car costs under every route.

Simple: plain words. Detailed: exact figures, codes and methods.

In short: whichever way you pay, a car loses value while you use it. Leasing, buying with finance and buying with cash share out that loss, the interest and the risk in different ways.

Simple version

Four routes, side by side

Cash Loan or hire purchase Balloon plan (PCP-type) Lease
What you pay The full price at once Monthly payments that cover the whole price A deposit, lower monthly payments, and an optional final payment A monthly rental, sometimes an upfront payment
What you own at the end The car The car Your choice: pay the final sum and keep it, hand it back, or trade it in Nothing; the car goes back
If you stop early Sell the car Repay the balance, or use the legal early-exit rights where they exist Repay the balance, or use early-exit rights; a gap can remain Often a large early-termination charge
Limits None None Mileage and condition, if you hand it back Mileage and condition

The same car four ways

Example

A car costs ¤30,000 (example) and is forecast to be worth ¤16,500 after three years. All numbers are illustrations.

  • Cash: you pay ¤30,000 and own a car worth ¤16,500. The car has cost ¤13,500 in lost value. The ¤30,000 could also have earned interest: at 3% a year, about ¤2,782 over three years.
  • Loan or hire purchase: with a ¤3,000 deposit and ¤27,000 borrowed at 7% over three years, you pay about ¤833.68 a month, ¤33,012.54 in total including the deposit. You own the car, worth ¤16,500.
  • Balloon plan: with a ¤3,000 deposit, a ¤15,000 final payment and 7%, the monthly payment is about ¤458.03. After three years you have paid about ¤19,489. You then pay ¤15,000 to keep the car or hand it back.
  • Lease: priced on the ¤13,500 of lost value plus a finance charge at a rate equal to 7%, the payment is about ¤510.63 a month, or ¤18,382.50 over three years. You own nothing at the end.

The monthly payment is lowest on the balloon plan and the lease, because you are paying mostly for the value the car loses, not for the whole car.

What is the same under every route

Depreciation is the biggest cost in the first years. Insurance, road tax, fuel or charging, tyres and servicing are the same car costs whichever way you pay. Some leases include maintenance, which is then part of the payment rather than separate.

Questions to ask

These questions bring out how a contract works:

  1. What is the total I pay over the contract, including deposit and fees?
  2. What is the mileage limit, and the charge per extra kilometre or mile?
  3. What counts as fair wear and tear when the car goes back?
  4. What does it cost to end the contract after one year? After two?
  5. For a balloon plan: is the final payment guaranteed, and what happens if the car is worth less?
  6. Is a commission paid to the dealer for arranging the finance?

Detailed version

Residual value and who carries the risk

The residual value (the forecast value at the end of the term) decides the payment on leases and balloon plans. Its risk sits with:

  • the owner under cash or a loan (if the car is worth less, the owner absorbs it),
  • the lessor under a closed-end lease (the customer hands back the car regardless of its market value, subject to mileage and condition),
  • the customer under an open-end lease, which settles the difference between residual and actual value,
  • the finance company under a balloon plan with a guaranteed future value (GFV), because the customer can hand the car back instead of paying the balloon.

In the United States, consumer leases must disclose the gross capitalised cost, residual value, rent charge and early-termination conditions (Regulation M).

Money factor and APR

Many leases quote a money factor rather than a rate. The monthly finance charge is (capitalised cost + residual) × MF, and:

APR ≈ MF × 2400        e.g. MF 0.0025 → about 6%

The approximation works because (C + R) / 2 is close to the average amount outstanding over the term. In the example, depreciation of 13,500 / 36 = ¤375.00 plus finance of 46,500 × 0.0029167 = ¤135.63 gives ¤510.63.

Guaranteed future value and negative equity

With a balloon plan, the outstanding balance at the end equals the GFV. Before the end, the balance can exceed the car’s market value, so trading in early leaves a gap that is paid or rolled into a new agreement. At the end, if the market value is above the GFV, the difference is the customer’s equity, which dealers use as the deposit on the next agreement. That cycle is a feature of the product, not a sign of a problem with any one contract.

Commission rulings

UK regulators banned discretionary commission in motor finance, under which a dealer’s commission rose with the rate it set, from January 2021, and later handled complaints about earlier arrangements (FCA). Disclosure of intermediary pay is also part of EU consumer credit rules (Directive (EU) 2023/2225).

Depreciation by type of car

Depreciation rates differ by model, age, mileage and drivetrain, and they change as technology and demand change. Residual-value forecasts for newer technologies, including electric vehicles, have a wider range of uncertainty because there is less resale history. That uncertainty is priced into leases through the residual value the lessor chooses.

Common misconceptions

  • “Leasing is throwing money away.” A lease buys the use of a car for a period; the cost comparison is with the depreciation and interest an owner bears over the same period.
  • “Buying is cheaper.” Over a short holding period with fast depreciation, it may not be; over a long holding period it often is. The tool shows the numbers.

Varies by country

Car finance products differ in name and rules. In the United Kingdom, PCP and hire purchase agreements carry a legal right to hand the car back once half of the total amount payable has been paid. In the United States, consumer leases are disclosed under Regulation M, and both closed-end and open-end leases exist. In the European Union, car loans to consumers fall under consumer credit rules, including the right to repay early.
