Unit Prices and Pack Sizes

How to compare like with like at the shelf, using the price per kilo, litre or item.

Everyday·4 min (Simple) · 7 min (Detailed)·Updated 6 November 2026

Read first: Percentages, Averages and Charts, Step by Step

Key points

  • The price per unit (per kilo, litre or item) is the fair way to compare packs of different sizes.
  • A bigger pack is not always cheaper per unit.
  • Unit-price labels are required by law in some countries and not in others.
  • A multi-buy deal lowers the price per unit only if you use everything you buy.
  • Branded and own-label products can differ less than their prices suggest; the label and the contents are separate questions.

Simple: plain words. Detailed: exact figures, codes and methods.

In short: to compare two packs fairly, divide the price by the amount. The pack with the lower price per kilo, litre or item costs less for the same quantity.

Simple version

Price per unit

The unit priceUnit priceThe price per standard quantity, such as per kilo or per litre.Read more about Unit price is the price divided by the amount in the pack:

unit price = price ÷ amount

It answers one question: what does the same quantity cost in each pack?

Three shelf examples

Example

Rice (example). A 500 g bag costs ¤2.40. A 1 kg bag costs ¤5.20.

  • 500 g bag: ¤2.40 ÷ 0.5 kg = ¤4.80 per kg.
  • 1 kg bag: ¤5.20 ÷ 1 kg = ¤5.20 per kg.

Here the smaller bag costs less per kilo, even though it is the smaller pack.

Example

Drinks (example). A 1.5 litre bottle costs ¤1.80. A pack of six 330 ml cans costs ¤3.00.

  • Bottle: ¤1.80 ÷ 1.5 L = ¤1.20 per litre.
  • Cans: 6 × 0.33 L = 1.98 L, so ¤3.00 ÷ 1.98 L = about ¤1.52 per litre.

The cans cost about a quarter more per litre. They may still suit someone who wants single portions; the unit price shows what that convenience costs.

Example

Multi-buy (example). Yoghurt costs ¤2.00 a pot, or 3 for ¤5.00.

The deal makes each pot about ¤1.67. But if one pot goes off before it is eaten, you paid ¤5.00 for 2 pots: ¤2.50 each, more than the single price.

Doing it fast in your head

Exact division is not needed in the shop. Two shortcuts help:

  • Scale to the same size. If one pack is twice the size, double its rival’s price and compare.
  • Round, then compare. ¤3.00 for 1.98 L is close to ¤3.00 for 2 L, so about ¤1.50 a litre.

Many shelf labels already print the unit price in small type. Check that both labels use the same unit: one might show price per 100 g and the other price per kg.

When the unit price is not the whole story

A lower unit price saves money only on what you use. A big pack that goes stale, or a giant bottle that takes up a cupboard you need for something else, can cost more in the end. Some people also find that having a large pack at home means they use more of it. Lesson 11.7 looks at these cases in detail.

Price per unit also says nothing about quality. Two packs of washing powder with the same unit price might wash a different number of loads. For goods like this, the price per use (per wash, per sheet, per portion) is the fairer test.

Brands and own-label

Shops often sell their own label next to well-known brands. Sometimes the contents are close; sometimes they differ in recipe, quality or size. The unit price tells you the cost difference. Whether the products are the same is a separate question, answered by the ingredients list, the size and your own taste.

Detailed version

Non-linear pricing

Sellers rarely charge a constant price per unit. Non-linear pricing means the price per unit changes with the quantity bought. Common forms:

  • Quantity discounts: larger packs or volume tiers with a lower unit price.
  • Quantity premiums: larger packs with a higher unit price, which occur when sellers expect shoppers to assume “bigger is always cheaper”.
  • Two-part tariffs: a fixed fee plus a per-unit price (for example a warehouse club membership plus shelf prices).
  • Bundles and multi-buys: a lower price for a set number of units.

Price discrimination by self-selection

Offering several pack sizes lets a seller charge different customers different effective prices without knowing who they are. This is second-degree price discrimination: customers sort themselves by choosing a pack. Households with storage space, cash to spare and predictable use tend to buy larger packs; households with tight budgets or little space pay a higher unit price for smaller packs. The same mechanism explains why the unit price gap between sizes can differ between shops and over time.

Shrinkflation and unit price

When a pack shrinks and the price stays the same, the unit price rises by:

change = (old size ÷ new size) − 1

From 500 g to 450 g: 500 ÷ 450 − 1 = 11.1%. Price statisticians count this as a price rise, because the CPI prices a fixed quantity (ONS).

Unit-pricing regulation

Unit-price labels lower the time cost of comparison. Regulation differs in which shops and goods are covered, the units allowed (per kg, per 100 g, per item) and the size and placement of the label. The EU requires the unit price to be shown for most goods sold to consumers by traders, with permitted exemptions for small shops in some member states (Directive 98/6/EC). The UK has its own Price Marking Order (legislation.gov.uk). Australia applies a mandatory grocery unit-pricing code under its competition and consumer law (Federal Register of Legislation).

Limits of the unit price

The unit price compares cost per quantity, not cost per use. Waste, spoilage, storage, quality and how much of the product is consumed all change the cost per useful unit. Lesson 11.7 extends the calculation to bulk buying.

Varies by country

In the European Union, traders are required to show the unit price for most goods, alongside the selling price (EUR-Lex). In Australia, large grocery retailers are required to display unit prices under an industry code. In the United States there is no national requirement; some states and cities require unit pricing and others do not. Where labels are not required, the calculation can still be done by hand.
