What Money Is (and Isn't)

Money is anything a society agrees to use to measure value, trade and store wealth.

Beginner·5 min (Simple) · 9 min (Detailed)·Updated 6 November 2026

Key points

  • Money has three jobs: it measures value, it is used to exchange, and it stores value for later.
  • Modern money is “fiat” money: it has value because people accept it and the law backs it, not because it is made of gold or silver.
  • In many economies, most money is held as bank deposits, not as notes and coins.
  • The value of money is what it can buy (its purchasing power), and that changes over time.

Simple: plain words. Detailed: exact figures, codes and methods.

In short: money is a tool that a whole society agrees to use. It works because everyone expects everyone else to accept it.

Simple version

The problem money solves

Imagine a world without money. A baker who wants shoes has to find a shoemaker who wants bread, at the same time and in the same amount. Economists call this the double coincidence of wants, and it makes trade slow. Swapping goods directly is called barter.

Money removes that problem. The baker sells bread for money and uses the money to buy shoes from anyone who sells them.

The three jobs of money

Money does three jobs at once:

  1. It measures value. Prices in one unit (for example ¤) make it possible to compare a bus ticket with a sandwich. Economists call this a unit of account.
  2. It is used to exchange. You hand it over and get goods or services back. This is a medium of exchange.
  3. It stores value. You can earn money today and spend it next month. This is a store of value.

Coins, notes and bank balances

Money comes in several forms. Coins and notes are the ones you can touch. The number in a bank account is money too: you can pay with a card or a transfer without any cash changing hands.

Example

A nurse is paid ¤2,000 (example) into a bank account. She pays rent by transfer, buys food by card and keeps ¤40 in cash. All three are money; the cash is the smallest part.

Why does a note have value?

A banknote is printed paper or plastic. It has value because people accept it in payment, and because the law says it can be used to settle debts in that country (it is legal tender). Money like this is called fiat money. It is not exchangeable for a fixed amount of gold.

Too much or too little money

What money can buy is called its purchasing power. If the amount of money grows much faster than the goods and services there are to buy, prices tend to rise. A general rise in prices over time is called inflationInflationA general rise in prices over time, which reduces what money buys.Read more about Inflation, and it means each ¤ buys a little less. Later lessons explain how this is measured.

Detailed version

Functions of money

Textbooks list three functions: unit of account (prices are quoted in it), medium of exchange (it settles transactions) and store of value (it carries purchasing power through time). An asset can do one job well and another badly. A currency with high and variable inflation can still be used to pay for things while being a poor store of value; in that situation people tend to save in other assets or in a foreign currency.

Measuring the money stock

Central banks publish monetary aggregates, labelled differently in each country. The European Central Bank, for example, defines a narrow aggregate (M1: banknotes, coins and deposits that can be withdrawn quickly) and a broad aggregate (M3: M1 plus longer-term deposits and similar assets) (ECB). Generically:

Layer Typical contents
Cash Notes and coins held by the public
Narrow money Cash plus overnight (instant-access) deposits
Broad money Narrow money plus time deposits, savings deposits and close substitutes

Definitions, names (M1, M2, M3, M4) and the exact items included vary by country, so cross-country comparisons use each central bank’s own notes.

How bank lending creates money

When a commercial bank makes a loan, it credits the borrower’s account with a new deposit. That deposit is new money. When the loan is repaid, the deposit is extinguished. The Bank of England describes this as the way most money in a modern economy is created, and notes that banks do not lend out existing deposits or “multiply up” central bank money in a fixed ratio (Bank of England, 2014). The total created depends on the central bank’s monetary policy, the demand for loans, and banks’ own lending standards and regulation.

Two kinds of money

  • Central bank money: banknotes, plus the deposits that commercial banks hold at the central bank (reserves). Only the central bank can create it.
  • Commercial bank money: the deposits households and firms hold at commercial banks. Banks settle payments between themselves using reserves.

This two-tier structure is why a card payment from a customer of one bank to a customer of another bank ends with a transfer of reserves between the two banks.

Legal tender versus acceptance

Legal tender is a legal status: money that is valid to settle a debt. It does not by itself oblige a shop to take cash for every purchase, and the rules differ by country. In some euro-area countries the law limits large cash payments; elsewhere shops may choose to be card-only. In practice, most payments rely on acceptance and trust rather than on legal-tender rules.

A short history

  • Commodity money: the item has value of its own (grain, metal coins).
  • Representative money: paper notes that could be exchanged for a fixed amount of gold or silver held by the issuer.
  • Fiat money: notes and deposits with no promise of conversion into a commodity. The value rests on acceptance, legal status and the central bank’s management of the money supply.

Digital central bank money

Several central banks are studying or testing a digital form of central bank money for everyday payments. The European Central Bank, for example, publishes its work on a digital euro (ECB). This is an area of ongoing work; designs, timelines and decisions differ between countries.
