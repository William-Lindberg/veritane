Income, Expenses and Cash Flow

The simplest model of personal finance: what comes in, what goes out, and what is left.

Beginner·5 min (Simple) · 8 min (Detailed)·Updated 6 November 2026

Key points

  • Cash flow is income minus expenses over a period, such as a month or a year.
  • Gross income is pay before deductions; net income is what actually arrives in your account.
  • Fixed expenses stay about the same each month; variable expenses change; occasional ones arrive once or twice a year.
  • A surplus (income left after expenses) is the only source of saving and of paying down debt.
  • Timing matters: several positive months can hide a negative year once yearly bills are counted.

Simple: plain words. Detailed: exact figures, codes and methods.

In short: cash flow is the money that comes in, minus the money that goes out, over a set period. What is left over is what you can save or use to pay down debt.

Simple version

Money in, money out

Picture your money as water in a bucket. Pay, benefits and gifts pour in at the top. Rent, food and bills drain out at the bottom. What stays in the bucket at the end of the month is your cash flow for that month.

  • If more comes in than goes out, you have a surplus.
  • If more goes out than comes in, you have a shortfall (also called a deficit).

A shortfall has to be covered from somewhere: savings, a loan, a credit card or help from family. That is why the size and direction of cash flow matter so much.

Reading a pay slip

A pay slip (the note an employer gives with your wages) has three main lines:

  1. Gross incomeGross incomeIncome before taxes and deductions.Read more about Gross income: your pay before anything is taken off.
  2. Deductions: amounts taken off before you are paid. These can include income tax, social insurance contributions and pension contributions. Which deductions apply, and how large they are, varies by country.
  3. Net incomeNet incomeIncome after taxes and deductions; take-home pay.Read more about Net income: what actually arrives in your account. This is sometimes called “take-home pay”.

When you plan, the number that matters is net income, because that is the money you can spend.

Three kinds of costs

Expenses fall into three groups:

  • Fixed costsFixed costAn expense that stays the same each period, such as rent.Read more about Fixed cost are about the same every month: rent, a phone contract, a transport pass, loan repayments.
  • Variable costsVariable costAn expense that changes from period to period, such as groceries.Read more about Variable cost change from month to month: food, energy, fuel, going out.
  • Occasional costs arrive once or twice a year: a yearly insurance bill, a car service, school uniforms, holiday gifts.

Occasional costs are easy to forget, because they do not appear every month. When they arrive, they can turn a good month into a shortfall.

Example

A shop worker has a net income of ¤2,000 a month (example).

Item Monthly amount
Fixed costs (rent ¤800, phone ¤30, transport ¤70, insurance ¤50) ¤950
Variable costs (food ¤350, energy ¤90, other ¤260) ¤700
Occasional costs (¤1,200 a year, spread as ¤100 a month) ¤100
Total expenses ¤1,750
Surplus (¤2,000 − ¤1,750) ¤250

The ¤250 is the money available each month for saving, paying down debt or spending on something else.

Where the surplus goes

A surplus does not have to sit still. People use it in different ways:

  • building a cash reserve for surprises (see the emergency fund lesson in the next module),
  • paying down a loan or card balance faster,
  • saving for a goal, such as a trip or a deposit on a home,
  • spending on things they enjoy.

There is no single right split. The point of seeing your cash flow is that you can decide where the surplus goes before it disappears.

A good month can hide a bad year

Cash flow depends on the period you look at. A month with no big bills can show a surplus, while the full year is negative.

Suppose a household has a surplus of ¤150 every month (example). Over 12 months that is ¤1,800. If one yearly bill of ¤2,000 arrives in March, the year ends ¤200 short, even though 11 of the 12 months looked fine. Looking at a whole year, or spreading yearly costs across the months, shows this in advance.

Detailed version

Two household statements

Companies publish a cash-flow statement and a balance sheet. Households can use the same two views:

Statement Question it answers Measured
Cash-flow statement How much came in and went out? Over a period (a flow)
Balance sheetBalance sheetA snapshot of what is owned and what is owed at one point in time.Read more about Balance sheet What do I own and owe right now? At a point in time (a stock)

The two are linked. A surplus raises cash or lowers debt, so it shows up on the next balance sheet as higher net worthNet worthEverything owned minus everything owed.Read more about Net worth. A shortfall does the reverse.

Income, consumption and saving

In national accounts, a household’s disposable incomeDisposable incomeIncome left after taxes and compulsory contributions.Read more about Disposable income is income after taxes and social contributions, plus benefits received (Eurostat). By definition, it is either spent (consumption) or not spent (saving):

disposable income = consumption + saving
saving rate = saving ÷ disposable income

Example: disposable income of ¤24,000 a year and consumption of ¤21,000 gives saving of ¤3,000 and a saving rate of 3,000 ÷ 24,000 = 12.5%. Statistics offices publish a household saving rate on this basis (Eurostat), though definitions differ in detail between countries. Note that paying down a loan’s principal counts as saving in this identity (it reduces a liability), while the interest counts as an expense.

Accrual thinking

Businesses use accrual accounting: a cost is counted in the period it relates to, not when the cash leaves. A household can do the same by spreading occasional costs:

monthly provision = yearly cost ÷ 12

A ¤1,200 yearly insurance premium becomes a ¤100 monthly provision. Money set aside this way in a separate pot is often called a sinking fundSinking fundMoney set aside each month for a known future expense.Read more about Sinking fund. When the bill arrives, the cash is already there, and the monthly cash-flow picture matches the yearly one.

Irregular income

Freelance, seasonal, commission and gig income can vary widely from month to month. Common smoothing approaches:

  • Baseline month: plan fixed costs against a low month (for example, the lowest of the last 12) rather than the typical one.
  • Holding account: all income lands in one account, and a fixed “salary” is moved from it to a spending account each month.
  • Percentage rules: a set share of each payment goes to tax, savings and spending as it arrives.

Each method trades simplicity against how quickly spending responds to a good or a poor month.

Data on household spending

Household budget surveys run by statistics offices collect detailed income and spending data from samples of households (Eurostat). They are the source of the expenditure weights used in consumer price indices, and they show how spending patterns differ by income, age and household type.

Common misconceptions

  • “A pay rise means a matching rise in take-home pay.” Deductions are often a percentage, and some rise in steps, so net pay rises by less than gross pay.
  • “Positive every month means positive every year.” Not when occasional costs are left out.
  • “Cash flow and profit are the same.” For a household, buying a car with cash is a large negative cash flow but swaps one asset (cash) for another (the car).

Varies by country

Pay slips look different around the world. In the United Kingdom, employers deduct income tax and National Insurance through the PAYE (pay as you earn) system. In the United States, employers withhold federal income tax and Social Security and Medicare contributions. In Australia, employers pay a compulsory retirement contribution (the superannuation guarantee) on top of wages rather than deducting it from them. In many countries, informal or cash work has no pay slip at all.
