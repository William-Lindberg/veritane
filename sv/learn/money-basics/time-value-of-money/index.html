Why ¤100 Today Beats ¤100 Next Year

The idea behind every loan, investment and pension: money now is worth more than the same money later.

Everyday·6 min (Simple) · 12 min (Detailed)·Updated 6 November 2026

Read first: Interest: The Price of Time

Key points

  • Money received earlier can be used or can earn interest; money promised for later is uncertain and loses buying power to inflation.
  • Present value turns a future amount into its equivalent in today's money.
  • The discount rate used for that conversion reflects interest, inflation and risk.
  • Two options can only be compared fairly when both are measured at the same point in time.

Simple: plain words. Detailed: exact figures, codes and methods.

In short: an amount of money today is worth more than the same amount later, because today’s money can earn interest, can be used now, and is certain. This idea is called the time value of moneyTime value of moneyThe principle that money available now is worth more than the same amount later.Read more about Time value of money.

Simple version

¤100 now or ¤105 in a year?

Someone offers you a choice (example): ¤100 today, or ¤105 in one year. Which is worth more?

It depends on three things.

  1. What you could earn. If a savings account pays 3% a year, ¤100 today becomes ¤103 in a year. The ¤105 offer is ahead by ¤2. If the account paid 6%, ¤100 would grow to ¤106, and taking the money today would come out ahead.
  2. What prices do. If prices rise by 4% over the year (inflationInflationA general rise in prices over time, which reduces what money buys.Read more about Inflation), ¤105 next year buys only a little more than ¤100 buys today.
  3. Whether you trust the promise. ¤100 in your hand is certain. ¤105 next year depends on the other person keeping their word.

So there is no single answer. But the method does not change: bring both amounts to the same moment and look at them side by side.

Bringing future money back to today

The amount that a future sum is worth today is called its present valuePresent valueWhat a future amount of money is worth today after discounting.Read more about Present value. To find it, you reverse compound interest. This is called discounting, and the rate you use is the discount rateDiscount rateThe rate used to convert future money into its value today.Read more about Discount rate.

At a 5% discount rate, ¤105 in one year is worth 105 ÷ 1.05 = ¤100 today. At 3%, it is worth about ¤101.94 today. At 6%, about ¤99.06. The higher the rate, the less a future amount is worth now.

A lottery choice (made-up numbers)

Imagine winning a prize with two payout options (example):

  • Option A: ¤600,000 today.
  • Option B: ¤50,000 a year for 20 years, starting one year from now. That adds up to ¤1,000,000.

Option B pays more in total, but most of it arrives far in the future. Its present value depends on the discount rate:

Discount rate Present value of Option B
2% a year about ¤817,572
4% a year about ¤679,516
7% a year about ¤529,701

At 2% or 4%, Option B is worth more than ¤600,000 in today’s terms. At 7%, it is worth less. The two options are worth the same at a rate of about 5.45%.

Taxes, the need for cash now and how sure the payments are also matter in real decisions. The calculation does not decide for you; it shows what you are trading.

Where you meet this idea

  • Loans: you get money now and pay more back later.
  • Savings and investing: you give up spending now for more later.
  • Pensions: a promise of income decades away.
  • Shopping: “pay ¤1,200 now or ¤110 a month for 12 months” is a time-value question.

Detailed version

Present and future value

FV = PV × (1 + r)^n
PV = FV ÷ (1 + r)^n

r is the rate per period and n the number of periods. With compounding m times a year at nominal rate j, use r = j/m and n = m × years.

Annuities and perpetuities

An annuityAnnuityA contract that turns a lump sum into a stream of regular payments, often guaranteed for life.Read more about Annuity is a stream of equal payments at equal intervals. For payment C at the end of each of n periods:

PV(annuity) = C × [1 − (1 + r)^−n] ÷ r
PV(annuity due) = PV(annuity) × (1 + r)     (payments at the start of each period)

A perpetuity pays C forever: PV = C ÷ r. Example: ¤1,000 a year forever at 5% has a present value of ¤20,000. A growing perpetuity, with payments rising at rate g < r, has PV = C ÷ (r − g).

The lottery table in the Simple layer uses the ordinary annuity formula with C = ¤50,000 and n = 20.

Net present value of a decision

Net present value (NPV) sums the present values of all cash flows, including the cost today:

NPV = Σ CF_t ÷ (1 + r)^t,  t = 0 … n

Example: pay ¤1,000 today to receive ¤300 at the end of each of the next 4 years. At 6%, the inflows are worth 300 × [1 − 1.06^−4] ÷ 0.06 = ¤1,039.53, so NPV = +¤39.53. A positive NPV means the project returns more than the discount rate; a negative one, less.

Internal rate of return

The internal rate of return (IRR) is the discount rate that makes NPV equal to zero. In the example above it is about 7.71%. IRR is convenient as a single percentage, but it can mislead: projects with cash flows that change sign more than once can have several IRRs, and IRR ignores the scale of a project. For this reason, finance textbooks treat NPV at a stated rate as the primary measure and IRR as a summary.

Real and nominal discounting

Cash flows and the discount rate need to be on the same basis. Either discount nominal cash flows (in money of the day) at a nominal rate, or real cash flows (in today’s prices) at a real rate. The link is:

(1 + nominal) = (1 + real) × (1 + inflation)
real = (1 + nominal) ÷ (1 + inflation) − 1

Example: a nominal rate of 6% with inflation of 2% gives a real rate of 1.06 ÷ 1.02 − 1 = 3.92%, slightly less than the shortcut 6 − 2 = 4%. Central banks describe how inflation erodes what money buys over time (Bank of England) and how interest rates relate to it (Bank of England).

What goes into the discount rate

A practical discount rate is often built from three parts: a risk-free rate (the return on a very safe asset over the same period), expected inflation (if the rate is nominal) and a risk premium for the uncertainty of the cash flows. Choosing it is a judgement, and results can change sign as it changes.

Common misconceptions

  • “The option with the larger total is worth more.” Totals ignore timing.
  • “The discount rate is just the bank rate.” It also reflects risk and the alternatives given up.
  • “NPV gives the answer.” It is only as good as the cash-flow forecasts and the rate.

Why people disagree

Which discount rate to use over very long periods, such as for climate policy or public infrastructure lasting a century, is debated among economists. One view holds that the rate should match observed market returns and how much people actually prefer the present, which gives a relatively high rate and a low weight on costs and benefits far in the future. Another view holds that, for ethical reasons, future generations’ wellbeing should count almost equally with today’s, which gives a low rate and a much higher weight on the distant future. Both sides accept the arithmetic; they disagree about what the rate should represent. Some governments publish an official discount rate for public projects, and these differ between countries.
