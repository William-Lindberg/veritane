Who Does What in the Financial System

A map of banks, central banks, markets, insurers, regulators and you, and how money moves between them.

Everyday·6 min (Simple) · 11 min (Detailed)·Updated 6 November 2026

Read first: What Money Is (and Isn't)

Key points

  • Households, firms and governments are the main players: they earn, spend, save and borrow.
  • Banks and financial markets move money from people who save to people who borrow.
  • Central banks manage the currency and short-term interest rates; regulators set and enforce the rules.
  • Payment systems are the plumbing that moves money between accounts.
  • Knowing the map shows where to look, and who to contact, when something goes wrong.

Simple: plain words. Detailed: exact figures, codes and methods.

In short: the financial system is the network of banks, markets, insurers, payment systems, central banksCentral bankThe institution that issues a country's or region's currency, sets the policy interest rate and often supervises banks.Read more about Central bank and regulators that moves money between people who have it and people who need it.

Simple version

The main players

Three groups do most of the earning, spending, saving and borrowing:

  • Households: people and families. They earn wages, pay for things, save and take out loans.
  • Firms: businesses of every size. They pay wages, sell goods and services, and borrow to invest.
  • Governments: they collect taxes, pay for public services and benefits, and borrow by selling bonds.

Around these players sit the organisations that make the system work.

Who What they do, in one line
Banks and credit unions Hold deposits, make loans and process payments
Financial markets Places where shares, bonds and currencies are bought and sold
Insurers Collect premiums and pay claims when an insured event happens
Pension funds and investment funds Pool many people’s savings and invest them
Payment systems Move money between accounts, at home and across borders
Central bank Issues the currency and sets the main interest rate
Regulators Write and enforce the rules for all of the above

Where does my salary come from and go?

Follow one month’s pay (example):

  1. A firm earns money from customers and pays you ¤2,000 by bank transfer. A payment system moves the money from the firm’s bank to yours.
  2. Part of your pay goes to taxes and social contributions, which fund public services.
  3. You pay rent to a landlord, who may use it to repay a mortgage to a bank.
  4. You buy food by card. The payment goes through a card network to the shop’s bank.
  5. You put ¤100 in savings. The bank uses deposits like yours, together with its other funding, when it makes loans to other households and firms.

Every step uses part of the financial system. Most of the time it is invisible.

Savers and borrowers

The central job of the system is to connect people who have spare money with people who want to borrow it. A bank does this by taking deposits and making loans. A market does it more directly: a firm sells bonds or shares to investors.

Example

A bank pays savers 2% a year on deposits and lends at 6% a year (example). On ¤1,000 of loans funded by ¤1,000 of deposits, it receives ¤60 and pays ¤20. The ¤40 difference has to cover the bank’s staff, systems and loans that are not repaid, before any profit.

When something goes wrong

When people have a problem with a bank, insurer or payment, the usual route is:

  1. A complaint goes to the firm itself first, often in writing.
  2. If that does not resolve it, it can go to an independent ombudsmanOmbudsmanAn independent body that resolves complaints between consumers and companies, often free of charge; names vary by country.Read more about Ombudsman or dispute service, where one exists.
  3. Wider problems, such as misleading adverts or scams, can be reported to the regulator or a consumer protection agency.

Which organisations exist, and what they can do, varies by country (see the box below).

Detailed version

Circular flow with intermediation

In the basic circular-flow model, households supply labour to firms and receive income, which they spend on firms’ output. Adding the financial sector closes the gap between saving and investment: household saving flows through intermediaries to firms and governments that borrow. The flow can also run the other way, for example when firms hold large cash balances.

Direct and indirect finance

  • Direct finance: borrowers raise money from investors by issuing securities (bonds, shares). Investment banks and brokers arrange the deal, but investors hold the claim.
  • Indirect finance: an intermediary (a bank, insurer or fund) issues its own liabilities (deposits, policies, fund units) and holds the loans or securities itself. This adds maturity transformation (short-term deposits funding long-term loans) and risk pooling.

Payment-system layers

Layer Examples Settles in
Retail Cards, instant transfers, direct debits, mobile money Commercial bank money
Clearing Card schemes and automated clearing houses that net many payments Net positions between banks
Settlement Real-time gross settlement (RTGS) systems run by central banks Central bank money (reserves)

Examples of central-bank settlement systems include the Federal Reserve’s services in the United States (Federal Reserve) and the Eurosystem’s TARGET services in the euro area (ECB).

Types of regulator

Regulators are usually grouped by objective:

  • Prudential: the safety and soundness of banks and insurers (capital, liquidity).
  • Conduct: how firms treat customers and behave in markets.
  • Market: trading venues, securities issuance and disclosure.
  • Data and competition: privacy, open banking and competition between providers.

Structures differ. The United Kingdom splits prudential regulation (the PRA, part of the Bank of England) from conduct regulation (the FCA) (Bank of England, FCA). Australia’s “twin peaks” model has APRA for prudential regulation and ASIC for markets and conduct (APRA, ASIC). In the European Union, national authorities work alongside EU-level bodies such as the EBA for banking and ESMA for securities markets (EBA, ESMA), and the ECB supervises the largest euro-area banks directly.

International bodies

  • International Monetary Fund (IMF): monitors member countries’ economies and exchange rates, lends to countries facing balance-of-payments problems, and provides technical assistance.
  • World Bank Group: finances and advises on development projects and policy in low- and middle-income countries (World Bank).
  • Bank for International Settlements (BIS): owned by central banks; supports their cooperation and hosts committees that set international standards, such as the Basel Committee on Banking Supervision (BIS).
  • Financial Stability Board (FSB): coordinates national authorities and standard setters on financial-stability policy, reporting to the G20 (FSB).

These bodies set standards and give advice; national law decides what applies in each country.

Common misconceptions

  • “The central bank regulates every financial firm.” In many countries it does not; several agencies share the work.
  • “An ombudsman is a regulator.” An ombudsman settles individual disputes; a regulator writes and enforces rules.

Varies by country

Dispute services differ. The United Kingdom’s Financial Ombudsman Service handles complaints that firms have not resolved (Financial Ombudsman Service). Australia has the Australian Financial Complaints Authority (AFCA). In the United States, the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) accepts consumer complaints about financial products. In some countries, the central bank or a consumer protection agency handles financial complaints directly.

Why people disagree

How to organise regulators is debated. Supporters of a single integrated regulator argue that one body sees the whole system and avoids gaps between agencies. Supporters of separate prudential and conduct regulators (“twin peaks”) argue that keeping a bank safe and treating customers fairly can pull in different directions, and that separate bodies with clear mandates can each focus on one objective. Others favour many specialised agencies. Countries have chosen different models and some have changed model over time.
