Rent vs Buy: The Real Comparison

Renting is not "throwing money away" and buying is not automatically the cheaper choice: the costs on each side, and what the comparison depends on.

Intermediate·7 min (Simple) · 12 min (Detailed)·Updated 6 November 2026

Read first: Mortgages Explained

Key points

  • Owning costs more than the price: mortgage interest, property taxes, maintenance, insurance and the costs of buying and selling.
  • Renting costs the rent, and gives up the chance to build up equity in a home, but leaves the deposit free to be saved or invested elsewhere.
  • The answer depends on how long you stay, the local ratio of prices to rents, and what the deposit could earn elsewhere.
  • Buying and selling costs are paid once per move, so a short stay spreads them over few years.
  • Stability, control over the home and the freedom to move are real factors that a money comparison does not capture.

Simple: plain words. Detailed: exact figures, codes and methods.

In short: a fair comparison sets the costs of owning that you do not get back against the rent, and asks how long you will stay.

Simple version

Two columns of costs

People often compare a monthly rent with a monthly mortgage payment. That comparison misses a lot. Part of a mortgage payment repays the loan, which you keep as equity. And owning brings costs that a renter does not pay.

Mortgage interest Rent
Property taxes and building fees Renter’s insurance for belongings
Maintenance and repairs Moving costs when leases end
Building insurance
Buying and selling costs (taxes, legal fees, agent fees)
What the deposit could have earned elsewhere

On the owning side, the repayment of the loan is not a cost: it turns into equityEquity (home)The value of a property minus the mortgage still owed on it.Read more about Equity (home), the share of the home you own outright. A rise or fall in the home’s price changes that equity too.

A worked example

Example

A home costs ¤300,000 (example). The buyer pays a ¤60,000 deposit and borrows ¤240,000 at 4% a year.

Yearly costs of owning that are not recovered, first year (example):

Item Yearly amount
Mortgage interest (¤240,000 × 4%) about ¤9,600
Property tax and fees (0.5% of price) ¤1,500
Maintenance (1% of price) ¤3,000
Insurance ¤500
Lost earnings on the deposit (¤60,000 × 4%) ¤2,400
Total about ¤17,000, or about ¤1,417 a month

A similar home rents for ¤1,250 a month, or ¤15,000 a year.

In this example, owning costs about ¤2,000 a year more before any change in the home’s price. If prices rise by more than that each year, owning comes out ahead; if they rise less, stay flat or fall, renting does. The numbers change with every input.

How long will you stay?

Buying and selling cost money: taxes, legal fees and agent fees. Suppose these add up to ¤18,000 over one purchase and one sale (example). Spread over 3 years, that is ¤6,000 a year. Spread over 10 years, it is ¤1,800 a year. This is why the length of stay is often the key question in the comparison.

Price-to-rent

The price-to-rent ratioPrice-to-rent ratioHouse prices divided by yearly rents, used to compare the cost of buying with the cost of renting.Read more about Price-to-rent ratio divides the price of a home by a year’s rent for a similar home. In the example, ¤300,000 ÷ ¤15,000 = 20. A higher ratio means homes are expensive compared with renting them; a lower ratio means the opposite. Ratios differ a lot between cities and countries, and over time.

Things money does not measure

Owning gives control: you can decorate, renovate and stay as long as you can pay. Renting gives flexibility: moving for a job is quicker and cheaper. How much each matters is personal. Detailed version

The user cost of housing

Economists compare owning and renting with the user cost: the yearly cost of owning one ¤ of housing, excluding the repayment of principal. A common form, following Poterba (1984) and used by Himmelberg, Mayer and Sinai (Journal of Economic Perspectives, 2005):

user cost = P × ( r + ω − τ(r + ω) + δ + γ − g )

P = price          r = interest / opportunity cost rate
ω = property tax   τ = income tax rate on deductible items (0 where not deductible)
δ = maintenance and depreciation
γ = risk premium   g = expected price growth

Owning is cheaper than renting when user cost < annual rent. Equivalently, the “fair” price-to-rent ratio is 1 ÷ (user cost rate). With r = 4%, ω = 0.5%, δ = 1%, τ = 0, γ = 0.5% and g = 2%, the user cost rate is 4.0%, so a price-to-rent ratio of 25 would make owning and renting cost about the same. A ratio above 25 does not by itself show prices are too high; it can reflect low rates or high expected growth. That is the main point of the 2005 paper: price-to-rent ratios are interpreted alongside interest rates.

Leverage

A mortgage magnifies changes in the price on the owner’s equity:

equity return ≈ (price change − interest and costs on the loan) / deposit

Example, before costs: a ¤300,000 home with a ¤60,000 deposit. A 10% rise (¤30,000) is a 50% gain on the deposit; a 10% fall is a 50% loss. Losses beyond the deposit create negative equityNegative equityOwing more on a loan than the asset it is secured on is worth.Read more about Negative equity, which limits the ability to sell or refinance.

Opportunity cost of the deposit

The renter’s deposit-equivalent can be invested. What it earns, and its riskRiskThe range of possible outcomes, including the chance of losing money.Read more about Risk, belong in the comparison. Using a high expected return for the renter’s investments and a high expected price growth for the owner makes both sides look favourable at once; consistent assumptions matter more than precise ones.

Data

Price-to-rent and price-to-income ratios are compiled by the OECD for its members. The BIS publishes residential property price series for many economies (BIS), and Eurostat publishes a house price index for the EU (Eurostat). Ratios built from indices show changes over time, not levels; comparisons of levels across countries need care.

Sensitivity

The answer moves most with: length of stay (through transaction costs), expected price growth, the interest rate and the return on the alternative investment. A useful test is to find the break-even price growth at which owning and renting cost the same, and to judge whether that growth is plausible.

Common misconceptions

  • “Rent is dead money; a mortgage is an investment.” Interest, taxes and maintenance are also paid and not recovered.
  • “A mortgage payment equal to rent means owning is cheaper.” Owner costs and transaction costs are missing from that comparison.
  • “Price growth is certain over a long period.” Price series include long periods of falling or flat real prices in several countries (BIS).

Varies by country

Transaction costs and taxes differ. England charges Stamp Duty Land Tax on purchases above a threshold, with different rules for first-time buyers (GOV.UK). In Germany, a property transfer tax is set by each federal state. In some countries, such as the United States, mortgage interest can be deducted from taxable income under conditions; in many others it cannot. The share of households who rent also differs widely between countries.
