Why Taxes Exist and Where They Go

Taxes pay for public goods and services, move income between people and change behaviour; how much is collected, and how, varies widely by country.

Everyday·5 min (Simple) · 9 min (Detailed)·Updated 6 November 2026

Read first: Who Does What in the Financial System

Key points

  • Governments pay for things that markets provide too little of on their own, such as defence, roads and courts, and in many countries a large part of health care and education.
  • Taxes have three jobs: they raise money, they redistribute income, and they make some activities more or less attractive.
  • Total tax as a share of the economy (the tax-to-GDP ratio) differs widely between countries.
  • A budget deficit is the gap when spending is higher than revenue; the government borrows to fill it.
  • The person or firm that hands the tax to the government is not necessarily the one who ends up paying it. Economists call this question tax incidence.

Simple: plain words. Detailed: exact figures, codes and methods.

In short: taxes are how a society pays for the things it decides to provide together. They also move money between people and change what things cost.

Simple version

What would be missing without taxes?

Some things are hard to sell one person at a time. A street light shines on everyone who walks past. A court protects contracts for everyone, not only for the people in a case. Economists call these public goodsPublic goodSomething that one person's use does not reduce for others and that no one can be excluded from, such as street lighting.Read more about Public good: once they exist, it is hard to stop anyone from using them, so few people would pay for them voluntarily.

Governments also pay for many services that could be sold privately but that a country has decided everyone should get. Schools, hospitals and pensions are common examples. Which services are paid for by taxes, and how much, is a political choice and varies by country.

The three jobs of taxes

  1. Raise money. This is the main job. Taxes pay for roads, police, schools, health care, pensions and the rest of public spending.
  2. Redistribute. Taxes and benefits move money from some people to others: from people in work to people who are retired, ill or caring for children, and in many systems from higher incomes to lower incomes.
  3. Change behaviour. A tax on something makes it more expensive, so people tend to buy less of it. Taxes on tobacco, alcohol or fuel work partly this way. A tax break does the opposite.

Where a tax ¤ goes

Every country divides its spending differently. Here is an illustrative split of ¤100 of public spending (example, not any real country):

Area Share of ¤100 (example)
Pensions, unemployment and family support ¤35
Health ¤15
Education ¤10
Interest on government debt ¤5
Defence, police and courts ¤8
Transport, energy and other economic services ¤12
Administration and everything else ¤15

In many richer countries, support for older people and health care are the largest items. The Professional layer gives an official breakdown.

When spending is more than taxes

If a government spends more in a year than it collects, the gap is called a budget deficitBudget deficitThe amount by which a government's spending exceeds its revenue in a year.Read more about Budget deficit. It fills the gap by borrowing, usually by selling bonds. The borrowing adds to public debt, and the interest on that debt becomes part of future spending.

Example

A government collects ¤470 billion in taxes and spends ¤500 billion in a year (example). The deficit is 500 − 470 = ¤30 billion. If the whole economy produces ¤1,000 billion a year, the deficit is 30 ÷ 1,000 = 3% of the economy.

Who really pays?

The person who hands a tax to the government is not necessarily the person who ends up paying it. A shop collects sales tax, but the customer pays it in the price. A tax on a business might be paid partly by its customers (higher prices), its workers (lower pay) or its owners (lower profits). Economists call this question tax incidence. The answer depends on who can most easily change what they do.

Detailed version

Market failure and the role of taxation

Public finance textbooks give three reasons for government activity: providing public goods (non-rival, non-excludable), correcting externalitiesExternalityA cost or benefit from an activity that falls on people who are not part of the transaction, such as pollution or vaccination.Read more about Externality (costs or benefits that fall on people outside a transaction), and redistributing income. Taxes finance the first, can address the second directly, and are one of the two main tools of the third, together with transfers.

Measuring the tax burden

The usual summary measure is the tax-to-GDP ratio: total taxes and compulsory social contributions as a share of gross domestic product. Eurostat publishes it for EU countries split into indirect taxes, direct taxes and net social contributions, and the ratio differs widely between member states (Eurostat). The OECD’s Revenue Statistics use a related classification (taxes on income and profits, social security contributions, payroll taxes, property taxes, taxes on goods and services, other).

On the spending side, statistics offices classify outlays by function using COFOG, the Classification of the Functions of Government. In EU data, social protection and health together account for more than half of general government expenditure (Eurostat).

Progressive, proportional, regressive

Defined by how the average tax rate (tax ÷ income) changes as income rises:

  • ProgressiveProgressive taxA tax whose rate rises as income rises.Read more about Progressive tax: the average rate rises with income.
  • Proportional: the average rate stays the same.
  • RegressiveRegressive taxA tax that takes a larger share of income from lower earners than from higher earners.Read more about Regressive tax: the average rate falls as income rises.

The label depends on the income measure. A tax on spending can look regressive against one year’s income and less so against lifetime income, because people spend from savings in some years and save in others.

Incidence and elasticity

For a small per-unit tax in a competitive market, the share of the tax paid by buyers is approximately:

buyers' share  ≈ Es / (Es + |Ed|)
sellers' share ≈ |Ed| / (Es + |Ed|)

Es = price elasticity of supply, Ed = price elasticity of demand

Example: a tax of ¤1 per unit, with Es = 1.0 and Ed = −0.5. Buyers pay about 1.0 ÷ 1.5 = ¤0.67 through a higher price; sellers receive about ¤0.33 less. The side that is less able to change its behaviour (the less elastic side) pays more of the tax.

Taxes also create a deadweight loss: trades that would have happened without the tax do not happen. For a linear demand and supply, the loss is about ½ × tax per unit × fall in quantity. The loss grows roughly with the square of the tax rate, which is the core of the efficiency argument for broad bases and lower rates. Equity arguments weigh this against who pays.

Tax expenditures

A deduction, exemption or reduced rate lowers revenue in the same way as a spending programme of the same size. Many finance ministries publish a tax expenditure report that estimates the revenue each one costs. These estimates are approximate, because removing a relief would change behaviour.

Common misconceptions

  • “The firm that pays the tax pays the tax.” Legal incidence and economic incidence differ.
  • “Deficits are paid for by printing money.” Governments fill deficits by issuing debt; how central banks interact with that debt is a separate question (see the Macroeconomics module).
  • “A higher tax-to-GDP ratio means people pay more for the same services.” Countries differ in what is provided publicly and what households buy privately.

Why people disagree

Who ultimately pays a tax on company profits is an open question. In the classic model of a closed economy, the burden falls mainly on the owners of capital, so a corporate tax is treated as a tax on shareholders and other investors. Other economists point out that capital can move between countries while workers move less, so part of the tax can be passed on to workers as lower wages. A study of local business taxes in Germany found that a substantial share was paid by workers through lower wages (Fuest, Peichl and Siegloch, American Economic Review, 2018). Estimates differ by country, time period and method, and official distribution tables in different countries use different assumptions.

Varies by country

Countries split public spending between taxes and private payment in different ways. In some, health care is paid for mainly from general taxation; in others, mainly through compulsory social insurance contributions; in others, a large part is paid privately or through employers. The tax-to-GDP ratio differs widely even among EU members (Eurostat).
