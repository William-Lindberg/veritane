Government Benefits and Social Insurance

How public support systems are built: contributions, means tests and entitlements, and how benefits are withdrawn as income rises.

Intermediate·6 min (Simple) · 11 min (Detailed)·Updated 6 November 2026

Read first: Income Tax: Brackets, Marginal and Average Rates

Key points

  • Social insurance (for unemployment, sickness, old age and health) is usually paid for by contributions and pays out when a defined event happens.
  • Means-tested benefits depend on income or assets and are reduced as these rise.
  • The rate at which a benefit is withdrawn, added to income tax, gives an effective marginal tax rate that can be high at low incomes.
  • Universal benefits, such as child benefit in some countries, are paid without a means test.
  • Many people entitled to a benefit do not claim it, because of complexity or stigma; eligibility rules change over time and vary by country.

Simple: plain words. Detailed: exact figures, codes and methods.

In short: public support comes in three main designs: insurance you pay into, help that depends on what you have, and payments everyone in a group receives. How each is withdrawn as income rises shapes what extra work is worth.

Social insurance works like the insurance in the Insurance module, but it is compulsory and run by the state. You pay in while working. If a defined event happens, such as losing your job or reaching pension age, you receive payments. The amount often depends on how much and how long you paid in.

A means testMeans testA check of income or assets that decides whether someone qualifies for a benefit, and how much.Read more about Means test checks income, and sometimes savings, to decide who qualifies and how much they get.

Why earning ¤100 more can leave ¤30

Means-tested benefits are reduced as income rises. This reduction is called a taper or withdrawal. It adds to income tax.

Example

A parent on a low income earns ¤100 more one month (example).

  • Income tax at 20%: −¤20
  • Social contributions at 10%: −¤10
  • Benefit reduced by ¤40 for each extra ¤100 earned: −¤40

The parent keeps 100 − 20 − 10 − 40 = ¤30. In effect, 70% of the extra ¤100 is lost.

This does not mean extra work leaves people with less. In this example the parent still has ¤30 more in cash terms. But the gain is smaller than the pay rise, and costs such as travel or childcare can take part of it. A budget that lists all income, including benefits, shows the full picture; the Budget PlannerSplit a monthly take-home income into needs, wants and savings with a rule such as 50/30/20, and see how actual spending lines up with it. can hold those lines.

Cliff edges

Some benefits do not taper. They stop completely when income passes a limit. Earning ¤1 above the limit can then mean losing the whole benefit. These are called cliff edges.

Checking what applies to you

Rules change and differ between countries, and sometimes between regions. Official calculators and finders run by governments are free. Some examples of public services:

  • United Kingdom: benefits calculators listed on GOV.UK.
  • United States: the benefits pages on USAGov, including a benefit finder.
  • Canada: the Government of Canada benefits finder.

Why many people do not claim

Not everyone who could get a benefit claims it. Common reasons include not knowing about it, forms that are hard to complete, and feeling uncomfortable about asking. Benefits are a part of the system people paid for through taxes and contributions; claiming one is using the system as designed.

Detailed version

Contributory and non-contributory design

The ILO distinguishes social insurance, financed by contributions and linked to past employment, from non-contributory schemes financed through general taxation, including social assistance and universal benefits (ILO). Contributory schemes give entitlements that are often earnings-related and not means-tested; non-contributory schemes reach people without a history of contributions. Many systems combine them, with means-tested assistance as a floor below insurance.

Tapers, cliffs and participation tax rates

Two measures summarise work incentives:

EMTR (effective marginal tax rate)
  = (Δ tax + Δ contributions + Δ benefits withdrawn) / Δ gross earnings

PTR (participation tax rate)
  = 1 − (net income in work − net income out of work) / gross earnings in work

Example: out of work, a household receives ¤8,000 a year in benefits. In work, gross earnings are ¤20,000 and net income after tax, contributions and reduced benefits is ¤14,000. PTR = 1 − (14,000 − 8,000) ÷ 20,000 = 70%.

A taper at rate w combined with income tax t and contributions c gives an EMTR of roughly t + c + w when the taper is applied to gross income, and less when it is applied to income after tax. In the United Kingdom, Universal Credit is reduced by a set amount for every £1 earned above any work allowance (GOV.UK). A cliff edge gives an EMTR above 100% over a narrow range.

Design choices trade off three goals that cannot all be met at once: a high income floor, low withdrawal rates and low cost. A lower taper keeps more support for people in work but extends benefits further up the income scale.

Automatic stabilisers

Unemployment insurance and means-tested benefits rise when the economy slows and fall when it recovers, without new decisions. This makes them automatic stabilisersAutomatic stabiliserA tax or benefit that cushions the economy without any new decision, such as unemployment benefit payments rising in a downturn.Read more about Automatic stabiliser, discussed in the Macroeconomics module’s lesson on fiscal policy.

Data

Eurostat publishes social protection expenditure for EU countries by function, such as old age, sickness and health care, family and unemployment (Eurostat); the OECD compiles comparable data for its members. The ILO reports on coverage worldwide and highlights gaps for workers in informal jobs, who are often outside contributory schemes (ILO). The World Bank works on social assistance and cash-transfer programmes in low- and middle-income countries (World Bank).

Common misconceptions

  • “Benefits are only for people out of work.” Many systems pay in-work benefits, family benefits and pensions.
  • “A 70% EMTR means work does not pay.” It means 30% of extra earnings is kept; the PTR measures the gain from working at all.
  • “Universal benefits go to people who do not need them.” Some universal benefits are recovered through the tax system from higher earners.

Why people disagree

Whether support should be targeted or universal, and whether it should carry conditions such as job search, is debated. Supporters of targeting argue that means tests direct limited budgets to the people with the least, at lower cost. Supporters of universal payments argue that they avoid high withdrawal rates and cliff edges, are simpler, and reach everyone entitled, including people who would not claim a means-tested benefit. Hoynes and Rothstein review this trade-off for universal basic income and existing programmes in advanced economies, noting that a universal payment at a meaningful level has a high gross cost (NBER, 2019). Evidence on conditionality also differs by programme and outcome, so the question involves both evidence and values.

Varies by country

Systems differ in their mix. The United Kingdom pays Child Benefit widely, but recovers it through a tax charge from people with income above a set level (GOV.UK). Canada pays a monthly child benefit that is calculated from family income, and lists federal and provincial programmes in an official benefits finder (Government of Canada). In many low- and middle-income countries, cash-transfer programmes reach households in informal work who are outside contributory insurance (World Bank).
