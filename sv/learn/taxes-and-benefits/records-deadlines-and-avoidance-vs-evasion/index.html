Records, Deadlines, and the Line Between Avoidance and Evasion

The habits that make tax simpler, how penalties for late filing and late payment work, and where the legal line between avoidance and evasion lies.

Intermediate·5 min (Simple) · 8 min (Detailed)·Updated 6 November 2026

Read first: Income Tax: Brackets, Marginal and Average Rates

Key points

  • Records of income, deductible costs and investment purchases support a tax return; how long they need to be kept varies by country.
  • Missed deadlines bring penalties and interest; in some systems the penalty for filing late is larger than the penalty for paying late.
  • Avoidance uses the rules as intended; aggressive avoidance uses gaps in the rules in ways lawmakers did not intend; evasion hides income or invents costs and is a crime.
  • Schemes that promise large tax savings with little effort are a common warning sign of fraud or of arrangements the tax authority challenges.
  • Tax authorities run official help channels; some paid services charge for claims that can be made directly at no cost.

Simple: plain words. Detailed: exact figures, codes and methods.

In short: much of the work of tax is paperwork and dates. Knowing what to keep, when things are due and where the legal lines are makes the rest simpler.

Simple version

What records are for

A tax return is a set of numbers. Records are the proof behind them. If the tax authority asks a question, records show where each number came from. They also help you claim everything you are entitled to.

Records commonly kept include:

  • Income: payslips, end-of-year pay statements, invoices if you work for yourself, and statements of interest and dividends.
  • Costs you claim: receipts and invoices for anything deducted, such as work expenses or business costs.
  • Investments: what you bought, when, and for how much, including fees. This is needed to work out a gain when you sell, which can be many years later.
  • Letters from the tax authority, and copies of returns already filed.

How long to hold on to records varies by country, and can be longer for self-employed people or when an asset is still owned. A folder per tax year, paper or digital, is a common way to organise them.

Deadlines, penalties and interest

Most systems have a date to file a return and a date to pay any tax due. Missing either usually costs money:

  • a penalty, often a fixed amount for filing late, growing the longer it is late,
  • interest on tax paid late, charged for each day or month.

In some countries the penalty for filing late is larger than the penalty for paying late. A return filed on time, even when the tax cannot all be paid yet, can then cost less than a late return. Many tax authorities offer payment plans for people who cannot pay in full.

Free help

Tax authorities run official help: websites, phone lines and online accounts. Some private firms offer to claim a refund for you in return for a fee or a share of the refund. The same claim can often be made directly with the tax authority at no charge.

Detailed version

Retention periods

Retention rules are tied to how long the authority can reopen a return. In the United States, records supporting an item on a return are kept until the period of limitations for that return runs out; longer periods apply in some situations, such as substantially under-reported income (IRS). In the United Kingdom, required periods differ for employees and for the self-employed (GOV.UK). Records of an asset’s cost basis are needed until the period for the year of disposal has closed, which can be decades after purchase.

How penalties are structured

Penalty regimes usually combine three elements:

  1. Late-filing penalties. Fixed or time-based. The United States charges a monthly percentage of unpaid tax for late filing that is larger than the monthly percentage for late payment (IRS: failure to file; IRS: failure to pay). The United Kingdom charges a fixed penalty for a late Self Assessment return, then daily and further penalties as the delay grows (GOV.UK).
  2. Interest on late payment. Compensation for the time value of money, usually not a punishment in itself.
  3. Inaccuracy penalties. Often scaled by behaviour: lower for careless errors, higher for deliberate ones, higher still for deliberate errors that are concealed, with reductions for disclosure.

Avoidance: how the line is drawn

HMRC describes tax avoidance as bending the rules of the tax system to gain a tax advantage that Parliament did not intend, often through contrived, artificial transactions, and publishes the schemes it is challenging (HMRC; HMRC). Countries use three types of tool:

  • Specific anti-avoidance rules that close a named arrangement.
  • General anti-avoidance rules (GAARs): a broad test that lets the authority counteract arrangements whose main purpose is a tax advantage contrary to the purpose of the law. Many countries have one; the EU requires member states to apply a GAAR in corporate tax.
  • Disclosure rules requiring promoters or users of certain schemes to report them early.

Information exchange

Tax authorities increasingly receive data directly from third parties: employers, banks, platforms and foreign authorities. Under the US FATCA regime, foreign financial institutions report accounts held by US taxpayers (IRS); the OECD’s Common Reporting Standard applies a similar model among participating jurisdictions. The practical effect is that income held abroad is often already known to the home authority.

What draws an enquiry

Authorities do not publish exact selection rules. In general terms, enquiries follow from mismatches between a return and third-party data, figures that differ from those of similar taxpayers, large or unusual claims, and random checks used to measure compliance.

Impersonation fraud

The IRS lists common tax scams, including fake bills and fake refund offers (IRS). HMRC publishes how to report suspicious messages and calls that use its name (GOV.UK), and explains how to claim a refund directly (GOV.UK).

Varies by country

Filing dates, retention periods and penalty amounts are set nationally. In the United States, records are tied to the period of limitations for each return (IRS). In the United Kingdom, Self Assessment penalties start with a fixed amount and rise with the length of the delay (GOV.UK). In countries where most employees have tax taken from pay and receive pre-filled returns, record-keeping matters most for the self-employed and for investors.
