Premiums, Deductibles and Excess

How the amount you pay yourself before a claim pays out changes the premium, and how the trade-off can be worked out.

Everyday·5 min (Simple) · 9 min (Professional)·Updated 6 November 2026

Key points

  • A higher deductible (excess) lowers the premium, because the policyholder pays more of each claim.
  • Whether the lower premium is worth it depends on how likely and how large claims are, and on whether the deductible could be paid from savings.
  • No-claims discounts change the arithmetic of making a small claim.
  • Co-payments and annual caps are related ways of sharing costs between insurer and policyholder.
  • Comparing policies means comparing cover and conditions as well as price.
Depth:

In short: the deductibleDeductible (excess)The part of an insured loss that the policyholder pays before the insurer pays the rest.Read more about Deductible (excess), also called the excess, is the part of each claim you pay yourself. A larger one makes the premium cheaper, and the trade-off can be worked out with simple arithmetic.

Simple version

What the words mean

  • Premium: the price of the policy, paid monthly or yearly.
  • Deductible or excess: the amount you pay towards each claim before the insurer pays the rest. “Deductible” is common in North America; “excess” in the UK, Ireland, Australia and elsewhere.
  • Claim: a request to the insurer to pay for a loss.

If the excess is ¤250 and a covered loss costs ¤1,000, the insurer pays ¤750.

Why a higher excess lowers the premium

With a higher excess, small losses are no longer worth claiming, and the insurer pays less on every larger claim. It also handles fewer claims, which saves costs. Part of that saving comes back as a lower premium.

Working out the trade-off

Example

Two quotes for the same cover (examples):

Policy A Policy B
Yearly premium ¤500 ¤420
Excess per claim ¤100 ¤500
  • Policy B saves ¤500 − ¤420 = ¤80 a year.
  • If a large claim happens, B costs ¤500 − ¤100 = ¤400 more out of pocket.
  • Suppose the chance of a claim in a year is 15 %. The average extra cost of B is 15 % × ¤400 = ¤60 a year.

On these numbers B has the lower expected cost, by ¤80 − ¤60 = ¤20 a year. The two break even when the chance of a claim is ¤80 ÷ ¤400 = 20 % a year. Above that, A has the lower expected cost. This is an illustration, not a recommendation.

The average is not the whole story. With Policy B, a household must be able to pay ¤500 at short notice. If that would mean borrowing, the lower premium comes with a cost that the average does not show.

Should a small loss be claimed? The no-claims discount

Many motor and some home policies give a no-claims discount: the premium falls for each year without a claim. A claim can reduce or remove the discount.

Example

A ¤300 loss with a ¤100 excess would pay ¤200 (example). If the claim raises the premium by ¤60 a year for the next three years, that adds 3 × ¤60 = ¤180. The claim is worth ¤200 − ¤180 = ¤20 overall, before the time spent on it.

Some insurers let customers protect the discount for an extra fee. The rules differ by insurer and by country.

Co-payments and caps

Health and some other policies add other ways of sharing costs:

  • Co-payment (co-insurance): you pay a percentage of each bill, for example 20 %.
  • Annual cap: the most you pay in a year; after that the insurer pays everything covered.
  • Benefit limit: the most the insurer pays, per claim or per year.

Price is one line of the comparison

Two policies with the same price can pay out very differently. A comparison usually covers the excess, the limits, the exclusions, the basis of settlement (new for old or after wear) and any extra conditions.

Varies by country

  • Words: “deductible” in the United States and Canada; “excess” in the United Kingdom, Ireland, Australia and New Zealand.
  • Renewal pricing: in the United Kingdom, rules since 2022 stop home and motor insurers charging renewing customers more than an equivalent new customer.
  • Health deductibles: in some countries basic health insurance has a legally set minimum deductible; in others, public systems use fixed fees per visit instead.

Professional version

Deductible optimisation

With a deductible D, the insurer pays max(0, X − D) for a loss X. For a choice between deductibles D₁ < D₂:

premium saving        ΔP = P(D₁) − P(D₂)
extra expected cost   ΔE = E[min(X, D₂) − min(X, D₁)] × expected number of losses
choose D₂ on average  if ΔP > ΔE

If every loss exceeds D₂, ΔE = frequency × (D₂ − D₁), which gives the break-even frequency ΔP ÷ (D₂ − D₁), 20 % in the example. Losses between D₁ and D₂ add a smaller amount each. A risk-averse buyer with limited liquid savings may still prefer the lower deductible at a small expected cost; the expected-value comparison is the starting point, not the full answer.

Claims-frequency distributions

Insurers model the number of claims (often Poisson or negative binomial) and claim size (often right-skewed, such as lognormal or Pareto) separately. Most claims are small and a few are very large, so a deductible removes many claims but a smaller share of total claim cost.

Experience rating

Experience rating adjusts premiums to a policyholder’s own claims history. No-claims discount scales are one form; in commercial insurance, premiums may be adjusted directly by past losses. A consequence is that small claims carry a hidden cost in future premiums, which encourages policyholders to absorb them (the “hunger for bonus” effect described in actuarial literature).

Price optimisation and regulatory responses

Pricing can depend on factors beyond expected claims, including how likely a customer is to switch. In the United Kingdom, the Financial Conduct Authority introduced rules requiring that renewal prices for home and motor insurance be no higher than the equivalent new-business price (FCA PS21/5). The FCA also publishes value measures data, such as claims frequencies and claims acceptance rates, for many general insurance products (FCA). Other regulators use filing and approval of rates; in the United States, rate regulation is set by each state (NAIC).

Common misconceptions

  • “The cheapest premium is the cheapest policy.” Total cost includes the expected deductible payments and any uncovered losses.
  • “Any loss above the excess is worth claiming.” Future premium effects can reduce or reverse the gain.
  • “A higher excess applies only to small claims.” It applies to every claim, including large ones.
