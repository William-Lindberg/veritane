Nudges, Defaults and Choice Design

How the design of choices steers outcomes, for people and against them, and the ethics of doing it.

Everyday·5 min (Simple) · 8 min (Detailed)·Updated 6 November 2026

Read first: Present Bias and Self-control

Key points

  • Defaults are powerful because doing nothing is easy.
  • Automatic enrolment and default funds raise participation in retirement saving.
  • The same techniques, used against a person's interests, are called dark patterns.
  • Transparency and easy reversal separate helpful nudges from manipulation.
  • People can design their own defaults.

Simple: plain words. Detailed: exact figures, codes and methods.

In short: every choice is presented in some way, and the presentation shapes the result; the same design tools can help people reach their own goals or steer them away from them.

Simple version

Every choice has a design

There is no neutral way to present a choice. Something has to be listed first. Something happens if you do nothing. Some option takes one click and another takes five. Whoever designs the form, the website or the contract makes these decisions. Economists call this choice architecture.

A nudgeNudgeA change to how choices are presented that steers behaviour without removing any options.Read more about Nudge is a design feature that changes what people tend to choose without banning any option or changing prices much.

Why defaults are powerful

A defaultDefault (choice)What happens if a person does nothing, which has a large influence on what people end up with.Read more about Default (choice) is what happens if you make no active choice. Defaults matter because doing nothing is easy. Changing a default takes time, attention and a decision, and people put off decisions (see the lesson on present bias).

Example

A company offers a retirement savings plan (example). Under design A, workers must fill in a form to join. Under design B, workers are enrolled automatically and can leave by filling in a form. The plan, the money and the freedom to choose are the same. In real cases like this, far more people end up saving under design B (Madrian and Shea, 2001).

Helpful and harmful nudges, side by side

Helpful to the person’s own goals Harmful to them (“dark patterns”)
Automatic enrolment in a pension, easy to leave A subscription that is easy to start and hard to cancel
A reminder before a payment is due A countdown timer that is not real (“offer ends in 5:00”)
Showing the total cost of a loan in ¤ Showing only the low monthly payment
A pre-ticked box for paper-free statements A pre-ticked box adding insurance to a purchase
Clear “no thanks” button “No, I don’t want to save money” as the only way to decline

A dark patternDark patternA design choice in an app or website that steers or pressures users into decisions they would not otherwise make.Read more about Dark pattern is a design that leads people into choices they would not make if the options were presented plainly.

What separates help from manipulation

Two tests are widely used:

  1. Transparency. Would the design still work if it were explained openly? A pension default still works when people are told about it. A fake countdown does not.
  2. Easy reversal. Can the person undo the choice as easily as it was made? Leaving a pension scheme with one form is easy reversal. Cancelling a subscription by phone during office hours only is not.

Designing your own defaults

People can use the same tools on themselves. Five personal defaults some people set up, described here as examples rather than recommendations:

  1. a scheduled transfer to savings on pay day;
  2. bills paid by automatic payment, so a missed date does not happen by default;
  3. a removed saved card on shopping sites, adding a pause;
  4. calendar reminders before free trials end;
  5. a yearly date to review insurance, subscriptions and accounts.

Varies by country

Rules on dark patterns differ. The EU’s Digital Services Act bans online platforms from designing interfaces that deceive or manipulate users (EUR-Lex). In the United States, the Federal Trade Commission has published a staff report on dark patterns and has acted against some under consumer-protection law. The OECD has surveyed approaches across its member countries (OECD, 2022).

Detailed version

Evidence on default effects

Madrian and Shea found that automatic enrolment raised plan participation among new employees substantially, and that a large share of enrolled employees kept the default contribution rate and default fund (Madrian and Shea, 2001). Choi, Laibson, Madrian and Metrick describe this as the “path of least resistance”: across several firms, outcomes followed whichever option required no action, for participation, contribution rates and asset allocation (Choi et al., 2002). A consequence is that a default contribution rate set low can anchor savers below what they would otherwise have chosen.

How large are nudge effects?

The answer depends on the evidence base.

  • A meta-analysis of over 200 studies estimated a small-to-medium average effect (Cohen’s d ≈ 0.43), larger for defaults and other “decision structure” changes than for information provision (Mertens et al., 2021).
  • A re-analysis adjusting for publication bias found no evidence of an average effect across domains once the bias was corrected, while noting that some kinds of nudge could still be effective (Maier et al., 2022).
  • DellaVigna and Linos analysed every trial run by two US nudge units. The average effect was 1.4 percentage points (about an 8% relative increase), compared with 8.7 percentage points in published academic studies; publication selection explains much of the gap (DellaVigna and Linos, 2022).

A balanced summary: defaults in retirement saving show large, well-replicated effects; many other nudges have smaller effects than early studies suggested, though even small effects can be cost-effective at scale.

The libertarian paternalism debate

Why people disagree

Thaler and Sunstein argue that because some choice architecture is unavoidable, designers can choose defaults that help people by their own standards while preserving freedom to opt out, which they call libertarian paternalism (Thaler and Sunstein, 2003). Critics raise several objections: Glaeser argues that the officials designing nudges are subject to the same biases and to political pressures, and that “soft” paternalism can be less transparent than explicit rules (Glaeser, 2006); others question who decides what counts as a person’s “own” interest. Supporters reply that leaving design to sellers is not neutral either. The debate is about who should design choices and under what accountability, more than about whether design matters.

Dark-pattern regulation

The OECD groups dark commercial patterns into categories such as forced action, interface interference, nagging, obstruction, sneaking, social proof and urgency, and reviews evidence that they affect consumer decisions (OECD, 2022). The FTC staff report documents similar practices, including hidden fees revealed late (“drip pricing”) and cancellation obstacles (FTC staff report “Bringing Dark Patterns to Light”, 2022). In the EU, the Digital Services Act addresses deceptive interface design by online platforms (EUR-Lex), alongside older consumer-protection rules on unfair commercial practices.

A designer’s checklist

Question Helpful design Concern
Who benefits from the default? The chooser, by their stated goals Mainly the designer
Is it disclosed? Explained openly Works only if hidden
Is reversal symmetric? Undo as easy as do Undo is harder
Is it tested? Effects measured and published Unknown or private
