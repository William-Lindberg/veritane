Anchoring and Framing

The first number you see and the way a choice is worded both move your answer.

Everyday·5 min (Simple) · 8 min (Detailed)·Updated 6 November 2026

Read first: Why Money Decisions Are Hard for Everyone

Key points

  • Anchors such as list prices, "was" prices, suggested tips and minimum payments pull estimates and choices towards them.
  • Framing ("90% fat-free" versus "10% fat", a price per day versus per year) changes choices without changing the facts.
  • Re-expressing a number in a different unit is a simple way to see past an anchor or a frame.
  • Sellers, lenders and sometimes governments use both.

Simple: plain words. Detailed: exact figures, codes and methods.

In short: the numbers and words around a choice change the choice, even when the facts underneath stay the same.

Simple version

What an anchor is

An anchorAnchoringThe tendency for the first number a person sees to pull their estimate or willingness to pay towards it.Read more about Anchoring is a number that pulls your estimate towards it. It works even when you know the number is random.

Three experiments

1. The wheel

Researchers spun a wheel that landed on 10 or 65, in front of the participants. Then they asked what percentage of countries in the United Nations were African. People who saw 10 gave a median estimate of 25%; people who saw 65 gave 45% (Tversky and Kahneman, 1974). The wheel had nothing to do with the question.

2. The last two digits

Students wrote down the last two digits of an identification number, then bid on items such as wine and computer accessories. Students with higher digits bid much more than those with lower digits (Ariely, Loewenstein and Prelec, 2003).

3. The same fact, two frames

“This yoghurt is 90% fat-free” and “this yoghurt is 10% fat” describe the same product. Many people rate the first as healthier. “¤1 a day” and “¤365 a year” are the same price; the first can sound like nothing, the second like a real decision (example).

What framing is

FramingFramingHow a choice is worded or presented, which can change decisions without changing the facts.Read more about Framing is the way a fact is presented: as a gain or a loss, per day or per year, as a percentage or as a count. Changing the frame changes how the fact feels, so it can change the choice.

Anchors on a receipt

  • “Was ¤80, now ¤50.” The ¤80 is the anchor. The useful question is whether ¤50 is a fair price today, not how far it is from ¤80.
  • Suggested tips of 18%, 20% and 25% on a payment screen set the range people choose from.
  • A large first option on a menu or price list makes the next one look moderate.

Anchors on a credit card statement

A statement shows a minimum payment. It is often a small share of the balance. For some people that number becomes the amount they pay, even when they could pay more.

Example

A card balance of ¤3,000 has an interest rate of 20% a year (example). The minimum is 3% of the balance including that month’s interest, or ¤25 if higher.

  • Paying only the minimum, the balance takes about 141 months (almost 12 years) to clear and costs about ¤3,003 in interest.
  • Paying a fixed ¤150 a month, it takes 25 months and costs about ¤680 in interest.

Changing the unit

A simple way to see past an anchor or a frame is to put the number in a different unit:

  • turn a per-day or per-month price into a per-year price;
  • turn a percentage into a count (“10% fat” is 10 g in every 100 g);
  • turn a “saving” into the amount actually paid.

Varies by country

Some countries regulate how minimum payments and prices are shown. In the United States, card statements must show how long repayment takes if only the minimum is paid. The United Kingdom has rules requiring card providers to contact customers who stay in long-term “persistent debt”. Many countries also have consumer rules about “was” prices and reference prices in sales. The details differ in each case.

Detailed version

Anchoring evidence and its limits

Anchoring is among the more consistently replicated findings in judgement research: in the Many Labs project, anchoring effects replicated across dozens of laboratories and were among the largest effects tested (Klein et al., 2014). Two mechanisms are proposed: insufficient adjustment from a starting value, and selective accessibility (the anchor makes anchor-consistent information easier to recall). Effects tend to be smaller when people have firm knowledge of the quantity, and larger for uncertain values.

Ariely, Loewenstein and Prelec report “coherent arbitrariness”: absolute valuations were swayed by an arbitrary anchor, but relative valuations across items stayed orderly (Ariely, Loewenstein and Prelec, 2003). Some later replications found smaller effects of arbitrary anchors on willingness to pay, so magnitudes in market settings are debated.

Mental accounting

Thaler’s mental accounting describes how people sort money into separate “accounts” by source or purpose, and evaluate transactions within each account rather than against total wealth (Thaler, 1985). Consequences include treating a tax refund differently from salary, holding savings at a low rate while carrying expensive debt, and judging a ¤20 saving as large on a ¤50 item and small on a ¤5,000 item. Framing determines which account a transaction lands in.

The minimum-payment anchor

Stewart found in an experiment that including a minimum payment on a hypothetical statement lowered the amounts people chose to repay compared with a statement without one (Stewart, 2009). Using US account data, Keys and Wang found that a substantial share of cardholders pay exactly or close to the minimum, and that some of this behaviour responds to changes in the minimum formula itself, consistent with anchoring (Keys and Wang, 2019).

Worked arithmetic for the example above, monthly rate i = 20% / 12 ≈ 1.667%: the first month’s interest is ¤3,000 × 0.01667 = ¤50.00, and the first minimum payment is 3% × ¤3,050 = ¤91.50, of which ¤41.50 reduces the balance. Because the minimum is a percentage of a shrinking balance, the payment shrinks too, which extends the payoff to about 141 months.

Disclosure design

Disclosure regimes increasingly use framing deliberately: showing total cost and payoff time alongside the minimum, expressing rates as annual percentages, and showing fees in money rather than percentages. Evidence suggests that the form of disclosure matters as much as its presence, and that some disclosures change behaviour less than intended because the anchor they present is still salient.

Why people disagree

Some researchers and regulators favour stronger rules on how prices, minimums and reference prices are presented. Others argue that additional disclosures add complexity, that people adapt to frames over time, and that measured effects in experiments overstate what happens with repeated real choices. Both positions cite the same experimental literature and draw different conclusions about policy.
