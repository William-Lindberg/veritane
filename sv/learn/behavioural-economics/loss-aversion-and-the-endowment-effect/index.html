Loss Aversion and the Endowment Effect

Losses tend to weigh about twice as much as gains of the same size, and that shapes selling, insuring and holding.

Everyday·5 min (Simple) · 9 min (Detailed)·Updated 6 November 2026

Read first: Why Money Decisions Are Hard for Everyone

Key points

  • Loss aversion helps explain holding investments that have fallen, selling ones that have risen, heavily insuring against small losses and turning down fair bets.
  • The endowment effect: owning something tends to raise the value its owner puts on it.
  • Free trials and generous returns policies work partly through these effects.
  • A change described as avoiding a loss tends to motivate more than the same change described as a gain.
  • Knowing the pattern can inform how a person sets up their own decisions.

Simple: plain words. Detailed: exact figures, codes and methods.

In short: for many people, the pain of losing ¤100 is larger than the pleasure of winning ¤100, and that difference shows up in everyday money choices.

Simple version

The coin-flip test

Imagine this offer. A fair coin is tossed. Heads, you win ¤150. Tails, you lose ¤100 (example). Would you accept?

The bet favours you. Over many tosses the expected gain is ¤25 per toss: half the time +¤150, half the time −¤100. Yet many people turn it down. When researchers ask what win would make the bet acceptable, people often name a figure around twice the possible loss (Kahneman and Tversky, 1979).

This is loss aversionLoss aversionFeeling losses more strongly than gains of the same size.Read more about Loss aversion: a loss tends to feel stronger than a gain of the same size, often by a factor of about two.

The mug experiment

In a well-known experiment, half the people in a room were given a coffee mug. The others were not. Then owners were asked the lowest price at which they would sell, and the others the highest price at which they would buy.

Owners asked roughly twice as much as buyers offered (Kahneman, Knetsch and Thaler, 1990). The mugs were identical and handed out at random. Owning one for a few minutes was enough to raise its value to the owner. This is the endowment effectEndowment effectValuing something more simply because one owns it.Read more about Endowment effect.

Where it shows up in money

  • Holding investments that have fallen. Selling makes a loss feel “real”, so people tend to wait to get back to the purchase price. The purchase price matters to the owner but not to the market.
  • Selling investments that have risen. Taking a gain feels good, so gains are often sold sooner.
  • Insuring small things. Extended warranties on cheap items protect against small, unlikely losses. The expected cost can be higher than the expected payout.
  • Saying no to fair or favourable bets. A new job, a move or a change of supplier can involve a small possible loss that feels larger than a bigger possible gain.
Expected ValueMultiply each outcome by its probability to see the average result of a choice: a general table, an extended warranty, an insurance deductible choice or a lottery ticket.

Free trials and returns

Once something is in your home or on your phone, giving it up feels like a loss. That is one reason free trials and easy returns are common: some people who intended to cancel or return end up keeping the item or subscription.

Framing as a loss

“You will lose ¤300 a year by not insulating your loft” tends to motivate more than “you will save ¤300 a year”, even though they describe the same money (example). Messages from sellers, charities and governments use this.

Using the pattern for yourself

Some ways people use this knowledge, as examples rather than instructions:

  • asking “would I buy this today at this price?” about something already owned;
  • deciding in advance at what point an investment would be sold, before a loss happens;
  • working out the expected cost of a warranty before the sales conversation.

Detailed version

Prospect theory’s value function

Prospect theory evaluates outcomes as gains and losses relative to a reference point, not as final wealth (Kahneman and Tversky, 1979). In the cumulative version, a common parameterisation is:

v(x) = x^α             for x ≥ 0
v(x) = −λ × (−x)^β     for x < 0

with median estimates α = β = 0.88 and λ = 2.25 in Tversky and Kahneman’s experiments (Tversky and Kahneman, 1992). The function is concave for gains, convex for losses and steeper for losses. Probabilities are also transformed by a weighting function that overweights small probabilities.

Worked example (value only, equal weights for simplicity): for the coin flip +¤150 / −¤100, v(150) ≈ 82.22 and v(−100) ≈ −2.25 × 57.54 ≈ −129.47. Weighted at 0.5 each, the total is ≈ −23.63, so the bet is rejected even though it has a +¤25 expected valueExpected valueThe average outcome of a decision if it were repeated many times: each result weighted by its probability.Read more about Expected value. The win that makes the bet neutral solves X^0.88 = 2.25 × 100^0.88, giving X ≈ ¤251.

The disposition effect

Odean analysed trading records at a US discount broker and found investors realised gains more readily than losses: the proportion of gains realised was about 1.5 times the proportion of losses realised, and the winners sold went on to outperform the losers held (Odean, 1998). Prospect theory combined with a purchase-price reference point is one explanation; others include a belief in mean reversion and tax considerations, which Odean examined.

Myopic loss aversion and the equity premium

Benartzi and Thaler proposed that loss-averse investors who evaluate their portfolios frequently experience more frequent losses and so demand a high premium to hold shares, which could help explain the size of the historical equity premium (Benartzi and Thaler, 1995). The implication is that the evaluation period, not only the holding period, affects behaviour.

Magnitudes and critiques

Why people disagree

Estimates of λ vary widely by method, context and population; some studies find values near 1 for small or routine stakes, and higher values for larger or unfamiliar ones. Gal and Rucker argue that many findings attributed to loss aversion can be explained by other mechanisms, such as status-quo preference or inertia, and that the evidence for a general principle that losses loom larger is weaker than often stated (Gal and Rucker, 2018). Other researchers respond that loss aversion remains well supported for risky choices with meaningful stakes. The debate is about scope and size more than existence.

For the endowment effect, results depend on the good, the wording of the task and the participants’ trading experience. Effects tend to be smaller for goods held for resale and for experienced traders.

Implications for choice design

Reference points can be shifted by framing, defaults and expectations. A fee described as a “surcharge” or a discount described as a “loss of discount” changes the reference point without changing the price, which is one reason consumer-protection rules in some countries address how price reductions and comparisons are presented.
