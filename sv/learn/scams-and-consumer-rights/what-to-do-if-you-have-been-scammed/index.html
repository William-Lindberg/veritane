What to Do If You Have Been Scammed

The steps people commonly take in the first hour, the first day and the first month after fraud.

Everyday·5 min (Simple) · 8 min (Detailed)·Updated 6 November 2026

Read first: Så fungerar bedrägerier: bedrägeriets anatomi

Key points

  • Speed matters: banks and payment providers can sometimes stop or recall a payment, and dispute windows are limited.
  • Fraud is reported to the police and to a national reporting body; which body that is varies by country.
  • Changing passwords and securing accounts stops a scam from continuing through the same door.
  • Messages, receipts, account numbers and dates are the evidence a bank or police officer will ask for.
  • 'Recovery' offers that arrive after a loss are, in documented cases, run by the same criminals.
  • Fraud is a crime done to the victim. Reporting it helps the victim and warns others.

Simple: plain words. Detailed: exact figures, codes and methods.

In short: after a scam, time is the most valuable thing. The bank first, then the reporting bodies, then the accounts, then the paperwork.

Simple version

It happens to many people

Being scammed is not a sign of being foolish. Scams are run by organised criminals using scripts that are tested on thousands of people. Shame is part of how they work: a victim who stays quiet does not warn anyone. Reporting helps the victim and the next person.

A timeline

Below is the order of steps that fraud agencies commonly describe (Scamwatch; IdentityTheft.gov). It is a description of the usual route, not legal advice.

The first hour

  • Contacting the bank or payment provider. People use a number they already trust, such as the one on the back of the card or in the banking app. A bank can sometimes stop a payment that is still going through, freeze money at the receiving bank, or block a card.
  • Closing the door. If the scammer still has access, through remote-access software, a shared code or a saved card, that access is what lets the losses continue.

The first day

  • Reporting the crime. Most countries have a police route and a national fraud or cybercrime reporting body. A report number is often needed for the bank’s investigation.
  • Changing passwords. Email usually comes first, because email can reset every other password. Banking comes next, then any account that shared the same password.
  • Turning on two-factor authentication where it is available.
  • Gathering the evidence. Screenshots of messages, the phone numbers and websites used, the payment amounts, dates, and the account details the money went to.

The first month

  • Watching statements and credit reports for new activity.
  • Following up with the bank in writing, with copies kept.
  • Treating new contact about the scam with caution (see below).

What to say to the bank

A short, clear account helps: “I have been the victim of a scam. On [date] I sent ¤[amount] to account [details]. I was told [story]. I am reporting it now.” Bank staff hear these reports often; the details let them act quickly.

The recovery scam

People who have lost money are often contacted again. The caller may claim to be a lawyer, a regulator or a “fund recovery” firm that can get the money back for a fee. The UK’s financial regulator describes these “recovery room” scams and notes that they target people who have already lost money (FCA).

Example

A victim loses ¤2,000 (example) to a fake investment site. A week later a “recovery agent” offers to retrieve it for an upfront fee of ¤300. The fee is paid and nothing comes back. The total loss is now ¤2,300, and the victim’s details are passed to the next group.

Detailed version

Recall, freezing and dispute routes

  • Bank transfers. An authorised transfer is final in principle. Recovery depends on the sending bank requesting a recall, the receiving bank freezing the funds before they move on, and any national reimbursement rule. Money laundered through several accounts within hours is rarely traced in full.
  • Card payments. The card schemes run chargeback processes with their own reason codes and time limits, counted from the transaction or the expected delivery date. A chargebackChargebackA reversal of a card payment, requested through the card provider, when goods or services were not delivered as agreed.Read more about Chargeback is easier where goods were not delivered or the payment was not authorised than where the cardholder knowingly paid a fraudster.
  • Crypto, gift cards and cash. Recovery generally depends on a platform or issuer freezing the funds before they are spent or withdrawn.

Reimbursement frameworks for authorised scams

Rules for scams where the victim made the payment differ sharply:

Example Approach
United Kingdom Since October 2024, a mandatory reimbursement requirement covers most authorised push payment scams over Faster Payments and CHAPS, with costs shared between sending and receiving payment firms and some exceptions (PSR).
United States Federal electronic-transfer rules focus on transfers the consumer did not authorise; reimbursement of authorised scam payments is largely a matter of bank policy.
Many other countries Reimbursement is decided case by case, by the bank or through a financial ombudsman or court.

Rules in this area have changed often in recent years, so the current text from the national regulator or ombudsman is the reference.

Reporting routes

Generic route: the bank or payment provider, then the police, then the national fraud or cybercrime reporting body, then the data-protection or consumer authority where personal data or a trader is involved. Examples:

  • Australia: Scamwatch collects scam reports and shares them with law enforcement and other agencies (Scamwatch). IDCARE offers identity-theft support in Australia and New Zealand (IDCARE).
  • Canada: the Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre takes reports nationally (CAFC).
  • India: the National Cyber Crime Reporting Portal takes online reports of financial cyber fraud (cybercrime.gov.in).
  • United States: the Federal Trade Commission collects fraud reports and publishes identity-theft recovery plans (ReportFraud; IdentityTheft.gov).

Cross-border cases. When the trader or scammer is abroad, econsumer.gov, run by an international network of consumer-protection agencies, accepts complaints and shares them with participating authorities (econsumer.gov). Recovery across borders is slower and less likely, but reports help authorities link cases.

Recovery-scam markers

Documented recovery scams share features: unsolicited contact after a loss; knowledge of the original amount; an upfront fee or “tax” before any release; claims of links to regulators, police or courts; and pressure to keep the contact secret (FCA). Legitimate routes (the bank, the police, an ombudsman) generally do not charge victims an upfront fee to investigate.
