Tax-advantaged Accounts: The Generic Pattern

Many countries offer accounts where long-term saving is taxed less; the designs follow a few patterns.

Intermediate·6 min (Simple) · 10 min (Detailed)·Updated 6 November 2026

Read first: How Pension Systems Work, Income Tax: Brackets, Marginal and Average Rates

Key points

  • Saving can be taxed at three moments: when money goes in, while it grows, and when it comes out.
  • Labels such as EET and TEE describe which of the three moments is exempt (E) and which is taxed (T).
  • Contribution limits and minimum withdrawal ages are common features.
  • Taking money out early often brings a penalty or a tax charge.
  • Which account suits a person depends on local rules; this lesson explains the patterns, not the choice.

In short: a tax-advantaged accountTax-advantaged accountAn account where saving or investing is taxed less, under rules that vary by country.Read more about Tax-advantaged account is one where saving is taxed less than usual. The designs look different from country to country, but they follow a small number of patterns.

Simple version

Three moments when saving can be taxed

Picture money on a journey through a savings account:

  1. Going in. You put money in. Is it money you have already paid income tax on, or is the tax removed or refunded?
  2. Growing. The money earns interest, dividends or gains. Is that growth taxed each year?
  3. Coming out. You take the money out in retirement. Is the withdrawal taxed as income?

Each moment can be E (exempt, no tax) or T (taxed). A three-letter label puts them in order: in, growth, out.

Two common patterns

  • EET: no tax going in, no tax on growth, tax on the way out. Many workplace pensions work like this.
  • TEE: money goes in from taxed income, then growth and withdrawals are tax-free.

Example

Assume an income tax rate of 20 %, growth of 5 % a year, and 30 years (all examples).

EET: ¤1,000 of pay goes in before tax. It grows to about ¤4,322. Tax of 20 % on the way out leaves about ¤3,458.

TEE: the same ¤1,000 of pay is taxed first, leaving ¤800. It grows to about ¤3,458, and nothing more is taxed.

With the same tax rate at both ends, the two patterns give the same result. The difference appears when the tax rate in retirement differs from the rate while working.

When the tax rate changes

  • If the tax rate in retirement is lower (say 10 %), the EET account above ends with about ¤3,890.
  • If it is higher (say 30 %), the EET account ends with about ¤3,025.

Nobody knows their future tax rate for certain, and tax rules themselves change over the decades.

Common rules attached to these accounts

  • Contribution limits: a cap on how much can go in each year, and sometimes over a lifetime.
  • Minimum age: money is meant for retirement, so there is often an age before which withdrawals are restricted.
  • Early-withdrawal penalties: taking money out early may bring an extra tax charge or a loss of the tax benefit.
  • Employer contributions: some workplace accounts receive money from the employer, which is a separate benefit on top of the tax treatment.
  • Salary sacrificeSalary sacrificeGiving up part of pay in exchange for a benefit such as pension contributions, with tax effects that vary by country.Read more about Salary sacrifice: in some countries, workers can give up part of their pay in exchange for a pension contribution, with tax effects that vary.

Questions to ask about any account

  • Which of the three moments is taxed?
  • What are the yearly and lifetime limits?
  • From what age can the money be taken out, and what happens if it is taken out earlier?
  • Does an employer add money, and under what conditions?
  • What are the fees?

Varies by country

  • United States: a traditional IRA broadly follows an EET pattern, while a Roth IRA broadly follows a TEE pattern.
  • United Kingdom: private pensions receive tax relief on contributions and are taxed on withdrawal (with a tax-free portion), while an ISA holds money from taxed income and pays out tax-free.
  • New Zealand: KiwiSaver contributions come from taxed income and fund earnings are taxed, which is often described as a TTE pattern.

Detailed version

Equivalence of EET and TEE

Let C be pre-tax contribution, g the annual growth rate, n years and t a constant income tax rate.

EET:  C × (1 + g)^n × (1 − t)
TEE:  C × (1 − t) × (1 + g)^n

Multiplication is commutative, so with a constant t and the same g the two are identical. In the example above: 1,000 × 1.05^30 × 0.8 = 3,457.55 and 800 × 1.05^30 = 3,457.55.

The equivalence breaks when:

  • Tax rates differ. With a withdrawal-time rate t₂ different from a contribution-time rate t₁, EET/TEE = (1 − t₂)/(1 − t₁). Progressive tax schedules make this common, because retirement income is often lower than peak earnings.
  • Limits bind. A TEE limit of ¤X holds more pre-tax value than an EET limit of ¤X, because the TEE contribution is already net of tax.
  • Growth is taxed (T in the middle). Taxing returns each year compounds the drag: after-tax growth is g(1 − t) per year, not g.
  • Means-tested benefits interact. Taxable pension income can reduce income-tested benefits in some countries, which raises the effective withdrawal rate.

Rate arbitrage

Deductible contributions at a high marginal tax rateMarginal tax rateThe tax rate on the next unit of income earned.Read more about Marginal tax rate followed by withdrawals at a lower average or marginal rate produce a gain beyond the tax-free growth. The gain is policy-dependent: future rate schedules, thresholds and withdrawal rules are set by future legislatures.

Limits and allowances

Annual limits, lifetime limits and tapered allowances for high earners are common design tools. They change frequently, so official pages such as the IRS retirement-plan pages (IRS) or GOV.UK’s pension tax relief page (GOV.UK) are the reference for current values.

Employer match economics

A match of 50 % on contributions up to 6 % of salary (example) is an immediate 50 % addition on the matched amount, before any investment return. Its value depends on vesting: if the match vests after several years, its expected value to a worker who may leave earlier is lower. The match and the tax treatment are separate effects and are best evaluated separately.

Why people disagree

Economists disagree about how much tax incentives add to total saving. One view is that incentives raise saving, particularly when combined with automatic enrolment and employer contributions. Another view is that a large part of incentivised saving is moved from other accounts rather than newly saved, and that the tax relief goes disproportionately to higher earners, who save more and face higher marginal rates. A third position points to defaults, rather than tax rates, as the main driver of participation. Designs such as flat-rate relief or government matching are attempts to address these concerns.
