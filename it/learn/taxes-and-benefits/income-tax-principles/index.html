Income Tax: Brackets, Marginal and Average Rates

A higher tax bracket applies only to the income above its threshold, so the rate on your next ¤ and the rate on all your income are two different numbers.

Intermediate·6 min (Simple) · 10 min (Detailed)·Updated 6 November 2026

Read first: Income, Expenses and Cash Flow, Why Taxes Exist and Where They Go

Key points

  • Tax brackets apply to slices of income, not to the whole income.
  • The marginal rate is the tax on the next ¤ earned; the average rate is total tax divided by total income.
  • Under a pure bracket system, a pay rise cannot leave you with less after income tax. Benefit withdrawal can change this in some countries.
  • Allowances and deductions reduce the income that is taxed; tax credits reduce the tax itself.
  • Some countries take tax from pay automatically; others rely more on people filing their own return. This varies by country.

Simple: plain words. Detailed: exact figures, codes and methods.

In short: income tax is charged in slices. Moving into a higher bracket raises the rate only on the income inside that bracket, not on everything you earn.

Simple version

Income comes in slices

Most income tax systems split income into bands, called brackets. Each bracket has its own rate. Here is an illustrative system (example, not any real country):

Slice of yearly income Rate on that slice
¤0 to ¤10,000 0%
¤10,000 to ¤40,000 20%
Above ¤40,000 40%

The first ¤10,000 of everyone’s income is taxed at 0%. The next ¤30,000 is taxed at 20%. Only income above ¤40,000 is taxed at 40%.

Example

A teacher earns ¤50,000 a year (example).

  • First ¤10,000 at 0% = ¤0
  • Next ¤30,000 at 20% = ¤6,000
  • Last ¤10,000 at 40% = ¤4,000

Total tax: ¤10,000. The teacher’s income after this tax is ¤40,000.

Two rates, two questions

  • The marginal tax rateMarginal tax rateThe tax rate on the next unit of income earned.Read more about Marginal tax rate is the rate on the next ¤ you earn. For the teacher, it is 40%.
  • The average tax rate is total tax divided by total income. For the teacher, ¤10,000 ÷ ¤50,000 = 20%.

The marginal rate tells you what happens to extra income, such as overtime. The average rate tells you how much of your whole income goes in tax.

The pay-rise myth

A common worry is: “If my pay rise puts me in a higher bracket, I will take home less.” Under a bracket system like the one above, that cannot happen. If the teacher’s pay goes up by ¤100 to ¤50,100, the extra ¤100 is taxed at 40%. Tax rises by ¤40 and the teacher keeps ¤60 more than before.

There is one exception to know about. In some countries, benefits or credits are reduced as income rises. Together with income tax, this can take a large share of extra income. The lesson on benefits and social insurance explains how.

Allowances, deductions and credits

  • An allowance is income you can earn before any tax is charged. In the example, it is the 0% slice.
  • A deductionTax deductionAn amount subtracted from income before tax is calculated.Read more about Tax deduction lowers the income that is taxed. A ¤1,000 deduction saves ¤1,000 × your marginal rate. For the teacher, that is ¤400.
  • A tax creditTax creditAn amount subtracted directly from the tax owed.Read more about Tax credit lowers the tax itself. A ¤400 credit saves ¤400 whatever your rate.

That is why a deduction is worth more to someone in a higher bracket, while a credit is worth the same to everyone who can use it in full.

How the tax is collected

In many countries, an employer takes income tax from each pay packet and sends it to the tax authority. This is called withholding or pay as you earn. In others, more people file a yearly return and pay or get a refund. Many systems combine the two. Self-employed people usually report their own income.

Detailed version

Definitions

tax(y) = Σ over brackets k of  rate_k × max(0, min(y, upper_k) − lower_k)

average rate  = tax(y) / y
marginal rate = d tax / d y   (the rate of the bracket that contains y)

Under a schedule in which every marginal rate is below 100%, after-tax income rises with gross income. That is the formal version of the pay-rise point.

Effective marginal tax rates

The marginal rate in the income tax schedule is often not the rate that applies to the next ¤. The effective marginal tax rate (EMTR) adds every change that extra earnings trigger:

EMTR = (Δ income tax + Δ social contributions + Δ benefits withdrawn) / Δ gross earnings

Example: a worker earns ¤100 more. Income tax takes ¤20, social contributions ¤10, and a means-tested benefit is reduced by ¤40. The EMTR is (20 + 10 + 40) ÷ 100 = 70%, and the worker keeps ¤30. In the United Kingdom, for example, Universal Credit is reduced by a set amount for every £1 earned above any work allowance (GOV.UK), which combines with income tax and National Insurance to give the EMTR.

Bracket creep and indexation

If thresholds stay fixed while wages rise with prices, more income moves into higher brackets and real tax rises without any change in law. This is bracket creepBracket creepInflation pushing incomes into higher tax brackets when the thresholds are not raised in line with prices.Read more about Bracket creep. Using the example schedule:

Case Income Thresholds Tax Average rate
Year 1 ¤40,000 10,000 / 40,000 ¤6,000 15.0%
Year 2, prices and wages +5%, thresholds fixed ¤42,000 10,000 / 40,000 ¤6,800 16.2%
Year 2, thresholds raised 5% ¤42,000 10,500 / 42,000 ¤6,300 15.0%

Raising thresholds in line with prices is called indexationIndexationAutomatically adjusting a payment or threshold in line with inflation.Read more about Indexation. Some countries index automatically each year; others decide each year or freeze thresholds for a period.

Individual or household taxation

Some systems tax each person separately; others tax a couple or family together. With joint taxation and progressive rates, a second earner’s first ¤ can be taxed at the first earner’s marginal rate, which raises the second earner’s EMTR. With individual taxation, two couples with the same total income can pay different total tax depending on how the income is split.

Social contributions

Compulsory social insurance contributions are levied on earnings like a tax, but are often linked to future entitlements (pensions, unemployment benefits). Statistical agencies count them with taxes in tax-to-GDP ratios. For comparing take-home pay across countries, income tax alone is an incomplete measure.

How professionals read a tax schedule

  • Check whether thresholds apply to gross income or to taxable income after deductions.
  • Check whether the published rate is marginal or average.
  • Look for allowances that are withdrawn as income rises: they create a hidden marginal band. In the United Kingdom, the Personal Allowance is reduced for incomes above a set level (GOV.UK).

Why people disagree

How progressive income tax ought to be is a long-running debate. One side argues that higher top rates raise revenue from people with the most ability to pay, with modest effects on how much they work; Diamond and Saez set out this case using estimates of how taxable income responds to rates (Journal of Economic Perspectives, 2011). The other side argues that a single rate with a large allowance is simpler, harder to avoid and less discouraging to work, saving and enterprise; Hall and Rabushka’s flat tax is a well-known proposal of this kind (Hoover Institution). The disagreement turns partly on evidence (how strongly people respond to tax rates) and partly on values (how to weigh fairness against simplicity and efficiency).

Varies by country

How income tax is collected differs. In the United Kingdom, most employees have tax taken from their pay through PAYE and many do not file a return. In the United States, employers withhold tax, but most people also file an annual return that settles the final amount. In Sweden, the tax agency sends many people a pre-filled return that they check and approve (Skatteverket). Whether couples are taxed separately or together also differs: the United Kingdom taxes individuals, while Germany and the United States allow married couples to be assessed jointly.
