Anatomy of a Loan

Principal, rate, term, amortisation, security and covenants: the parts of every loan agreement.

Intermediate·6 min (Simple) · 10 min (Detailed)·Updated 6 November 2026

Read first: Il costo reale di un prestito

Key points

  • Instalment loans repay principal and interest on a schedule; early payments are mostly interest.
  • Secured loans are backed by an asset the lender can take if payments stop; unsecured loans are not.
  • A longer term lowers each payment but raises the total interest paid.
  • Fees can be charged at the start, during the loan or on early exit, and they add to the real cost.
  • The key facts page sets out the rate, total cost and fees in one place before the signature page.

Simple: plain words. Detailed: exact figures, codes and methods.

In short: every loan, from a small personal loan to a mortgage, is built from the same few parts: how much you borrow, the price of borrowing it, how long you take to pay it back, and what happens if you cannot.

Simple version

The parts of a loan

A loan agreement looks long, but it rests on a handful of ideas:

  • Principal: the amount you borrow. This is the principalPrincipalThe original amount borrowed or invested, before interest.Read more about Principal.
  • Interest rate: the price of borrowing, shown as a percentage of the amount you still owe each year.
  • Term: how long you have to pay it back, for example three years or 25 years.
  • Payment: the amount you pay each month (or week).
  • Fees: extra charges, such as an arrangement fee at the start or a fee for paying early.

The APR (the yearly cost of borrowing including most fees) puts the rate and most fees into one number. The annual percentage rateAnnual percentage rate (APR)The yearly cost of borrowing as a percentage, usually including interest and most fees, with exact rules that vary by country.Read more about Annual percentage rate (APR) lesson in the Credit & Debt module explains it in detail.

How the payments work

Most personal loans and mortgages are instalment loans (loans paid back in equal regular payments). Each payment does two things. Part of it pays the interest for that month. The rest reduces the amount you owe.

At the start you owe the most, so the interest part is large. As the amount owed falls, the interest part shrinks and more of each payment goes to the principal. Paying a loan down this way is called amortisationAmortisationPaying off a loan in scheduled instalments, where early payments are mostly interest and later payments are mostly principal.Read more about Amortisation.

Example

You borrow ¤10,000 (example) at 6% a year for three years. The monthly payment is about ¤304.22.

Month Interest part Principal part Still owed
1 ¤50.00 ¤254.22 ¤9,745.78
2 ¤48.73 ¤255.49 ¤9,490.29
18 ¤27.51 ¤276.71 ¤5,224.29
36 ¤1.51 ¤302.71 ¤0

Over the three years you pay about ¤951.90 in interest. Over five years the payment falls to about ¤193.33, but the total interest rises to about ¤1,599.68.

The same pattern shows why the term matters. A longer term means smaller payments, but you pay interest for more months.

Secured and unsecured

A secured loanSecured loanA loan backed by an asset that the lender can take if the borrower does not repay.Read more about Secured loan is backed by something you own, called collateralCollateralAn asset a borrower pledges that the lender can take if the loan is not repaid.Read more about Collateral. A mortgage is secured on a home; many car loans are secured on the car. If the payments stop, the lender has a legal right to take and sell that asset.

An unsecured loanUnsecured loanA loan backed only by the borrower's promise to repay.Read more about Unsecured loan has no asset behind it. The lender relies on your promise to pay and on court action if you do not. Because the lender has less protection, unsecured loans often carry higher rates.

Some loans need a guarantorGuarantorSomeone who agrees to repay a loan or rent if the borrower or tenant does not.Read more about Guarantor: another person who agrees to pay if you cannot. A guarantor takes on a real debt, not a formality.

What happens if a payment is missed?

The details are in the agreement, but a missed payment commonly leads to:

  1. a late fee and extra interest on the missed amount,
  2. a note on your credit report, which other lenders can see,
  3. letters and calls from the lender,
  4. after several missed payments, the loan being treated as in default (the lender can then demand the full balance or, for a secured loan, start taking the asset).

Many lenders have a process for borrowers in difficulty, such as a short payment break or a lower payment for a while. Contacting the lender early is the usual route to these options.

Five questions before signing

These questions help reveal how a loan works:

  1. What is the total amount I will pay back, including all fees?
  2. Is the rate fixed for the whole term, or can it change?
  3. Is the loan secured on anything I own?
  4. What does it cost to pay early, in part or in full?
  5. What happens, step by step, if I miss a payment?

The answers usually sit on the key facts page (a short summary the lender gives before you sign). It is a shorter read than the full contract.

Detailed version

The amortisation formula

For a loan of principal P, a periodic rate i (the annual nominal rate divided by the number of payments per year) and n payments, the level payment is:

M = P × i / (1 − (1 + i)^−n)

With P = 10,000, i = 0.06 / 12 = 0.005 and n = 36, M = 304.22. In period t, interest is the opening balance × i, and the principal repaid is M minus that interest. The outstanding balance after k payments is the present value of the remaining payments: B_k = M × (1 − (1 + i)^−(n−k)) / i. When i = 0, the payment is simply P / n.

Lenders quote rates in different ways. A nominal annual rate of 6% paid monthly is an effective annual rate of (1.005)^12 − 1 = 6.17%. Disclosure rules decide which figure appears in advertising (see “Varies by country”).

Other structures

  • Interest-only: the borrower pays only interest during the term and repays the whole principal at the end. On ¤10,000 at 6%, the payment is ¤50 a month, and ¤10,000 is still owed after the last payment. An interest-only loanInterest-only loanA loan where the regular payments cover only interest and the whole principal is due at the end.Read more about Interest-only loan needs a separate plan for that final sum.
  • Balloon: smaller regular payments plus a large final balloon paymentBalloon paymentA large final payment due at the end of a loan, after a series of smaller regular payments.Read more about Balloon payment. Common in some car finance.
  • Variable rate: the rate follows a reference rate or the lender’s own rate, so the payment is recalculated when it changes. The Bank of England’s explainer describes how a change in the policy rate passes through to borrowing costs (Bank of England).

Covenants, default and guarantees

  • Covenants are promises in the contract beyond paying: keeping the collateral insured, not selling it, or (in business loans) keeping certain financial ratios.
  • Events of default list what lets the lender demand early repayment: missed payments, a broken covenant, insolvency or false information in the application.
  • Cross-default clauses treat a default on one debt as a default on another debt with the same lender or group. They are common in business lending and less common in consumer contracts.
  • Guarantor liability can be “joint and several”, meaning the lender may pursue the guarantor for the whole balance without first exhausting action against the borrower. The wording of the guarantee decides this.

Disclosure and cooling-off

Consumer credit laws in many countries require a standard information sheet before signing and a period during which the borrower can withdraw. In the European Union, consumer credit rules give a 14-day right to withdraw from most credit agreements and require standardised pre-contract information (Directive 2008/48/EC; Directive (EU) 2023/2225). In the United States, the Truth in Lending rules require disclosure of the APR and finance charge, and give a three-business-day right of rescission for certain loans secured on the borrower’s main home (Regulation Z).

Common misconceptions

  • “A lower payment means a cheaper loan.” Not necessarily: a longer term lowers the payment and raises total interest.
  • “Overpaying reduces next month’s payment.” On many loans, an overpayment shortens the term instead; the agreement states which.
  • “The rate is the cost.” Fees paid at the start or end change the cost; the APR exists to capture them.

Varies by country

Cooling-off and disclosure rules differ. In the European Union, most consumer credit agreements can be withdrawn from within 14 days. The United Kingdom kept a similar 14-day withdrawal right for most regulated credit agreements. In the United States, a three-business-day right of rescission applies to certain loans secured on the borrower’s main home, such as many refinancings, but not to the loan used to buy the home.
