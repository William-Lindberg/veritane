Student and Education Loans

How education borrowing differs from other debt, and the questions to ask before taking it.

Intermediate·6 min (Simple) · 10 min (Detailed)·Updated 6 November 2026

Read first: Anatomy of a Loan

Key points

  • Education loans differ enormously by country: income-contingent public schemes, fixed-term government loans, private loans, or free tuition.
  • In some systems repayment depends on income rather than on the balance owed.
  • Interest can build up while you are still studying.
  • Education is an investment with uncertain returns; the loan is one cost to weigh against them.
  • Forgiveness and write-off rules are set country by country.

Simple: plain words. Detailed: exact figures, codes and methods.

In short: a student loan pays for study now, to be repaid from earnings later. How it is repaid, and how much of it is ever repaid, depends heavily on where you study and which scheme the loan comes from.

Simple version

Why education loans are different

Most loans are paid back in fixed amounts whatever your income. Many public student loans are not. They are linked to what you earn after you leave study. Some are written off after a set number of years. In some countries, tuition at public universities is free and the main costs are living costs.

So “a ¤30,000 student loan” can mean very different things in two countries.

Three broad models

1. Income-contingent loans. You repay a share of your income above a threshold. If you earn less than the threshold, you pay nothing that month. Any balance left after a set number of years is written off. England uses this model: repayments are a percentage of income above a threshold, collected through the tax system for employees (GOV.UK).

2. Fixed-instalment public loans. You repay a set amount each month over a long period, like an ordinary loan, but on public terms. Sweden’s student loans from CSN are repaid in regular instalments over many years (CSN).

3. Private loans. Banks or other lenders offer loans on commercial terms. The rate depends on your credit history or a co-signer’s, and repayment usually follows a fixed schedule. In the United States, private loans sit alongside federal loans.

Example

An income-contingent scheme (example) takes 9% of income above ¤25,000 a year. A graduate earning ¤35,000 repays 9% × (35,000 − 25,000) = ¤900 a year, or ¤75 a month. If income falls to ¤24,000, the repayment falls to ¤0. The balance owed does not change the monthly amount.

A fixed-instalment loan of ¤30,000 at 5% over ten years (example) costs about ¤318.20 a month whatever the graduate earns.

Interest while you study

On many loans, interest starts as soon as the money is paid out, even while you are still studying. It is added to the balance, so you then pay interest on the interest. This is compound interestCompound interestInterest earned on both the original amount and the interest already added.Read more about Compound interest working against the borrower.

Example

A student borrows ¤10,000 at the start of each of three years at 5% a year (example). By the end of the third year, the balance is about ¤33,101: ¤30,000 borrowed plus about ¤3,101 of interest, before any repayment starts.

Some schemes pay the interest for students during study, or charge none. The loan terms say which.

A loan for an investment

Education often raises earnings later, but not by the same amount for everyone. The gain depends on the subject, the institution, the job market and the person. The loan is certain; the gain is not. Thinking about both sides helps: the likely cost of the loan in your system, and the range of possible outcomes for the course.

Questions to ask

  1. Is repayment linked to income, or fixed?
  2. When does interest start, and is it fixed or linked to something like inflation?
  3. When does repayment start, and is there a threshold?
  4. Is any balance written off, and after how long?
  5. What happens if I move abroad, stop working or earn very little?
  6. If it is a private loan, does it need a co-signer, and what happens to them if I cannot pay?

Detailed version

Income-contingent repayment as a graduate tax

Under an income-contingent loan (ICL), the annual repayment is:

R_t = max(0, s × (Y_t − T))

where s is the repayment rate, Y_t income in year t and T the threshold. The loan balance accrues interest at the scheme rate; the balance affects how long repayments last, not their size. With a write-off after N years, total repayment equals the smaller of (balance plus interest) and the sum of R_t over N years.

For graduates who will not clear the balance before write-off, the loan behaves like a time-limited extra tax on income above T: a higher interest rate on the balance changes nothing they pay. For high earners who repay in full, it behaves like an ordinary loan, and the interest rate matters. This is why the same scheme is regressive or progressive depending on where in the earnings distribution a graduate sits. England’s scheme is described on GOV.UK. The United States offers income-driven repayment plans for federal loans alongside standard fixed schedules (Federal Student Aid), and federal loan rates are fixed for each loan, with new rates set each year by a formula in law (Federal Student Aid).

The earnings premium as a present value

The financial return to a course compares the present value of expected extra earnings with the present value of costs (fees, living costs, and earnings forgone while studying):

NPV = Σ (ΔE_t − C_t) / (1 + r)^t

The OECD’s Education at a Glance reports that, across member countries, adults with tertiary education earn more than those with upper-secondary education, with large differences between countries and fields of study (OECD, 2024). These are averages over groups. They do not separate the effect of the education from the effect of who chooses to study, and they give the past premium, not the future one.

Default and collection

Public loans collected through the tax or payroll system have low arrears by design. Fixed-instalment loans can go into default, and public lenders in some countries have collection powers that private lenders lack, such as offsetting tax refunds or deducting from wages. Write-off rules on death or disability also differ.

Refinancing trade-offs

Refinancing a public loan with a private lender can lower the rate for a high earner, but it usually ends public features: income-linked payments, write-off and hardship options. That option value is easy to miss when only rates are compared.

Common misconceptions

  • “A bigger balance means bigger payments.” Not under an income-contingent scheme, where income sets the payment.
  • “Student debt works like a credit card.” Collection, credit reporting and insolvency treatment are often different; in some countries student loans cannot be discharged in bankruptcy in the usual way.

Varies by country

Education finance differs widely. England uses income-contingent loans repaid through the tax system and written off after a set period. Australia’s HELP loans are also repaid as a share of income above a threshold, and their balance is indexed to inflation rather than charged a commercial rate. In Germany, public universities charge no tuition fees for most students, and the state support scheme (BAföG) is part grant and part interest-free loan. In the United States, federal loans with fixed rates and private loans exist side by side.

Why people disagree

How to pay for higher education is a long-running policy debate. One view holds that graduates gain most of the benefit, so they should pay much of the cost, and that income-contingent loans do this while protecting low earners. Another view holds that education benefits society as a whole, through higher productivity, tax revenue and civic life, so public funding or free tuition is justified and loans can discourage students from poorer backgrounds. A third view stresses that the design details (thresholds, interest rates and write-off periods) matter more than the label. Countries have chosen different mixes.
