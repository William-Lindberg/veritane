What Insurance Is

Many people pay a little so that the few who suffer a loss receive a lot.

Everyday·5 min (Simple) · 9 min (Detailed)·Updated 6 November 2026

Read first: Il fondo di emergenza

Key points

  • Insurance pools risk: premiums from many people pay the claims of a few, plus the insurer's costs and margin.
  • Insurance is mainly useful for losses a person could not absorb from savings, not for every possible loss.
  • A premium reflects how likely a loss is and how large it would be, plus a loading for costs and profit.
  • A policy is a contract: its exclusions and conditions matter as much as the headline cover.
  • Public insurance, such as health or unemployment cover, exists in many countries and changes which private cover is relevant.

Simple: plain words. Detailed: exact figures, codes and methods.

In short: insurance is a way of sharing losses. Many people each pay a small, known amount so that the few who suffer a large loss are paid.

Simple version

The pool

Imagine 1,000 households in a town. Each year, about 1 in 100 of them has a fire or flood that costs ¤20,000 to repair (example). Nobody knows in advance which household it will be.

A household facing that bill alone might have to borrow, sell things or go without. If all 1,000 households put money into a shared pot, the pot pays the households that are hit.

Example

  • Expected losses: 1,000 households × 1 % × ¤20,000 = ¤200,000 a year.
  • Shared equally, that is ¤200,000 ÷ 1,000 = ¤200 per household.
  • The insurer also has costs (staff, systems, claims handling, taxes) and aims to make a profit. If it adds 30 % on top, the premium becomes ¤200 × 1.3 = ¤260.

Each household swaps an uncertain chance of losing ¤20,000 for a certain cost of ¤260.

The regular price paid for cover is called the insurance premiumInsurance premiumThe regular price paid for insurance cover.Read more about Insurance premium.

Why pay more than the average loss?

In the example, the premium (¤260) is higher than the average loss (¤200). Across all households, the pool pays out less than it collects. So why buy insurance at all?

Because a large loss can do lasting harm. Losing ¤260 every year is a known, manageable cost. Losing ¤20,000 in one year might mean debt for years. Insurance makes sense for many people where the loss would be too big to absorb.

“Could I absorb this loss?”

A useful question for any type of cover is: if this happened, could I pay for it from savings without serious harm?

  • A broken phone screen: for some households, yes.
  • A house fire, a serious injury, or being sued for damage to someone else: for most households, no.

The parts of a policy

A policy is a contract. Its main parts are:

  • Cover: what events and losses are paid for, and up to what limit.
  • Exclusions: what is not covered, even if it looks similar.
  • Conditions: what the policyholder has to do for a claim to be paid (for example, lock doors, report quickly, tell the truth when applying).
  • Excess or deductible: the part of each claim you pay yourself.
  • Premium: the price, and how often it is paid.

The headline (“home insurance”) says little. The exclusions and conditions decide what is actually paid.

Public and private insurance

Many governments run insurance too: public health systems, unemployment benefits, workplace injury schemes, disability pensions. What these cover changes which private insurance is relevant in each country.

Detailed version

Expected loss and loading

For a single risk with loss probability p and loss size L:

expected loss (pure premium) = p × L
gross premium                = pure premium × (1 + loading)

The loading covers acquisition costs (including commission), administration, claims handling, the cost of holding capital, taxes and profit. The economic case rests on risk aversion: a certain small cost is preferred to an uncertain large one when the large loss would cut deeply into wealth or income.

Adverse selection and moral hazard

  • Adverse selectionAdverse selectionWhen the people keenest to buy a product, such as insurance, are the ones most likely to claim, because they know more about their own risk than the seller does.Read more about Adverse selection: buyers know more about their own risk than the insurer. If one price is charged to everyone, higher-risk people buy more cover, average claims rise, and premiums rise in turn. Underwriting questions, waiting periods and compulsory or group schemes are responses.
  • Moral hazardMoral hazardTaking more risk because someone else bears the cost, such as after being insured or rescued.Read more about Moral hazard: cover can change behaviour, for example less care with insured property. Deductibles, co-payments and policy conditions keep some of the loss with the policyholder.

Underwriting

Underwriting is the process of assessing and pricing a risk. Insurers group policyholders by characteristics that predict claims (location, property type, age of a vehicle, claims history). Some characteristics are restricted by law. In the European Union, for example, the Court of Justice ruled in 2011 that sex could no longer be used as a factor that leads to different individual premiums and benefits (CJEU, Test-Achats).

Solvency regulation

Because premiums are collected before claims are paid, regulators require insurers to hold capital and reserves. The International Association of Insurance Supervisors publishes the Insurance Core Principles used as a common standard (IAIS). In the European Union, the Solvency II directive sets risk-based capital requirements (EUR-Lex). In the United States, insurance is regulated mainly by the states, coordinated through the national association of commissioners (NAIC).

Reinsurance

Insurers insure themselves too. Reinsurance transfers part of an insurer’s risk to another company, which limits the effect of a single large event (such as a storm affecting many policies at once) on the original insurer.

Correlated losses

Pooling works best when losses are independent: one house fire does not cause another. When losses are correlated (floods, earthquakes, pandemics), the pool gives less protection and premiums or public schemes reflect that.

Why people disagree

How finely insurers should be allowed to price individual risk is debated. One view holds that risk-based pricing is fair, because people pay for the cover they are likely to use, and that it encourages risk reduction. Another view emphasises solidarity: some sources of risk (such as genetic conditions or living in a flood area) are not chosen, and pricing them closely can make cover unaffordable for those who need it most. Countries answer differently, through bans on certain rating factors, community rating in health insurance, or public reinsurance schemes.
