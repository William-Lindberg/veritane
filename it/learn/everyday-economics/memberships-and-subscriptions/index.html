Memberships and Subscriptions

How recurring charges work, why businesses offer them, and how to measure what each one costs per use.

Everyday·4 min (Simple) · 7 min (Detailed)·Updated 6 November 2026

Read first: Reducing Spending Without Misery

Key points

  • A subscription turns a one-time decision into a default: it continues until someone stops it.
  • Cost per use (the yearly price divided by how often you use it) shows what each use really costs.
  • Annual plans are often cheaper per month than monthly plans, in exchange for less flexibility.
  • Free trials usually turn into paid plans on a set date; cancellation rules differ by country.
  • A yearly list of every recurring charge makes forgotten ones visible.

Simple: plain words. Detailed: exact figures, codes and methods.

In short: a subscription keeps charging until it is stopped. Dividing the cost by the number of times you use it shows whether each use is worth its price to you.

Simple version

A decision that repeats itself

When you buy a coffee, you decide each time. When you sign up for a streaming service, a gym, a meal box or a software plan, you decide once and the payment repeats every month. Doing nothing means the payment continues.

Economists call the option that happens when you do nothing the defaultDefault (choice)What happens if a person does nothing, which has a large influence on what people end up with.Read more about Default (choice). Defaults have a large effect on behaviour, because changing them takes time and attention (Nobel Prize, 2017). For a business, a subscription means steady income. For a customer, it means convenience, and a risk of paying for things no longer used.

Cost per use

The fairest test of a subscription is what each use costs:

cost per use = cost for a period ÷ number of uses in that period

Example

A gym membership costs ¤40 a month (example). You go 4 times a month. Each visit costs ¤40 ÷ 4 = ¤10. If the gym also sells single visits for ¤8, paying per visit would cost ¤32 a month for the same 4 visits.

If you went 8 times a month, each visit would cost ¤5, and the membership would cost less than paying per visit.

The Is the Membership Worth It?A yearly membership fee set against the savings and perks it brings, minus any extra spending it leads to, with the number of visits or orders that cover the fee. does this sum and finds the number of uses where the membership and pay-per-use cost the same.

Monthly or yearly?

Many services offer a lower price for paying a year in advance.

Example

A service costs ¤12 a month or ¤120 a year (example). Twelve monthly payments add up to ¤144, so the yearly plan saves ¤24 if you keep the service all year.

But ¤120 ÷ ¤12 = 10. If you would stop after fewer than 10 months, the monthly plan costs less. The yearly plan trades flexibility for a discount.

Free trials

A free trial usually turns into a paid plan on a set date unless it is cancelled first. Writing down the end date of each trial when you start it makes that date visible. Some services ask for card details at the start for this reason.

The yearly audit

Small monthly charges are easy to forget. Listing them all once a year shows the total.

Example

Five charges (example): ¤9.99, ¤12.99, ¤4.99, ¤40.00 and ¤7.50. Together they are ¤75.47 a month, or ¤905.64 a year.

Detailed version

Subscription economics

Recurring-revenue businesses track a few standard measures:

  • Churn rate: the share of subscribers who cancel in a period.
  • Average revenue per user (ARPU): revenue per subscriber per period.
  • Customer lifetime value (LTV): a simple version is
LTV = ARPU × gross margin ÷ churn rate

With ¤10 a month, a 70% margin and 5% monthly churn: 10 × 0.7 ÷ 0.05 = ¤140. Because LTV rises as churn falls, firms invest in retention. Some retention is service quality; some comes from the effort it takes to cancel. Annual plans lower measured churn by fixing the customer for a year in exchange for a discount.

Inertia and attention

Behavioural economics explains why defaults persist: switching takes time, people put off small tasks (present bias), and a small monthly sum receives little attention. A subscription business benefits from this inertia even when no one designs for it. Module 14 covers these biases in detail.

Dark patterns and regulatory responses

Dark patterns are interface designs that steer users towards choices they would not otherwise make, such as hidden cancellation links, extra confirmation steps, or pre-ticked boxes. The UK Competition and Markets Authority describes how such “online choice architecture” can harm consumers and competition (CMA). Responses include:

  • Cancellation buttons: German law requires online contracts that can be concluded on a website to be cancellable through a clearly labelled button on that website (§ 312k BGB).
  • Subscription contract rules: the UK Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024 includes a chapter on subscription contracts, covering information before signing, reminders and ways to leave (legislation.gov.uk).
  • Automatic-renewal laws: several US states regulate automatic renewals and online cancellation.

Bundling

Bundles (several services for one price) make the cost of each part harder to see. A bundle can lower total cost for someone who uses every part, and raise it for someone who uses only one. The cost-per-use test applies to each part the household actually uses.

Common misconceptions

  • “It is only ¤5 a month.” ¤5 a month is ¤60 a year, and several such charges add up.
  • “The annual plan is cheaper.” It is cheaper per month only for the months the service is used.

Varies by country

Cancellation rules differ. In Germany, online contracts concluded on a website are required to offer a cancellation button (§ 312k BGB). In the United Kingdom, a 2024 law adds rules for subscription contracts (legislation.gov.uk). In the United States, rules on automatic renewal come mainly from state laws, which differ from state to state. Many countries also give a cooling-off period for contracts made online, with exceptions.
