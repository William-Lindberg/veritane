The Yield Curve

What the curve of government bond yields across maturities shows, and why its shape is watched.

Professional·5 min (Simple) · 10 min (Detailed)·Updated 6 November 2026

Read first: Bonds and Fixed Income

Key points

  • The yield curve plots the yields of government bonds against their maturity, from a few months to 30 years or more.
  • The usual shapes are upward-sloping (normal), flat and inverted (short yields above long yields).
  • The short end follows expectations of the central bank's policy rate; the long end adds expectations about growth and inflation, plus a term premium.
  • In several countries inversions have come before many recessions, but they do not tell when, or whether, one will start.
  • Spreads such as 10-year minus 2-year or 10-year minus 3-month are shorthand for the slope.

Simple: plain words. Detailed: exact figures, codes and methods.

In short: the yield curve is a single line that summarises what lenders charge a government to borrow for different lengths of time. Its slope reflects what markets expect from interest rates, growth and inflation.

Simple version

What the curve is

Governments borrow by selling bonds. Some are repaid in three months, some in two years, some in thirty years. The time until repayment is the bond’s maturityMaturityThe date on which a loan or bond has to be repaid.Read more about Maturity. Each bond has a yieldBond yieldThe yearly return a bond gives at its current price, which rises when the price falls.Read more about Bond yield: the yearly return a buyer gets if they hold it to the end.

The yield curveYield curveA chart of bond yields across maturities, read as a signal of expectations for interest rates and growth.Read more about Yield curve is a chart. Maturity goes along the bottom; yield goes up the side. Each point is one maturity.

The three shapes

Shape What it looks like What it usually reflects
Normal Rises from left to right Lenders ask more for tying money up longer; markets do not expect rates to fall much
Flat Roughly level Markets expect rates to stay near where they are, or the extra pay for long loans is small
Inverted Falls from left to right Markets expect the central bank to cut rates in future, often because they expect slower growth

What each end reflects

  • The short end (months to two years) closely follows the central bank’s policy ratePolicy rateThe interest rate a central bank sets, which influences other interest rates in the economy.Read more about Policy rate and what markets expect it to be soon.
  • The long end (ten years and more) reflects expected short rates over many years, expected inflation and growth, and an extra amount for the uncertainty of lending for a long time. That extra amount is the term premiumTerm premiumThe extra yield investors ask for lending over longer periods rather than rolling over short-term loans.Read more about Term premium.

Example

Two-year bonds yield 3.0% and ten-year bonds yield 3.5% (example). The 10-year minus 2-year spread is 3.5 − 3.0 = 0.5 percentage points, or +50 basis pointsBasis pointOne hundredth of a percentage point, so 25 basis points equal 0.25 percentage points.Read more about Basis point (hundredths of a percentage point). A positive spread means a normal, upward slope. If the two-year yield rose to 3.8% with the ten-year unchanged, the spread would be −30 basis points: an inverted curve.

Why the long end usually sits higher

Lending for thirty years carries more uncertainty than lending for three months. Inflation could change, interest rates could change, and the lender cannot use the money for anything else in the meantime. Lenders usually ask for some extra yield to accept that. This is why, in ordinary times, the curve slopes upwards. When the curve is flat or inverted, the extra yield is small, or markets expect short-term rates to fall enough to outweigh it.

The inversion caveat

An inverted curve gets attention because, in the United States and several other countries, it has come before many past recessions. Research at the Federal Reserve Bank of New York uses the gap between 10-year and 3-month yields to estimate the chance of a recessionRecessionA broad decline in economic activity lasting more than a few months, dated differently by different countries.Read more about Recession about a year ahead (New York Fed). That is a probability, not a forecast date. The time between an inversion and a recession has varied a lot, and an inversion does not cause a recession by itself.

Detailed version

Construction

Published curves are fitted, not read off individual bonds. Statistics teams estimate a smooth function (for example Nelson–Siegel–Svensson) from the prices of many bonds, then report par yields, zero-coupon (spot) yields and instantaneous forward rates at standard maturities. The ECB publishes daily euro-area curves of these kinds, for AAA-rated central government bonds and for all euro-area central government bonds (ECB). Analysts comparing countries use each central bank’s or treasury’s own curves, checking that the yield type (par, spot) and the day count match.

Expectations and term premium

The pure expectations hypothesis says a long yield equals the average of expected future short rates. For two one-year periods:

(1 + y₂)² = (1 + y₁) × (1 + E[y₁ next year])

If the one-year rate is 3% and the expected one-year rate a year from now is 4% (example), the two-year yield is √(1.03 × 1.04) − 1 ≈ 3.50%. In practice, long yields also include a term premium: extra compensation for the risk that rates, inflation or supply differ from expectations. The premium is not observed; it is estimated with models, and estimates differ. It can be negative, for example when investors value long bonds as a hedge or when central bank purchases of long bonds reduce the supply available to other buyers (Federal Reserve).

Breakeven inflation

Where governments issue inflation-linked bonds, the difference between a nominal yield and a real (inflation-linked) yield of the same maturity is breakeven inflation:

breakeven ≈ nominal yield − real yield

With a 10-year nominal yield of 3.5% and a 10-year real yield of 1.2% (example), breakeven is about 2.3%. It is a market-based gauge of expected inflation but also contains an inflation-risk premium and a liquidity premium for the less-traded linked bonds, so it is not a pure expectation.

Slope measures

Spread Common use
10y − 2y Market convention in daily commentary
10y − 3m Used in the New York Fed’s recession-probability model (New York Fed)
Near-term forward spread Expected change in short rates over the next 18 months, derived from forward rates

Curve movements

Analysts describe changes as parallel shifts, steepening or flattening, and qualify them by which end moves: for example, a steepening in which short yields fall more than long yields. Strategies built on these moves exist in bond portfolio management; they are described here only as vocabulary.

Why people disagree

Economists differ on how much weight an inversion deserves today. One view holds that the slope remains informative because it reflects expected policy-rate cuts, which tend to come when growth slows. Another view holds that central bank bond purchases and a low or negative term premium have pushed long yields down for reasons unrelated to growth expectations, so the slope signals less than it did in earlier decades. Both sides draw on the same data and differ mainly on how to estimate the term premium.
