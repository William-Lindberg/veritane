Labour Market Indicators in Depth

Unemployment, participation, employment rates, vacancies, wages and hours: reading the whole set rather than one number.

Professional·5 min (Simple) · 10 min (Detailed)·Updated 6 November 2026

Read first: Disoccupazione e mercato del lavoro

Key points

  • No single number describes the labour market; the unemployment rate needs participation, employment, vacancies, wages and hours beside it.
  • Household surveys and employer payroll or registry data measure different things, and both are revised.
  • Participation and prime-age employment rates avoid the definitional pitfalls of the unemployment rate.
  • The ratio of vacancies to unemployed people, and how often workers quit, signal how tight the market is.
  • Average wage measures change when the mix of jobs changes; underemployment and informal work matter especially outside high-income countries.

Simple: plain words. Detailed: exact figures, codes and methods.

In short: the unemployment rate is one gauge on a dashboard. Read alone, it can move in the “wrong” direction for reasons that the other gauges explain.

Simple version

Three groups of adults

Labour statistics sort working-age adults into three groups:

  • Employed: did paid work in the survey week, even a few hours.
  • Unemployed: had no work, were looking for work and could start soon.
  • Inactive (outside the labour force): not working and not looking. This includes many students, carers, retired people and people too ill to work.

The employed plus the unemployed make up the labour forceLabour forceEveryone who is working or actively looking for work.Read more about Labour force.

Three rates from the same people

Example

A town has 100 working-age adults (example): 60 employed, 4 unemployed and 36 inactive.

  • Unemployment rateUnemployment rateThe share of the labour force without work but available and looking for it.Read more about Unemployment rate = unemployed ÷ labour force = 4 ÷ 64 = 6.25%.
  • Participation rateParticipation rateThe share of the working-age population that is working or looking for work.Read more about Participation rate = labour force ÷ adults = 64 ÷ 100 = 64%.
  • Employment rate = employed ÷ adults = 60 ÷ 100 = 60%.

Now 2 of the unemployed stop looking for work. Nothing else changes.

  • Unemployment rate: 2 ÷ 62 = 3.23%. It fell by about 3 percentage points.
  • Participation: 62%.
  • Employment rate: still 60%.

The unemployment rate fell, but no one found a job. The employment rate shows that.

Wages and hours

Average pay can rise even when nobody gets a pay rise. If lower-paid jobs disappear, the average of the jobs that remain goes up. That is why statistics offices also publish measures that follow the same workers, or hold the mix of jobs fixed. Hours matter too. A worker who wants full-time work but can only find part-time work counts as employed, yet has less work than they want.

Two kinds of source

Some numbers come from surveys of households (asking people what they did). Others come from employers, payrolls or tax and social-security records (counting jobs). A person with two jobs counts once in a household survey and twice in a payroll count. The two can move differently for months, and both are revised.

Varies by country

  • United Kingdom: headline labour force survey figures cover rolling three-month periods (ONS).
  • United States: the monthly jobs report presents a household survey and an employer (payroll) survey side by side.
  • Many countries also publish registered unemployment from benefit or employment-office records, using national definitions that differ from the survey measure.

Detailed version

Definitions and comparability

The international definition of unemployment comes from the International Labour Organization (ILO): people without work during the reference week, available to start within a short period, and who have actively sought work in a recent period. Eurostat applies it with a four-week search period and availability within two weeks (Eurostat). Activity (participation) and employment rates use the working-age population as the denominator (Eurostat; Eurostat). Registered unemployment (people claiming benefits or registered with an employment office) follows national rules and is not comparable across countries. The World Bank publishes modelled ILO estimates designed for cross-country comparison (World Bank).

Survey design and sampling error

Labour force surveys sample households, so estimates carry sampling error. Monthly or rolling-quarter changes in the unemployment rate are often within the confidence interval, which statistics offices publish (ONS). Falling response rates raise non-sampling error as well. Population estimates used to weight the survey are themselves revised, which can shift levels.

Payroll and establishment data

Employer surveys and administrative records (payroll tax, social security) count jobs, not people, and have larger samples. They face a different problem: new firms enter and others close faster than the sample frame updates. In the United States, the payroll survey uses a statistical “birth-death” model to estimate this, and the annual benchmark revision against tax records can be sizeable.

Tightness: vacancies and the Beveridge curve

The job vacancy rate is vacancies ÷ (occupied posts + vacancies) (Eurostat). Plotting the vacancy rate against the unemployment rate gives the Beveridge curve. Movements along the curve reflect the business cycle; shifts outward suggest that matching workers to jobs has become less efficient (skill or location mismatch), although that interpretation is debated. The vacancies-to-unemployed ratio and the quits rate are common summary measures of tightness.

Wages and composition

Average earnings change when the mix of jobs changes, even if no one’s pay changes.

before: 5 workers at ¤20/h, 5 at ¤40/h  → average ¤30.00
after:  2 of the ¤20/h jobs are lost   → (3×20 + 5×40) / 8 = ¤32.50  (+8.3%)

This composition effect was large in many countries in 2020, when job losses were concentrated in lower-paid work. Composition-adjusted measures (fixed-weight indices, median wage growth of the same individuals over time, or wage regressions with worker characteristics) separate the price of labour from changes in who is employed.

Beyond high-income countries

Where informal work is widespread, the unemployment rate can be low simply because few people can afford to be without any work. Time-related underemployment, informal employment shares and working-poverty rates then say more about the labour market than unemployment does. ILO statistical standards include measures of labour underutilisation designed for this purpose.
