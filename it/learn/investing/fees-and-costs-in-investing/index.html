Fees and Costs

The costs of investing, where they appear and how their effect builds up over decades.

Intermediate·5 min (Simple) · 9 min (Detailed)·Updated 6 November 2026

Read first: La crescita composta e il tempo

Key points

  • Ongoing charges, platform fees, trading costs, spreads, entry and exit fees and taxes all reduce what is left to grow.
  • In an example at 6% a year over 30 years, a fee of 1 percentage point reduces the final sum by about a quarter.
  • Fees are known in advance; returns are not.
  • Fee disclosure formats vary by country: ongoing charges figure, expense ratio, total costs in a key information document.
  • Two questions show most costs: how much, and who pays whom?

Simple: plain words. Detailed: exact figures, codes and methods.

In short: every investment has costs, and they are taken every year whether returns are good or bad. Because they reduce the amount that compounds, a small yearly fee can add up to a large share of the final sum.

Simple version

The fee-drag picture

Suppose ¤10,000 (example) grows at 6% a year before fees, for 30 years.

Yearly fee Growth after fees Value after 30 years
0% 6% ¤57,435
1% 5% ¤43,219

The 1% fee costs about ¤14,215, which is about a quarter of what the money would have grown to with no fee. The fee is taken every year from a growing balance, so its effect builds over time in the same way returns do. This effect is called fee dragFee dragThe reduction in long-term returns caused by ongoing charges.Read more about Fee drag.

Investment Fee ImpactSee how much two yearly investment fees, for example 0.1 % and 1 %, take from the final balance over many years.

The same idea applies to regular saving. ¤200 a month for 30 years grows to about ¤200,903 at 6% and about ¤166,452 at 5% (examples). The difference, about ¤34,451, is almost half of the ¤72,000 paid in.

Where fees appear

  • Ongoing fund charge: a yearly percentage taken by the fund for running it. It is taken inside the fund, so you do not see a bill.
  • Platform or account fee: what the service holding your investments charges, as a percentage or a flat amount.
  • Advice fee: if an adviser is involved, paid directly or through the product.
  • Trading costs: commissions when you buy or sell, and costs inside the fund when it trades.
  • The spread: the gap between the price to buy and the price to sell. Buying at ¤100.10 and selling at ¤99.90 costs ¤0.20 per unit even if the price has not moved.
  • Entry and exit fees: charged by some products when you put money in or take it out.
  • Taxes: on dividends, interest or gains, depending on the country and the account.

Certain costs, uncertain returns

Nobody knows next year’s return. The fee is known in advance and is taken in good years and bad. That is why fees are one of the few parts of investing that can be compared with certainty before buying.

A fee in percent can also be turned into ¤. A 0.5% yearly charge on ¤20,000 is ¤100 in the first year (example). As the balance grows, the same percentage takes more ¤ each year, even though the rate has not changed.

Questions that show the costs

  1. What is the total yearly cost, as a percentage and in ¤ on my amount?
  2. Are there costs to buy, to sell or to switch?
  3. Who pays whom? Is anyone receiving a commission from the product?
  4. Are there performance fees, and how are they calculated?

Detailed version

Fee-drag arithmetic

A yearly fee f charged on the balance changes growth from (1 + r) to roughly (1 + r)(1 − f), often approximated as (1 + r − f). The share of the no-fee outcome lost after n years is:

1 − ((1 + r − f) / (1 + r))^n
r = 6%, f = 1%, n = 30:  1 − (1.05 / 1.06)^30 ≈ 24.8%

The multiplicative version gives ¤42,485 instead of ¤43,219 in the example; the difference depends on how and when the fee is deducted. The loss share rises with both f and n, and it does not depend on the amount invested.

Cost disclosure regimes

Disclosure formats differ:

  • The expense ratio (United States) is a fund’s yearly operating costs as a percentage of assets, stated in the prospectus fee table (Investor.gov).
  • The ongoing charges figure (OCF) is used for many funds in Europe and the United Kingdom.
  • The EU’s key information document for packaged retail products shows total costs in money over several holding periods, and their effect on return each year (Regulation (EU) No 1286/2014).

These measures exclude different items. Fund trading costs, platform fees and advice fees can sit outside the headline figure.

Commission and fee-only advice

Under a commission model, an adviser or distributor is paid by the product provider, often from the product’s charges. Under a fee-only model, the client pays the adviser directly and the adviser receives nothing from providers. Regulators have examined the conflict of interest in commission models: some countries have restricted commissions on retail investment advice, while others require disclosure. The price and the incentive both sit in the “who pays whom” question.

Spreads and market impact

The bid-ask spread is the difference between the price at which a market maker buys and sells. A round trip costs about one full spread. In liquid markets it is small; in small companies, some bonds and stressed markets it is wider. Large orders also move prices (market impact), which affects funds that trade often.

Tax drag

Taxes on dividends, interest and realised gains reduce the amount compounding each year. Turnover realises gains sooner, which brings tax forward. Tax-advantaged accounts reduce or defer this drag.

Active and passive costs

Sharpe’s “arithmetic of active management” shows that, before costs, the average actively managed dollar must earn the market return, because active and passive investors together hold the market; after costs, the average active dollar must earn less than the average passive dollar, since active management costs more (Sharpe, 1991). French estimated the total yearly cost of active investing in the US stock market and compared it with passive investing (French, 2008). The argument concerns averages; it does not say any one manager will under- or outperform.

Common misconceptions

  • “1% is small.” It is small for one year, and large over 30.
  • “A higher fee buys a higher return.” Fees are certain; any extra return is not.

Varies by country

Fee disclosure differs. In the United States, funds publish an expense ratio in a standard fee table in the prospectus. In the European Union, packaged retail investment products come with a key information document that shows total costs in money and their yearly effect on return. In the United Kingdom, an “ongoing charges figure” is widely used for funds. The European Securities and Markets Authority publishes guidance for investors on costs and fees (ESMA).

Why people disagree

Active versus passive investing is a long-running debate. Supporters of passive investing point to the cost arithmetic: after fees, the average active investor must trail the average passive one. Supporters of active management argue that some managers have skill, that active investors set the prices that passive funds rely on, and that some markets are less efficient than others. Researchers disagree about how many managers show persistent skill after costs and how to identify them in advance.
