Turning Savings Into Income

The ways a retirement pot becomes a regular income, and the uncertainties attached to each.

Intermediate·6 min (Simple) · 11 min (Detailed)·Updated 6 November 2026

Read first: How Much Is Enough?

Key points

  • An annuity exchanges a lump sum for a guaranteed income; drawdown keeps the pot invested and takes money out over time.
  • Longevity risk is the chance of living longer than the money lasts.
  • Sequence risk means that poor returns in the first years of withdrawals do more harm than the same returns later.
  • Withdrawal rates are rules of thumb with wide uncertainty, not guarantees.
  • Public pensions and other guaranteed income reduce how much has to come from savings.

Simple: plain words. Detailed: exact figures, codes and methods.

In short: a pot of savings is not yet an income. There are two basic ways to turn it into one, buying a guaranteed income or taking money out step by step, and many people combine them.

Simple version

Living longer than the money

Nobody knows how long they will live. A person retiring at 65 could live to 70 or to 100. The chance of outliving savings is called longevity risk. An annuity for life removes it, because the insurer keeps paying. With drawdown, the person carries it.

The “bad first years” problem

With drawdown, the order of good and bad years matters. Losses early on, while money is also being taken out, leave a smaller pot to recover. This is called sequence riskSequence riskThe danger that poor returns early in retirement permanently reduce how long savings last.Read more about Sequence risk.

Example

Two retirees each start with ¤100,000 and take out ¤6,000 at the start of every year (example). Over ten years they get exactly the same yearly returns, only in the opposite order:

  • Retiree A: −15 %, −10 %, then +5 %, +8 %, +8 %, +8 %, +8 %, +10 %, +10 %, +15 %.
  • Retiree B: the same returns reversed, so the two bad years come last.

Without any withdrawals, both pots would end at about ¤152,100. With the withdrawals, A ends with about ¤56,800 and B with about ¤89,800. Same returns, same withdrawals, very different results.

How much can be taken out each year?

The withdrawal rateWithdrawal rateThe share of a retirement pot taken out as income each year.Read more about Withdrawal rate is the share of the pot taken as income each year. A higher rate gives more income now and raises the chance that the money runs out. Rules of thumb exist, but they come from particular countries, periods and assumptions. They are starting points for discussion, not guarantees.

Mixing approaches

Some people combine the two. For example, part of the pot buys an annuity to cover essential bills, and the rest stays in drawdown for flexible spending. A public pension already acts like an annuity for many people: it pays for life and is often adjusted for prices.

Varies by country

  • United Kingdom: people with a defined contribution pension can choose between buying an annuity, drawdown, taking lump sums, or a mix.
  • Sweden: the public income pension is paid as a lifelong income, so it works like an annuity provided by the state.
  • United States: annuities are regulated by state insurance departments; the national association of insurance commissioners publishes consumer guides.

Detailed version

How annuities are priced

An insurer prices a lifetime annuity as the expected present value of payments, using interest rates and a mortality table, plus expenses and a margin:

price ≈ Σ (payment × probability of being alive at time t ÷ (1 + i)^t) + loadings

Mortality credits are what makes the guarantee possible: the money of annuitants who die earlier helps pay those who live longer. That pooling lets a lifetime annuity pay more per year than a person could safely draw alone to a very old age. Annuity rates rise with interest rates and with age at purchase, and fall when life expectancy assumptions rise. Inflation-linked annuities start lower than level ones because their payments rise later.

Withdrawal-rate research

Studies of “safe” withdrawal rates test a fixed real withdrawal against historical or simulated return paths and report how often a pot lasts 30 years. The results depend heavily on the asset mix, fees, the country and period studied, and the horizon. Figures that come out of one country’s historical data do not transfer automatically to another country or to future returns.

Sequence risk in numbers

In the example above the arithmetic mean return is 4.7 % and the compound (geometric) return is about 4.3 % a year in both orders. Without withdrawals the order does not matter: both pots end at 152,065. With withdrawals the result is path-dependent: A ends at 56,810 and B at 89,831. The years just before and just after retirement carry the largest exposure.

Dynamic withdrawal rules

Alternatives to a fixed real amount include:

  • a fixed percentage of the current pot (income varies, and in theory the pot does not reach zero);
  • guardrails that cut or raise spending when the withdrawal rate moves outside a band;
  • life-expectancy-based rules that divide the pot by remaining years.

Each trades income stability against the chance of running out.

Inflation and tax

Level annuities lose purchasing power as prices rise. Drawdown leaves inflation exposure with the investor. Tax treatment of annuity payments, drawdown withdrawals and lump sums differs between countries and between the patterns described in the previous lesson.

Common misconceptions

  • “An annuity loses money if I die early.” It is insurance against living long; the cost of early death is the price of that cover.
  • “Average return decides the outcome.” With withdrawals, the order of returns matters as well.

Why people disagree

Many economists find that fewer people buy lifetime annuities than simple models predict (the “annuity puzzle”). One set of explanations treats this as rational: public pensions already provide a lifelong income, people value flexibility and bequests, and annuity prices include loadings. Another set points to behavioural factors, such as the framing of an annuity as a gamble on lifespan. Views also differ on fixed versus flexible withdrawal rules: some value predictable income, others value the lower chance of depletion that flexible rules give.
