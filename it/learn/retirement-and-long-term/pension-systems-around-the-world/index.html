How Pension Systems Work

The common building blocks of public, workplace and private pensions, and how they vary between countries.

Intermediate·6 min (Simple) · 11 min (Detailed)·Updated 6 November 2026

Read first: Why Retirement Planning Exists

Key points

  • Public pensions are usually paid for by today's contributions or taxes rather than by a saved pot.
  • Workplace schemes are either defined benefit (a promised income) or defined contribution (a pot whose size depends on contributions and returns).
  • Several countries enrol workers into a workplace pension automatically, with the right to opt out.
  • Tax rules typically favour long-term retirement saving, but the details differ by country.
  • Vesting, transfer and employer-matching rules decide what happens to a pension when someone changes jobs or countries.

Simple: plain words. Detailed: exact figures, codes and methods.

In short: almost every pension system is built from the same few parts. Knowing the parts makes any country’s system easier to read.

Simple version

Public pensions

A public pension is paid by the state. In many countries, the money paid to today’s pensioners comes from today’s workers, through contributions or taxes. This is called a pay-as-you-go pensionPay-as-you-go pensionA public pension funded by today's workers' contributions rather than by a saved pot.Read more about Pay-as-you-go pension. Nobody has a personal pot; the system relies on a promise between generations.

The amount usually depends on how many years a person worked, paid contributions or lived in the country. Some public pensions pay the same flat amount to everyone who qualifies. Others are linked to past earnings.

Workplace pensions: two main types

Defined benefit (DB) Defined contribution (DC)
What is promised An income, set by a formula Nothing fixed; you get the pot
Typical formula Years of work × a fraction × salary Contributions + investment growth − fees
Who carries the investment risk Mainly the employer or scheme Mainly the member
What you see each year Your pension so far, as income Your pot value

A defined benefit pensionDefined benefit pensionA pension that promises a set income, usually based on salary and years of service.Read more about Defined benefit pension promises an income. A defined contribution pensionDefined contribution pensionA pension in which contributions build a pot whose final value depends on investment returns.Read more about Defined contribution pension builds a pot, and the final size depends on how much goes in and how investments perform.

Example

DB (example): a scheme pays 1/60 of final salary for each year of work. After 30 years on a final salary of ¤30,000, the pension is 30 × 1/60 × ¤30,000 = ¤15,000 a year.

DC (example): the worker and employer together pay ¤2,400 a year for 30 years. That is ¤72,000 paid in. If the pot grows by 4 % a year after fees, it reaches about ¤134,600. At 2 % growth it would be smaller; at 6 % it would be larger.

Auto-enrolment

Many people put off joining a pension. To change the starting point, some countries use auto-enrolmentAuto-enrolmentA system in which workers are placed into a pension scheme by default unless they choose to opt out.Read more about Auto-enrolment: workers are placed in a workplace scheme by default and can choose to leave. The UK and New Zealand are two examples. The worker stays free to opt out.

Employer money and vesting

Some employers add money to a worker’s pension, sometimes as a “match” (for example, ¤1 for every ¤1 the worker pays in, up to a limit). VestingVestingThe point at which employer pension contributions, or share awards, become fully the employee's.Read more about Vesting rules say when that employer money fully belongs to the worker. Under some schemes, leaving a job early means losing part of the employer’s share.

Changing jobs or countries

When someone changes jobs, a DC pot usually stays theirs. It can often be left where it is or moved to a new scheme. A DB pension is usually kept as a promise to pay later, and some schemes allow a transfer.

Moving countries is more complicated. Within the European Union, each country where a person was insured pays a pension for that period. Some other countries have agreements with each other; some have none.

Varies by country

  • United Kingdom: employers enrol eligible workers in a workplace pension automatically and contribute to it.
  • New Zealand: KiwiSaver is a voluntary scheme with automatic enrolment for many new employees.
  • Sweden: the public pension is built from individual notional accounts plus a funded premium pension.

Detailed version

Funding a defined benefit promise

A DB scheme holds assets against a liability: the present value of all promised pensions. Actuaries estimate the liability from assumptions about salary growth, inflation, mortality and a discount rate.

funding ratio = scheme assets ÷ present value of promised benefits
deficit       = present value of promised benefits − scheme assets

A lower discount rate raises the measured liability, so funding ratios move with interest rates even when nothing else changes. When a sponsoring employer becomes insolvent, some countries run protection funds that pay part or all of the promised pension, such as the UK Pension Protection Fund (PPF) and the US Pension Benefit Guaranty Corporation (PBGC).

Risk transfer from DB to DC

In a DB scheme the sponsor carries investment, inflation and longevity risk. In a DC scheme the member carries them. Hybrid designs share them: cash-balance plans, collective DC and DB schemes with conditional indexation. Employers also transfer DB liabilities to insurers through buy-ins and buy-outs.

Default funds and lifecycle glide paths

Most DC members do not choose a fund, so the default fund carries most of the money. A common default is a lifecycle (target-date) design: a higher share of shares (equities) when retirement is far away, moving gradually towards bonds and cash as it approaches. The glide path describes that schedule. Its purpose is to reduce the chance of a large fall in value just before the pot is used.

Notional defined contribution (NDC)

An NDC system records contributions in individual accounts and credits them with a notional return (often linked to wage or contribution growth), but the money itself pays current pensions. At retirement the notional balance is converted to an income using life expectancy. Sweden’s income pension is a well-known example (Pensionsmyndigheten).

Vesting and portability

Vesting schedules may be cliff (nothing until a date, then 100 %) or graded (a rising share each year). The US Internal Revenue Service describes the permitted schedules for US workplace plans (IRS).

Cross-border coordination

EU rules coordinate social security: each country where a person was insured calculates and pays a pension for its period, and insurance periods in other member states can count towards minimum qualifying periods (Your Europe). Outside the EU, bilateral social-security agreements play a similar role between specific pairs of countries.

Why people disagree

Pay-as-you-go versus funding is a long-running debate. Supporters of more funding argue that saved assets make pensions less dependent on the ratio of workers to pensioners and can add to national saving. Supporters of pay-as-you-go social insurance argue that it pools longevity and investment risk across generations, avoids the costs of individual accounts, and can protect people with low lifetime earnings. A further argument is that both systems ultimately rely on the output of future workers. The World Bank’s work on multi-pillar systems (World Bank) and the ILO’s social-security standards (ILO) set out different emphases. Countries mix the two.
