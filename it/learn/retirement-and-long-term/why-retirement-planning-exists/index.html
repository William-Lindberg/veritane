Why Retirement Planning Exists

People can live for decades without income from work; something has to pay for those years.

Intermediate·5 min (Simple) · 9 min (Detailed)·Updated 6 November 2026

Read first: La crescita composta e il tempo

Key points

  • Longer lives mean longer retirements, and those years need an income that does not come from work.
  • Retirement income usually comes from three sources, often called pillars: public pensions, workplace schemes and personal savings.
  • Starting early lets compounding do more of the work; starting later means larger contributions for the same result.
  • At 2–3 % inflation a year, prices roughly double over 30 years, so a fixed income buys about half as much by the end.
  • Plans change over a working life; the regular habit of saving matters more than any single forecast.

Simple: plain words. Detailed: exact figures, codes and methods.

In short: many people stop working long before they stop needing money. Retirement planning is the name for working out how those years will be paid for.

Simple version

A long time without a pay cheque

Think about a person who starts work at 22 and stops at 67. That is 45 years of earning. If that person lives to 90, there are 23 more years with bills to pay and no wage coming in. For some people the gap is shorter, for others it is longer. Nobody knows their own number in advance.

Food, rent, energy and health costs do not stop at retirement. So the money for those years has to come from somewhere.

Where the money comes from: the three pillars

Most countries combine three sources. People often call them pillars:

Pillar What it is Who arranges it
Public pension A regular payment from the state, often based on years of work or residence The government
Workplace pension A scheme linked to a job, often with money from the employer as well as the worker The employer
Personal savings Your own savings and investments, in any form You

A pensionPensionRegular income in retirement from the state, an employer scheme or personal savings.Read more about Pension is simply a regular income in retirement. Each pillar is a different way of building one. How large each pillar is varies a great deal between countries.

What do 25 years of retirement cost?

Example

A retired person spends ¤20,000 a year (example) in today’s prices. Over 25 years that adds up to 25 × ¤20,000 = ¤500,000 in today’s money.

Some of that comes from a public pension. The rest comes from workplace schemes and savings. Investment growth and inflation change the exact figure, but the order of size explains why planning starts decades ahead.

Why starting early matters

Money saved early has more years to grow. Growth that builds on earlier growth is called compound interestCompound interestInterest earned on both the original amount and the interest already added.Read more about Compound interest. The example below assumes a steady growth of 5 % a year, which is an illustration, not a forecast.

Example

  • Person A saves ¤100 a month for 40 years. A pays in ¤48,000. At 5 % a year this grows to about ¤152,600.
  • Person B saves ¤200 a month for 20 years. B also pays in ¤48,000. At 5 % a year this grows to about ¤82,200.

Both paid in the same amount. A ends with more because A’s money had twice as long to grow. Someone who starts later can still reach a target, but the monthly amount has to be larger.

Inflation over a long retirement

InflationInflationA general rise in prices over time, which reduces what money buys.Read more about Inflation is a general rise in prices over time. It sounds small year by year, but it adds up. At 2–3 % a year, prices roughly double over 30 years. A fixed income of ¤1,000 a month would then buy about what ¤500 buys today. This is why many pensions are adjusted each year, and why retirement plans are usually written in “today’s money”.

Plans change, habits stay

Jobs change, families change and prices change. A plan made at 25 will look different at 45. Many people find that a regular, automatic contribution matters more than a perfect forecast, because it keeps going while the details are revised.

Detailed version

Demography: why the question has grown

The share of older people in the population is rising in most regions as life expectancy rises and birth rates fall. The United Nations publishes country projections and dependency ratios in its World Population Prospects (UN DESA). A common measure is the old-age dependency ratio:

old-age dependency ratio = population aged 65+ ÷ population aged 20–64 (or 15–64)

A ratio of 30 means 30 people of pension age for every 100 people of working age (example). The age bands differ between publishers, so comparisons use one source consistently.

Pay-as-you-go and funded systems

  • Pay-as-you-go (PAYG): today’s contributions or taxes pay today’s pensions. A pay-as-you-go pensionPay-as-you-go pensionA public pension funded by today's workers' contributions rather than by a saved pot.Read more about Pay-as-you-go pension depends on the ratio of contributors to pensioners and on wage growth.
  • Funded: contributions are invested in a fund that later pays the pension. The result depends on investment returns, fees and the price of turning the fund into income.

Most countries mix both. The World Bank describes multi-pillar designs that combine a basic public layer with mandatory and voluntary funded layers (World Bank). The International Labour Organization sets out social-security principles for old-age income within its work on social protection (ILO).

Replacement rates

The replacement rateReplacement rateRetirement income as a share of income before retirement.Read more about Replacement rate is retirement income as a share of earlier earnings. Gross rates compare pensions before tax with earnings before tax; net rates compare amounts after taxes and contributions. Published rates are usually for a stylised worker with a full career, so individual results with career breaks, part-time work or migration can differ widely.

Longevity risk

Longevity risk is the chance of living longer than the money lasts. Period life expectancy (based on today’s death rates) is shorter than cohort life expectancy (which allows for expected improvements), and the spread around either figure is wide. The UK Office for National Statistics, for example, publishes the probability of reaching ages such as 90 or 100 by sex and year of birth (ONS). Planning to the average age leaves a large share of people outliving the plan, which is why many projections use an age with a low probability of survival.

Inflation arithmetic

The purchasing power of a fixed amount after n years at inflation i:

real value = nominal ÷ (1 + i)^n
1 ÷ 1.02^30 ≈ 0.55     1 ÷ 1.025^30 ≈ 0.48     1 ÷ 1.03^30 ≈ 0.41

Price-indexed pensions (linked to a price index) keep purchasing power roughly constant; wage-indexed pensions keep pace with earnings; fixed nominal pensions lose purchasing power every year that prices rise.

Why people disagree

How much of retirement income the state provides, and how much individuals provide, is a political choice. One view emphasises funded individual accounts: they make the link between contributions and benefits visible and are less exposed to the ratio of workers to pensioners. Another view emphasises public pay-as-you-go social insurance: it shares longevity and investment risk across the whole population and can redistribute towards people with low lifetime earnings. Both views are represented in the work of international bodies such as the World Bank and the ILO (sources above). Countries combine the two in very different proportions.
