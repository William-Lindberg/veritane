Credit Cards Explained

Revolving credit, grace periods, minimum payments and the arithmetic of carrying a balance from month to month.

Everyday·6 min (Simple) · 10 min (Detailed)·Updated 6 November 2026

Read first: What Credit Is

Key points

  • A credit card is a reusable loan up to a limit: you borrow when you pay with it, and the limit frees up again as you repay.
  • On most cards, paying the full statement balance by the due date means no interest is charged on purchases.
  • A minimum payment set as a small share of the balance shrinks as the balance shrinks, so paying only the minimum stretches repayment over many years.
  • Interest on a balance that is carried over is usually worked out daily on what is owed.
  • Rewards and "free" features are paid for by fees and interest somewhere in the system.

Simple: plain words. Detailed: exact figures, codes and methods.

In short: a credit card lets you borrow up to a limit, again and again. If you repay the whole statement on time it usually costs no interest; if you carry a balance, interest is added every day.

Simple version

A reusable loan

A credit card is a form of revolving creditRevolving creditCredit that can be borrowed, repaid and borrowed again up to a limit, such as a credit card.Read more about Revolving credit. The card issuer sets a credit limitCredit limitThe maximum amount that can be borrowed on a card or line of credit.Read more about Credit limit, for example ¤2,000 (example). Each purchase uses part of that limit. Each repayment frees it up again.

A debit card is different. It spends money already in your account. A credit card spends the issuer’s money, which you owe back.

How a statement works

Once a month, the card issuer sends a statement. It shows:

  • every purchase and payment in the period;
  • the statement balance (what you owe at the end of the period);
  • the minimum payment (the smallest amount you have to pay to avoid a late fee);
  • the due date.

The grace period

On most cards, if you pay the full statement balance by the due date, you pay no interest on purchases. The time between a purchase and the due date is called the grace periodGrace periodThe time between a credit card statement and its due date during which paying the full balance avoids interest.Read more about Grace period. In effect, it is a short free loan.

If you pay less than the full balance, interest is charged on what is left, and on many cards the grace period on new purchases is lost until the balance is cleared.

The minimum payment

The minimum paymentMinimum paymentThe smallest amount a card issuer accepts each month, which clears the balance slowly.Read more about Minimum payment is often a small percentage of the balance, or the interest plus a small percentage, with a floor such as ¤25. Because it is a share of the balance, it gets smaller as the balance falls. That is why paying only the minimum takes a long time.

Example

A card balance of ¤1,000 (example), at 22% a year, with no new spending. The minimum is the month’s interest plus 1% of the balance, or ¤25, whichever is higher.

Paying each month Time to clear Total interest
The minimum (starts at about ¤28.52) 70 months (almost 6 years) about ¤762
A fixed ¤50 26 months about ¤257
A fixed ¤100 12 months about ¤115

The same debt costs very different amounts depending on the payment.

The minimum-payments tool runs this for your own numbers.

Costly features to know about

  • Cash withdrawals (cash advances): often a fee, a higher rate, and interest from the day of the withdrawal, with no grace period.
  • Spending abroad: some cards add a foreign-transaction fee of a few per cent.
  • Introductory rates: a low or 0% rate for a set time. When it ends, the standard rate applies to whatever is still owed.
  • Late and over-limit fees: charged when a payment is late or the limit is passed.

Rewards are not free

Points, cashback and airport lounges cost money. They are funded by the fees shops pay on card payments, by annual card fees, and by interest from customers who carry a balance. A customer who pays in full each month and uses the rewards gets value from that system; for one who carries a balance, the interest can exceed the value of the rewards, and putting both in money shows which is larger.

Detailed version

Daily periodic rate and average daily balance

Most issuers convert the annual rate to a daily periodic rate (DPR) and apply it to the balance each day:

DPR = APR ÷ 365        (some issuers use 360)
interest for the cycle = average daily balance × DPR × days in cycle
average daily balance = Σ daily balances ÷ days in cycle

Example (illustrative): 22% APR, a 30-day cycle with ¤1,000 owed for 10 days and ¤1,500 for 20 days. ADB = (10 × 1,000 + 20 × 1,500) ÷ 30 = ¤1,333.33. Interest = 1,333.33 × 0.22/365 × 30 = ¤24.11. Because interest is often added to the balance monthly, the effective annual rate exceeds the quoted APR: (1 + 0.22/12)^12 − 1 ≈ 24.4%.

Minimum-payment arithmetic

With the “interest plus 1%” rule, the principal reduction in month t is about 1% of the balance, so the balance falls roughly geometrically and repayment stretches over years. With a “2% of balance” rule and a rate near 24%, the payment barely exceeds the monthly interest of 2%, and repayment can take decades. A fixed payment, by contrast, gives a fixed term like an instalment loan.

Payment allocation

Cards can carry several balances at different rates (purchases, cash advances, balance transfers, promotional rates). Allocation rules decide which is repaid first. In the United States, amounts above the minimum have to be applied to the highest-rate balance first (Regulation Z). Where no such rule applies, the contract decides.

Interchange economics

Each card payment generates an interchange fee paid by the merchant’s bank to the issuer (Bank of England). Issuer revenue therefore comes from three sources: interchange, cardholder fees and interest on revolving balances. Where interchange is capped, as in the EU at 0.3% for consumer credit cards (Regulation (EU) 2015/751), reward schemes tend to be smaller and annual fees more common.

Regulatory examples

  • United States: card statements show how long repayment takes at the minimum, and how much a payment that clears the balance in three years would be (Regulation Z).
  • United Kingdom: “persistent debt” rules require card issuers to contact customers who have paid more in interest, fees and charges than in principal over 18 months, and eventually to offer a way to repay faster (FCA).
  • European Union: credit-card agreements fall under consumer credit rules on information and annual percentage rates of charge (Directive 2008/48/EC).
  • Interest caps: some countries set a maximum interest rate on consumer credit, including cards; others do not.

Common misconceptions

  • “Paying the minimum avoids interest.” It avoids late fees; interest is still charged on the remainder.
  • “Carrying a small balance helps a credit score.” Scores generally reflect on-time payments and the share of the limit used, not interest paid.
  • “0% means free.” Fees, and the rate after the offer ends, still apply.

Varies by country

Card rules differ. In the United States, statements show the time to repay at the minimum, and extra payments go to the highest-rate balance first (Regulation Z). In the United Kingdom, issuers have to act when a customer has been in “persistent debt” for 18 months (FCA). In the European Union, interchange on consumer credit cards is capped at 0.3% of the payment (Regulation (EU) 2015/751). In some countries, charge cards that have to be repaid in full every month are common, and revolving credit cards are less so.

Why people disagree

Should credit card interest rates be capped? Supporters of caps argue that card rates are high compared with other credit, that many borrowers underestimate how long minimum payments take, and that caps protect people with the least bargaining power. Opponents argue that caps lead lenders to stop lending to higher-risk borrowers, add other fees, or push borrowers towards unregulated lenders. Countries have chosen differently: some cap rates, others rely on disclosure rules and conduct rules such as the US minimum-payment warning (Regulation Z) and the UK persistent-debt rules (FCA).
