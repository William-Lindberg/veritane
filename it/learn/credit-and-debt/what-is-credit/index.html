What Credit Is

Credit is using money now and paying it back later, usually with interest, at a price that reflects how sure the lender is of being repaid.

Everyday·5 min (Simple) · 9 min (Detailed)·Updated 6 November 2026

Read first: Gli interessi: il prezzo del tempo

Key points

  • Every credit product has three parts: the amount borrowed (the principal), the price (the interest rate and fees) and the repayment terms.
  • Lenders price risk: the less certain they are of being repaid, the more they charge, or the less they lend.
  • A credit history is the information lenders use to judge that risk, and how it is collected and scored varies by country.
  • Credit can widen a person's options or narrow them, depending on what it costs and what it is used for.
  • Having no credit history is not the same as having a poor one.

Simple version

Three parts of any loan

Every form of credit, from a credit card to a mortgage, has the same three parts:

  1. The principalPrincipalThe original amount borrowed or invested, before interest.Read more about Principal: the amount you borrow.
  2. The price: the interest rateInterest rateThe price of borrowing or the reward for lending, written as a percentage of the amount per period, usually per year.Read more about Interest rate, plus any fees.
  3. The terms: how long you have, how often you pay, and what happens if you pay late.

When you know these three things, you can work out what a loan costs.

A ¤1,000 example

Example

You borrow ¤1,000 (example) at 12% a year.

  • Repaid in one go after a year: you pay back ¤1,000 plus ¤120 interest, so ¤1,120.
  • Repaid in 12 equal monthly instalments: each payment is about ¤88.85. In total you pay about ¤1,066, so the interest is about ¤66.

The second option costs less in interest because you pay back part of the loan every month. Interest is charged only on what is still owed, and that amount gets smaller each month.

In the first month of the instalment loan, interest is 1% of ¤1,000, which is ¤10. The other ¤78.85 of the payment reduces the debt. Each month after that, a little less goes to interest and a little more to the debt. The loan calculator shows this month by month.

Two main shapes of credit

  • Instalment loansInstalment loanA loan repaid in fixed regular payments over a set term.Read more about Instalment loan: you borrow a fixed amount and pay it back in set payments over a set time. Car loans, personal loans and mortgages work like this.
  • Revolving creditRevolving creditCredit that can be borrowed, repaid and borrowed again up to a limit, such as a credit card.Read more about Revolving credit: you can borrow, repay and borrow again up to a limit. Credit cards and overdrafts work like this.

Loans can also be secured or unsecured. A secured loanSecured loanA loan backed by an asset that the lender can take if the borrower does not repay.Read more about Secured loan is backed by something the lender can take if you do not pay, such as a house or a car. An unsecured loanUnsecured loanA loan backed only by the borrower's promise to repay.Read more about Unsecured loan has no such backing, so lenders usually charge more for it.

Why rates differ from person to person

A lender cannot know for sure who will repay. It uses information about you, such as your income, your other debts and how you have handled credit before, to estimate the chance. The more certain it is, the lower the price it offers. The less certain it is, the more it charges, or it may say no.

This information is often called a credit history. The lesson on credit scores explains it.

No history is not bad history

Someone who has not borrowed before has no credit history. That is different from a history of missed payments. A lender may still be cautious, because it has less to go on. In many countries, people new to credit, or new to the country, are in this position.

Useful or costly

Credit is neither good nor bad in itself. It can spread the cost of something that lasts for years, such as a home or training for a job. It can bridge a short gap in income. It can also become expensive when the rate is high, the term is long, or new borrowing is used to repay old borrowing (Bank of England). The cost and the purpose decide which way it goes.

Detailed version

Risk-based pricing

A lender’s rate can be decomposed into components (illustrative):

loan rate ≈ funding cost + expected loss rate + operating cost + capital charge and margin

The expected loss on a loan is the standard building block used in bank regulation and credit risk management (BIS):

EL = PD × EAD × LGD
  • PD (probability of default): the chance the borrower stops paying within a year.
  • EAD (exposure at default): how much is owed at that point.
  • LGD (loss given default): the share of the exposure the lender does not recover.

Worked example (illustrative): PD 3%, EAD ¤10,000, LGD 60%. EL = 0.03 × 10,000 × 0.60 = ¤180, or 1.8% of the exposure. With funding at 4%, operating costs of 2% and a capital charge and margin of 1.5%, the indicative rate is 4 + 1.8 + 2 + 1.5 = 9.3%. Unexpected losses (the variation around EL) are what bank capital is held against.

Amortising payments

For a loan of P at monthly rate i over n months:

M = P × i ÷ (1 − (1 + i)^−n)

For ¤1,000 at 1% a month for 12 months, M = ¤88.85 and total interest = 12 × 88.85 − 1,000 = ¤66.19 (rounded per payment).

Who holds credit information

System Who runs it Typical content
Private credit bureau Private companies Positive and negative information from many lenders, used for consumer scores
Public credit registry Usually the central bank or supervisor Loans above a reporting threshold, mainly for supervision
No formal system — Lenders rely on their own customer data, documents and references

The World Bank tracks credit reporting systems and their coverage across countries (World Bank). In the euro area, AnaCredit collects loan-level data on credit to companies and other legal entities, rather than to households (ECB).

Thin files and alternative data

A thin file has too little history to score reliably. Lenders and bureaus in some markets use alternative data, such as rent, utility or phone payments, or bank-account transaction data shared with consent, to assess these applicants. This raises questions about accuracy, privacy and fairness that the credit-scores lesson covers.

Reading the aggregate data

Statistical releases separate revolving from non-revolving consumer credit; for example, the Federal Reserve’s G.19 release reports both for the United States (Federal Reserve). Growth in credit outstanding reflects both new lending and repayments, so it is read alongside rates and lending standards.

Common misconceptions

  • “Interest is charged on the original amount for the whole term.” On amortising loans it is charged on the falling balance.
  • “A refused application means bad credit.” It can reflect a thin file, affordability or the lender’s own policy.
  • “Secured credit is low-risk for the borrower.” It is lower risk for the lender; the borrower can lose the asset.

Varies by country

Credit information systems differ. In the United States and the United Kingdom, private credit bureaus hold detailed positive and negative information and produce consumer scores. In several European countries, the central bank also runs a public credit registry used by supervisors and lenders, alongside or instead of private bureaus (World Bank). In countries without wide credit reporting, lenders rely on their own records, documents and references, and some use other kinds of data, such as phone or utility payments.
