Fees and How to See Them

The common charges on bank accounts and cards, where they are written down, and how small fees add up.

Everyday·5 min (Simple) · 8 min (Detailed)·Updated 6 November 2026

Read first: Types of Accounts

Key points

  • The common charges are monthly account fees, overdraft charges, fees for using another bank's cash machine, foreign-transaction fees and fees for paper statements.
  • Every bank publishes a fee list (often called a tariff or fee schedule), and customers can ask for it.
  • Small fees that repeat every month add up over years, and the money spent on them cannot earn interest.
  • Many fees have a no-fee alternative, such as a different account, a different cash machine or a different way to pay.
  • Rules on overdraft charges and fee caps vary by country.

Simple: plain words. Detailed: exact figures, codes and methods.

In short: bank fees are prices, and they are written down. Reading the fee list turns a surprise charge into a known cost.

Simple version

The usual charges

Most account and card fees fall into a few groups. Not every bank charges all of them, and some charge none.

Fee When it is charged A common no-fee alternative
Monthly account fee Every month, sometimes waived above a minimum balance or a minimum monthly pay-in A basic or fee-free account
OverdraftOverdraftSpending more than an account holds, which a bank may allow in return for a fee or interest.Read more about Overdraft charges When the balance goes below zero A buffer in the account; a cheaper form of credit

Where fees are written down

Every bank has a fee list. It may be called a tariff, a fee schedule, a price list or a fee information document. It is usually on the bank’s website and in the account terms. You can also ask a branch or call centre for it.

When reading one, three questions help:

  1. What do I pay each month, whatever I do?
  2. What do I pay when something goes wrong (an overdraft, a bounced payment, a late card payment)?
  3. What do I pay when I travel or pay in another currency?

Small fees add up

A fee of ¤5 a month (example) looks small. Over a year it is ¤60. Over ten years it is ¤600.

Example

If that ¤5 a month had gone into savings at 3% a year instead, after ten years it would be about ¤699 (example). The extra ¤99 is the interest the money would have earned. This is why the cost of a regular fee grows with time.

Overdrafts: the most expensive surprise

An overdraft lets you spend more than you have. It is a loan, and it has a price. Some banks charge interest; some charge a flat fee per day or per month; some do both.

A flat fee can be very expensive for a small, short overdraft. Suppose a bank charges ¤15 (example) for an overdraft of ¤100 that lasts a week. That is 15% of the amount borrowed in seven days. Expressed as a yearly rate, it is far higher than most loans. The Pro layer shows the calculation.

Why some cards are “free”

Shops pay a fee each time a customer pays by card. Part of that fee goes to the bank that issued the card. This income helps pay for cards that charge the cardholder nothing, and for some reward schemes. The costs still exist; they are paid by shops and, through prices, by all customers.

Detailed version

Effective cost of an overdraft fee

A flat fee F for borrowing an amount B for d days can be expressed as an annual rate:

simple annual rate    = (F ÷ B) × (365 ÷ d)
compounded (effective) = (1 + F ÷ B)^(365 ÷ d) − 1

Example (illustrative): ¤15 on ¤100 for 7 days. Simple annual rate = 0.15 × 52.14 = 782%. The compounded figure is far larger still. By comparison, an overdraft priced at 40% a year on ¤200 for 10 days costs 200 × 0.40 × 10/365 = ¤2.19. Flat fees weigh most heavily on small, short overdrafts, which is why regulators in several countries have looked at how overdrafts are priced.

Fee regulation: three approaches

  • Transparency (EU): the Payment Accounts Directive requires a standard fee information document before an account is opened, a yearly statement of fees paid, and common terms for the most representative services, so that accounts can be compared (Directive 2014/92/EU).
  • Pricing structure (UK): the Financial Conduct Authority’s consumer credit rules, as changed in 2020, require overdrafts to be priced with a simple annual interest rate rather than fixed daily or monthly fees (FCA).
  • Caps on interchange (EU, US): the EU caps interchange on most consumer card payments at 0.2% of the value for debit cards and 0.3% for credit cards (Regulation (EU) 2015/751). In the United States, Regulation II caps debit interchange for large card issuers (Federal Reserve).

Interchange and the economics of “free” cards

In a typical card payment, the merchant pays its bank (the acquirer) a merchant service charge. The acquirer passes an interchange fee to the cardholder’s bank (the issuer) and a scheme fee to the card network (Bank of England). Interchange income lets issuers fund card features and rewards. Where interchange is capped, issuers tend to recover costs through other fees or by reducing rewards.

Reading a fee schedule like an analyst

  1. Separate fixed fees (monthly) from event fees (overdraft, bounced payment, foreign use).
  2. Estimate a year of your own events and price them: annual cost = 12 × monthly fee + Σ (events × fee).
  3. Express credit-like fees as annual rates, as above, so they sit on the same scale as an annual percentage rateAnnual percentage rate (APR)The yearly cost of borrowing as a percentage, usually including interest and most fees, with exact rules that vary by country.Read more about Annual percentage rate (APR).
  4. Note conditions that waive fees (minimum pay-in, minimum balance) and what happens if they are missed.

Common misconceptions

  • “Free banking costs nothing.” Costs move to interest margins, interchange or event fees.
  • “Overdraft fees are small.” In annual terms, flat fees on small balances can exceed most other credit.
  • “All cash machines are free.” Operators can charge; the screen usually shows the fee before the withdrawal.

Varies by country

Fee rules differ. In the European Union, banks give a standard fee information document and a yearly statement of fees (Directive 2014/92/EU). In the United Kingdom, overdrafts are priced with an annual interest rate instead of daily or monthly fees (FCA). In the United States, debit interchange is capped for large issuers (Federal Reserve), while overdraft fees are set by each bank within federal and state rules. Whether a cash-machine operator can charge, and how the fee is shown, also varies.

Why people disagree

Should governments cap bank fees? Supporters of caps argue that fees such as overdraft charges fall most heavily on people with the least money, and that customers rarely compare these charges when choosing an account, so competition does little to lower them. Opponents argue that caps lead banks to raise other prices, withdraw services or turn down more applicants, which can reduce access for the same people. The Bank of England describes competition between banks as one way prices are kept in check (Bank of England); regulators differ in how far they rely on competition, transparency or direct price rules.
