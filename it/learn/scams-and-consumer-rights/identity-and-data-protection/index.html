Protecting Your Identity and Data

What identity theft is, how data leaks feed scams, and the habits that limit the damage.

Everyday·5 min (Simple) · 9 min (Detailed)·Updated 6 November 2026

Read first: Digital Banking and Keeping Accounts Secure

Key points

  • Identity theft is the use of your personal details to open accounts in your name or to take over accounts you already have.
  • Data breaches are common, so it is reasonable to assume that some of your details are already known to criminals.
  • A different password for every account, a password manager and two-factor authentication block most account takeovers.
  • Bank statements and credit reports show misuse early; some countries also let you freeze or flag your credit file.
  • Date of birth, address and ID numbers work like keys, so sharing fewer of them reduces what can be misused.

Simple: plain words. Detailed: exact figures, codes and methods.

In short: your personal details are keys. Criminals collect them from leaks and use them to open or take over accounts. A few fixed habits make the keys much less useful to them.

Simple version

What identity theft is

Identity theftIdentity theftUsing someone's personal details to open accounts in their name or take over their existing accounts.Read more about Identity theft is when someone uses your personal details to pretend to be you. There are two main kinds:

  • New accounts in your name. A criminal uses your name, date of birth and address to get a loan, a phone contract or a credit card. You find out when the bills or debt collectors arrive.
  • Account takeover. A criminal gets into an account you already have, such as email, banking or shopping, and uses it.

How your data gets out

Companies that hold your data are sometimes hacked. This is called a data breach, and it happens often enough that security agencies suggest assuming some of your details are already out there (NCSC). Data also leaks through phishingPhishingFake messages, calls or websites that pretend to come from a trusted organisation to get people to reveal passwords, codes or card details.Read more about Phishing (fake messages that ask you to log in or “confirm” details), lost phones, and public posts that show a birthday or a home address.

Leaked data makes scams believable. A caller who knows your bank and your address sounds real.

Five habits that limit the damage

  1. A different password for every account. When one site is breached, criminals try the same email and password on many other sites. A password used once is useless to them elsewhere.
  2. A password manager. This is an app that creates and remembers long, unique passwords, so you only need to remember one.
  3. Two-factor authentication. Two-factor authenticationTwo-factor authenticationProving identity with two separate things, such as a password and a code on a phone.Read more about Two-factor authentication asks for a second proof, such as a code from an app, as well as the password. A stolen password alone is then not enough.
  4. Watching statements and credit reports. A payment you do not recognise, or a loan you did not ask for on your credit reportCredit reportThe file of a person's borrowing and repayment history held by a credit bureau or registry.Read more about Credit report, is often the first sign of misuse.
  5. Sharing less. Your date of birth, address and ID number are the keys to new accounts. Fewer places holding them means fewer places they can leak from.

Example

Longer is harder to guess. A password of 8 lower-case letters has about 209 billion possible combinations. Four random words picked from a list of 7,776 words have about 3.7 million billion combinations, roughly 17,500 times as many. The UK’s cyber security agency suggests three random words as an easy-to-remember starting point (NCSC). Length helps against guessing; a unique password is what helps against reuse after a leak.

After a breach notice

A company may write to say your data was in a breach. People commonly do the following:

  • Changing the password for that account, and for any other account that used the same password.
  • Turning on two-factor authentication where it is offered.
  • Treating calls, texts and emails that mention the breach with extra care, because criminals use breach news as a story.
  • Checking bank statements and, where available, a credit report over the following months.

The code is the key

One-time codes sent by text or app are what let a payment or a login go through. Nobody legitimate asks you to read one out; a person who does is trying to use it.

Detailed version

Attack patterns

  • Credential stuffing. Automated reuse of username and password pairs from one breach against many other services. It succeeds in proportion to password reuse, which is why “a different password per account” is the first habit in national guidance (NCSC; CISA).
  • Phishing and real-time relay. A fake login page passes the victim’s password and one-time code to the real site within seconds. App-based prompts that show the site and amount, and hardware security keys, are harder to relay in this way than codes typed by hand.
  • SIM swap. A criminal persuades a mobile operator to move the victim’s number to a new SIM card, then receives text-message codes. Accounts that rely only on text codes are exposed to it.
  • Synthetic identity fraud. Real fragments (often an ID number) are combined with invented details to create a new “person” who builds a credit history and then borrows heavily. Because no real person sees the account, it can run for a long time before detection.

Data-protection rights

Many countries give individuals legal rights over data held about them. The EU’s General Data Protection Regulation includes rights of access (Article 15), correction (Article 16) and erasure in defined circumstances (Article 17), and requires organisations to report certain breaches (EUR-Lex; European Commission). Australia’s Privacy Act gives rights to access and correct personal information, overseen by the OAIC (OAIC). Canada’s federal private-sector privacy law includes access rights, overseen by the Privacy Commissioner (OPC). Scope, exemptions and deadlines differ between these regimes.

Credit file protections

Where credit bureaus exist, a credit file can show new-account fraud early. The tools for locking a file differ:

Example Mechanism
United States A credit freeze with each of the national credit bureaus blocks new lenders from seeing the file; it is free by federal law. A fraud alert asks lenders to verify identity first (IdentityTheft.gov).
Australia A person who believes they are a victim of fraud can ask credit reporting bodies for a ban period, during which the file is not disclosed (OAIC).
Many other countries No consumer freeze exists, or credit data sits in a public credit register run by the central bank; protection then relies on lenders’ identity checks.

Limits of the habits

Two-factor authentication by text message is far stronger than a password alone but weaker than app prompts or security keys, because of SIM swaps and real-time phishing. Monitoring detects misuse; it does not prevent it. And the largest stores of personal data are held by organisations, not individuals, so individual habits reduce exposure but cannot remove it.
