Why Money Decisions Are Hard for Everyone

Human minds evolved for the here and now; modern finance asks for abstraction, probability and the distant future.

Everyday·5 min (Simple) · 9 min (Detailed)·Updated 6 November 2026

Read first: Che cos'è davvero un budget

Key points

  • Fast, intuitive thinking handles most everyday decisions well, but it mis-handles probability, compounding and delay.
  • Biases are systematic, not random, so they can be anticipated.
  • Experts show the same biases as everyone else.
  • The way choices are designed (defaults, wording, order) shapes what people choose.
  • Knowing about a bias helps a little; changing the structure of a decision tends to help more.

Simple: plain words. Detailed: exact figures, codes and methods.

In short: money decisions are hard because they ask human minds to do things they were not built for, and everyone, including experts, finds the same things hard.

Simple version

Two ways of thinking

Psychologists describe two kinds of thinking.

  • Fast thinking is quick, automatic and effortless. It recognises a face, finishes a familiar sentence and judges whether a price “feels” right.
  • Slow thinking is careful and effortful. It does long division, reads a contract and works out a loan’s total cost.

Fast thinking does most of the work, most of the time, and it does it well. It struggles with three things that money decisions often need: probability, compounding and delay.

Three quick demonstrations

Answer each one quickly first, then slowly.

1. The bat and the ball

A bat and a ball cost ¤1.10 in total. The bat costs ¤1.00 more than the ball. How much does the ball cost?

The quick answer is 10 cents. But then the bat would cost ¤1.10 and the total would be ¤1.20. The correct answer is 5 cents: the ball is ¤0.05 and the bat ¤1.05. This puzzle is from a widely used test of reflective thinking (Frederick, 2005).

2. Growing money

¤1,000 grows by 7% a year for 30 years (example). Guess the final amount before reading on.

A common quick guess adds ¤70 a year: ¤1,000 + 30 × ¤70 = ¤3,100. The actual amount is about ¤7,612, because each year’s growth also grows. Underestimating this is called exponential growth bias (Stango and Zinman, 2009).

3. The coin

A fair coin lands heads five times in a row. Is tails now more likely?

No. The coin has no memory: the chance of tails is still one in two. Feeling that tails is “due” is called the gambler’s fallacy.

If your quick answer was wrong, you are in large company. The point of the puzzles is that the wrong answer comes to mind first, for nearly everyone.

Why “biases” is not an insult

A bias in this sense is a predictable error, not a random one. Because it is predictable, it can be planned for. That is the useful part.

These patterns are features of human minds. People with high incomes, advanced degrees and finance jobs show them too. Researchers who study biases report finding them in themselves.

Design shapes choices

The way a choice is presented changes what people pick, even when the facts are the same. What happens if you do nothing (the default), how options are worded, and which option appears first all matter. Later lessons look at each of these.

Awareness helps a little; structure helps more

Knowing about a bias is a start, but in the moment, fast thinking still runs first. Changing the structure of a decision tends to have a bigger effect: a written rule decided in advance, a waiting period before a large purchase, or a transfer that happens without a decision each month. These are design choices people can make for themselves.

Detailed version

The heuristics-and-biases programme

Tversky and Kahneman described three heuristics people use to judge probability and magnitude: representativeness (judging by similarity to a prototype), availability (judging by how easily examples come to mind) and anchoring and adjustment (starting from a number and adjusting too little) (Tversky and Kahneman, 1974). Each works reasonably in many settings and produces systematic errors in others. Kahneman’s 2002 prize recognised this work for integrating psychological insights into economics, especially about judgement and decision-making under uncertainty (Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences, 2002).

The “fast and slow” (System 1 / System 2) language is a description, not a claim about two physical systems in the brain.

Bounded rationality

Herbert Simon argued that decision-makers have limited information, time and computing capacity, and so “satisfice” (choose an option that is good enough) rather than optimise (Nobel Prize, 1978). On this view, a heuristic can be a reasonable response to limits rather than an error. Behavioural economics asks where the gap between such rules and the standard optimising model is large enough to matter for outcomes such as saving, borrowing and investing.

Where financial decisions are hardest

Feature of the decision Why it is hard Example
Rare, high-stakes Little chance to learn from feedback Choosing a mortgage or pension
Delayed outcome Costs now, benefits years later Retirement saving
Probabilistic Outcomes are distributions, not single numbers Insurance, investing
Compounding Non-linear growth Interest, fees, inflation
Complex pricing Many parts, some hidden Card fees, bundled products

Exponential growth bias, for instance, has been linked to lower saving and higher borrowing in US survey data (Stango and Zinman, 2009).

Evidence quality, stated honestly

Behavioural findings vary in strength. Large replication projects in psychology reproduced some well-known effects and not others: the Open Science Collaboration obtained significant results in 36% of replications of 100 published studies, with effect sizes on average about half the originals (Open Science Collaboration, 2015). A later project replicated 13 of 21 social-science experiments from two leading journals (Camerer et al., 2018). Some classic effects, including anchoring, replicated clearly across many laboratories (Klein et al., 2014).

A reasonable reading: the existence of systematic judgement errors is well supported; the size of a particular effect in a particular setting is often smaller and more context-dependent than early studies suggested. Lessons in this module flag where an effect is contested.

Why people disagree

Some researchers see biases as departures from rationality that justify careful choice design by firms and governments. Others, following Simon and later work on “ecological rationality”, argue that many heuristics are well adapted to real environments and that labelling them errors depends on the benchmark chosen. A third group stresses that measured effects in laboratories can shrink in real markets where people learn and stakes are higher. All three positions appear in the research literature.
