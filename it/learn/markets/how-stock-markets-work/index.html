How Stock Markets Work

What a share is, how exchanges match buyers and sellers, and what moves prices.

Intermediate·6 min (Simple) · 11 min (Detailed)·Updated 6 November 2026

Read first: Rischio e rendimento

Key points

  • A share is a small piece of ownership in a company; if the company fails, shareholders are paid last.
  • A share's price is the price of the last trade between a buyer and a seller; its "value" is an estimate that people disagree about.
  • Companies raise money when they first sell shares (the primary market); after that, investors trade shares with each other on an exchange (the secondary market).
  • An index summarises the prices of many shares in one number.
  • Companies can return cash through dividends and share buybacks; growth that is expected shows up in the price.

Simple: plain words. Detailed: exact figures, codes and methods.

In short: a stock market is a place where people buy and sell small pieces of companies, and the price is whatever the last buyer and seller agreed.

Simple version

What a share is

A company can be split into many equal pieces. Each piece is a shareShare (stock)A unit of ownership in a company.Read more about Share (stock) (also called a stock). If a company has one million shares and you own one thousand, you own one tenth of one per cent of it.

Owning a share gives you a claim on the company’s future profits. It often gives you a vote at the yearly meeting. It does not make you responsible for the company’s debts: the most you can lose is what you paid.

There is a catch. If the company fails, the people it owes money to are paid first. Workers, tax authorities, banks and bond holders come before shareholders. Shareholders get whatever is left, which is often nothing.

Where shares are traded

When a company first sells shares to the public, this is called an initial public offering (IPO). The company receives the money. This is the primary market.

After that, the shares belong to investors. When one investor sells to another on an exchange, the company receives nothing. This is the secondary market, and it is what most news about “the stock market” describes.

Private investors generally do not trade on an exchange directly. They use a brokerBrokerAn intermediary who arranges a product or a trade for a client and is paid by fee, commission or both.Read more about Broker: a firm that sends orders to the exchange for them.

How a price is set

An exchange keeps a list of orders called the order book. Buyers say the highest price they will pay. Sellers say the lowest price they will accept. A trade happens when the two meet.

Example

Order book for one share (example):

Buyers want to pay Sellers want to receive
¤9.98 (best buyer) ¤10.02 (best seller)
¤9.97 ¤10.03
¤9.95 ¤10.05

No trade happens until someone moves. If a new buyer agrees to pay ¤10.02, a trade happens at ¤10.02, and that becomes the latest price on the screen.

The price moves minute by minute because new orders keep arriving. People react to company news, interest rates, the wider economy and what other people are doing. The price is a fact. The “true value” of the company is an opinion, and buyers and sellers hold different opinions at the same moment. That is why a trade can happen at all.

How shareholders get money back

There are two ways:

  1. Dividends. The company pays part of its profit to shareholders in cash. A ¤1 yearly dividend on a ¤40 share is a 2.5% dividendDividendA share of profits that a company pays to its shareholders.Read more about Dividend yield (example).
  2. A higher price. If the company grows, or people expect it to grow, the share price can rise. You gain only when you sell, and only if the price is above what you paid.

Some companies also buy back their own shares. This leaves fewer shares, so each remaining share is a slightly larger slice.

Indices: a thermometer for the market

An index is a single number that summarises many share prices. It works like a thermometer. It tells you the temperature, not why it is hot or cold. Many indices give a larger weight to larger companies, so a few large companies can move the whole number.

Varies by country

Each country has its own main exchanges, trading hours and rules for listing a company. Shareholder rights also differ. Rules about voting, how much information companies publish and how dividends are taxed are set nationally; in the European Union, for example, many market rules are set at EU level and applied by national regulators.

Detailed version

Market microstructure

The bid is the highest price a buyer has posted; the ask (or offer) is the lowest price a seller has posted. The difference is the bid-ask spreadBid-ask spreadThe gap between the price buyers will pay and the price sellers will accept, which is a cost to whoever trades.Read more about Bid-ask spread, a cost paid by anyone who trades immediately. In the example above, the spread is ¤0.04 on a mid price of ¤10.00, or 0.4%. Spreads tend to be narrower for large, frequently traded shares and wider for small or rarely traded ones.

Common order types:

Order What it does Trade-off
Market order Trades now at the best available price Certain to execute; price not guaranteed
Limit order Trades only at the stated price or one more favourable to the trader Price controlled; might not execute
Stop order Becomes a market order once a trigger price is reached Can execute well away from the trigger in a fast market

Market makers post both bids and asks and earn the spread in exchange for providing liquidityLiquidityHow quickly something can be turned into cash without losing value.Read more about Liquidity: the ability to trade quickly without moving the price much.

Market capitalisation and index weighting

Market capitalisationMarket capitalisationA company's share price multiplied by its number of shares.Read more about Market capitalisation = share price × number of shares outstanding. A company with 10 million shares at ¤25 has a market capitalisation of ¤250 million (example).

In a capitalisation-weighted index, each company’s weight is its share of the total. Worked example: three companies are worth ¤600 million, ¤300 million and ¤100 million. If the largest rises 10% and the others are unchanged, the index rises (600 × 1.10 + 300 + 100) / 1,000 − 1 = 6%. Price-weighted and equal-weighted indices exist too, and give different results from the same prices.

Corporate actions

  • Dividend: the share price typically falls by roughly the dividend amount on the ex-dividend date, because a buyer after that date no longer receives it.
  • Buyback: cash leaves the company and the share count falls; the total value returned is similar to a dividend of the same size, but tax treatment differs by country.
  • Split: one share becomes several; ownership and total value are unchanged.
  • Rights issue: existing shareholders are offered new shares, usually at a discount; those who do not take them up own a smaller percentage.

Price discovery and the efficiency debate

Why people disagree

In 2013 the economics prize was shared by researchers whose findings point in different directions (Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences, 2013). One view, associated with Eugene Fama, holds that prices adjust quickly to new public information, so predicting short-term price changes from public data is very hard (Fama, 2014). Another, associated with Robert Shiller, finds that prices over several years move more than later dividends justify, and that this pattern is partly predictable from valuation ratios (Shiller, 2014). Researchers still debate whether such patterns reflect changing risk premiums or human psychology. Both views agree that short-term price changes are close to unpredictable.

A practical reading: a share price summarises many people’s estimates under uncertainty. It is not a measurement of value in the way a scale measures weight.

Common misconceptions

  • “The market went down, so money disappeared.” Prices fell, so holdings are valued lower; the shares still exist, and every trade still had a buyer.
  • “A low share price means a cheap company.” Price per share means little without the number of shares; market capitalisation and earnings matter more.
  • “Buying a share funds the company.” Only in the primary market. Secondary trades move money between investors.
