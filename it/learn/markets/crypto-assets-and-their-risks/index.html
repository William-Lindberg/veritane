Crypto Assets and Their Risks

What crypto assets are, how they differ from money and securities, and the specific risks they carry.

Intermediate·6 min (Simple) · 11 min (Detailed)·Updated 6 November 2026

Read first: Rischio e rendimento

Key points

  • Crypto assets are digital tokens recorded on shared databases called distributed ledgers; most are not legal tender and are not bank deposits.
  • Their prices move a lot, and for most of them there is no central issuer standing behind the value.
  • Whoever controls the private keys controls the asset; lost keys and failed trading platforms have cost users their holdings.
  • Stablecoins aim to hold a fixed value against a currency, and some have lost that value.
  • Regulation and tax treatment differ widely between countries and are still changing.
  • Crypto assets are a frequent vehicle for scams.

Simple: plain words. Detailed: exact figures, codes and methods.

In short: a crypto asset is a digital token whose ownership is recorded on a shared database; it can be bought and sold, but it is not money in a bank and nobody guarantees its value.

Simple version

What a token is

A crypto assetCrypto assetA digital token recorded on a shared ledger, usually not backed by any institution and with prices that can move a great deal.Read more about Crypto asset is an entry in a shared digital list of who owns what. Many computers keep copies of the same list, called a ledger. When a token moves from one person to another, the change is added to every copy. No single bank or company keeps the official list.

To move a token you need a private key: a long secret code. Whoever has the key can move the token. A wallet is software or a device that stores keys.

How it differs from money and shares

Bank deposit Share Most crypto assets
Who stands behind it A bank, supervised, often with deposit protection A company with assets and profits Usually no issuer with an obligation to holders
Legal tender The currency it is in, yes No In most countries, no
Protection if the firm holding it fails Deposit protection in many countries Investor protection rules in many countries Often limited or none
Payment reversible if sent by mistake Sometimes Not applicable Generally not

Five risks in plain words

  1. The price moves a lot. Large daily rises and falls are common. A token that falls 50% and then rises 50% ends at 75% of where it started: ¤1,000 becomes ¤500, then ¤750 (example).
  2. Nothing underneath. Most tokens pay no interest, dividends or rent. The price depends on what the next buyer will pay.
  3. Lose the key, lose the asset. A lost key cannot be reset. If a key is stolen, the tokens can be moved and are hard to recover.
  4. Platforms can fail. If you leave tokens on a trading platform, the platform holds the keys. The UK regulator lists the failure of a firm among the reasons people have lost money in crypto (FCA).
  5. Not insured, not reversible. In most places, crypto assets are not covered by deposit protection, and a transfer sent to the wrong address or to a scammer cannot be cancelled.

Stablecoins

A stablecoinStablecoinA crypto token designed to hold a fixed value against a currency, which sometimes fails.Read more about Stablecoin is a token that aims to stay worth a fixed amount, such as one unit of a national currency. Some hold reserves of cash and short-term bonds. Others rely on computer rules. The Bank of England notes that their value depends on the quality of what backs them (Bank of England). In 2022 a large stablecoin that relied on rules rather than reserves lost its fixed value within days (Federal Reserve, 2024).

Scam signals

Crypto assets are often used in fraud because transfers are fast, global and hard to reverse. Common signals: a stranger or new online friend suggests an investment; returns are promised or guaranteed; you are asked to send tokens to “unlock” a withdrawal; someone offers to recover lost crypto for a fee. The Protect section describes these patterns in detail.

Varies by country

Rules differ widely and are changing. The European Union has a single framework, MiCA, covering issuers of crypto assets and the firms that provide services in them (ESMA). Japan requires crypto-asset exchange service providers to register with its Financial Services Agency (FSA Japan). In the United Kingdom, the FCA describes crypto assets as high risk and largely unregulated for investment purposes, and says investors could lose all their money (FCA). Tax treatment also differs from country to country.

Detailed version

Consensus in one paragraph

A distributed ledger needs a way for many independent computers to agree on one history without a central operator. Proof of work makes adding a block costly in computing power and energy, so rewriting history would require outspending the honest network. Proof of stake selects validators in proportion to tokens they lock up and penalises misbehaviour by taking some of those tokens. The BIS analyses the trade-offs between decentralisation, scalability and security in these designs (BIS, 2018).

Custody models

  • Self-custody: the holder keeps the keys. No intermediary fails, but loss or theft of keys is final.
  • Custodial platforms: the platform holds keys and records customer balances in its own books. Customer protection then depends on segregation of client assets, the platform’s solvency and the applicable insolvency law, which differ between jurisdictions. Where client assets are not held separately, customers of a failed platform can end up as creditors waiting for whatever the insolvency process recovers.

Stablecoin designs

Design Backing Main failure channel
Fiat-backed Cash, deposits, short-term government debt Doubt about reserve quality or access leads to redemptions, like a bank run
Crypto-collateralised Other crypto assets, over-collateralised Collateral prices fall faster than positions are liquidated
Algorithmic Rules that expand or shrink supply, often against a sister token Loss of confidence feeds on itself: the sister token loses value as it is issued to defend the peg

TerraUSD, the largest algorithmic stablecoin before May 2022, followed the third design and lost its peg that month (Federal Reserve, 2024). US agencies had earlier described run risk, payment-system risk and concentration risk in stablecoins (US Treasury, 2021), and the FSB has published international recommendations for regulating them (FSB, 2023).

Volatility arithmetic

Losses and gains of the same percentage do not cancel: a fall of x requires a rise of x / (1 − x) to recover. A 50% fall needs +100%; an 80% fall needs +400%. With high volatilityVolatilityHow much and how fast a price moves up and down.Read more about Volatility, the compound (geometric) return sits well below the average (arithmetic) return, roughly by half the variance.

Regulatory approaches

Approaches include: bespoke regimes (the EU’s MiCA, EUR-Lex); registration of exchanges under payments or anti-money-laundering law (Japan’s FSA register); and applying existing securities and commodities law case by case. Whether a given token is a security, a commodity, a payment instrument or none of these is decided differently between jurisdictions and is contested within some of them.

Market structure concerns

The BIS describes concerns documented by authorities: conflicts of interest where one firm acts as exchange, broker, custodian and lender; concentration; opacity of trading on unregulated venues; and links to traditional finance through stablecoin reserves (BIS, 2023).

Why people disagree

Supporters argue that open ledgers allow payments and financial services without trusted intermediaries, and that programmable tokens can lower costs. Many central banks and the BIS argue that trust is moved rather than removed, that most activity is speculative, and that the useful features can be delivered within regulated systems (BIS, 2018). The evidence on long-term use cases is still developing.
