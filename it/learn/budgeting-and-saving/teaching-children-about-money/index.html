Teaching Children About Money

How families introduce money ideas by age, without a single "right" method.

Everyday·5 min (Simple) · 9 min (Detailed)·Updated 6 November 2026

Read first: Che cos'è davvero un budget

Key points

  • Children pick up money habits from what they see adults do, as well as from what they are told.
  • Pocket money, earned money and gifts teach different lessons; families choose their own mix.
  • Letting a child make a small, reversible money mistake is a widely used teaching approach.
  • Saving jars, prepaid cards and junior accounts are tools, not requirements.
  • Talking about household trade-offs at a level that suits the child's age is part of how money ideas are passed on.

Simple: plain words. Detailed: exact figures, codes and methods.

In short: children learn about money from everyday life at home, long before any lesson at school. Families use many different approaches, and there is no single right method.

Simple version

Children watch more than they listen

A child sees an adult pay by card, look at a price tag, put something back on the shelf, or say “not this month”. Those moments teach as much as any explanation. Saying out loud why a choice was made (“this one is cheaper per kilo”, “we are saving for the holiday”) turns an ordinary moment into a small lesson.

One idea for each age

Age One idea An everyday way to show it
Under 6 Money is swapped for things, and it runs out Paying with coins at a shop; waiting a day for something
6–11 Saving for something takes time, and choices have a cost A jar for a toy; choosing between two treats
12–15 A budget lasts a set time, and online money is real money A monthly allowance for some costs; checking in-app purchases together
16–18 Pay slips, accounts, contracts and the cost of borrowing Reading a first pay slip; looking at a phone contract’s total cost

Example

A 9-year-old gets ¤5 a week (example) and wants a ¤30 game. Saving ¤2 a week, it takes 30 ÷ 2 = 15 weeks. Some families add a “match”: for every ¤1 saved, the parent adds ¤0.50. With the match, ¤2 saved becomes ¤3 a week, and the game takes 30 ÷ 3 = 10 weeks. The child learns that waiting has a cost, and that saving can earn something extra, like interest.

Pocket money, earned money and gifts

  • Pocket money (an allowance) is a regular amount. It teaches planning: when it is gone, it is gone until next week.
  • Earned money comes from extra jobs. It links effort and reward.
  • Gifts arrive at birthdays and holidays. They raise the question of what to do with a larger, one-off amount.

Families combine these in different ways, and some give no regular money at all. Each choice teaches something slightly different.

Small mistakes

Many parents let a child spend all their money on something that turns out to be less fun than expected, or breaks quickly, without stepping in. The loss is small and the lesson is remembered. The same mistake at 25, with a larger amount, costs more.

Tools, not requirements

Saving jars (one for spending, one for saving, sometimes one for giving), prepaid cards with spending controls, and junior bank accounts are all common. None of them is necessary. A notebook and a conversation work too.

A weekly money talk

Some families keep a short weekly chat. Example prompts:

  • What did you spend money on this week? Was it worth it?
  • Is there something you are saving for? How far along are you?
  • Did you see an advert that made you want something? What was it selling?
  • What is one thing our family spends money on that you did not know about?

When a child is scammed in a game

Children are often targeted in games and chats with offers of free coins, skins or items. When it happens, families commonly:

  1. talk about it calmly, without criticism, so the child feels able to tell an adult next time,
  2. change the game account’s password and turn on two-step login,
  3. report the account or message to the game or platform,
  4. contact the bank or card provider if a card was used,
  5. report the scam to the national fraud or consumer service, where one exists.

Detailed version

Evidence on financial socialisation

Financial socialisation is the process by which people acquire money knowledge, attitudes and habits. Research in this area treats parents and carers as a primary channel alongside school, peers and media, and distinguishes explicit teaching (conversations, allowances, rules) from implicit modelling (what children observe). Studies differ in method and setting, and most rely on self-reported survey data, so links between childhood experiences and adult outcomes are associations rather than proven causes. Internationally, the OECD’s PISA programme includes an optional financial literacy assessment of 15-year-olds, which also asks students about money conversations at home.

Design choices for an allowance

Choice Option A Option B
Link to chores Allowance paid for chores Allowance separate; chores are part of family life
What it covers Treats only Some real costs (lunches, transport, clothes)
Frequency Weekly (easier for younger children) Monthly (practises longer budgeting)
Saving incentives Parental match or “family interest” No incentive

A parental match is an informal way to show compound growth: a “family interest rate” of 10% a month on savings shows in weeks what a bank account shows in years.

Junior and custodial account structures

Accounts for minors take several legal forms:

  • Accounts in the child’s name with a parent as signatory, where the child gains control at an age set by the provider or the law.
  • Custodial accounts, in which an adult manages assets that legally belong to the child until a set age.
  • Tax-advantaged junior savings schemes created by governments. In the United Kingdom, a Junior ISA can be opened for a child, the money belongs to the child, and it can usually only be withdrawn from age 18 (GOV.UK). An earlier scheme, the Child Trust Fund, was given to children born in a set period (GOV.UK).

Tax on gifts and children’s income

Tax rules on money given to children vary. In the United States, part of a child’s investment and other unearned income above a threshold can be taxed at the parent’s rate (the “kiddie tax”) (IRS). In the United Kingdom, income from money given by a parent can be taxed as the parent’s income above a small yearly amount. In other countries, gifts to children are subject to gift tax allowances, or not taxed at all. Thresholds change over time and are not given here.

Fraud reporting routes

Reporting routes differ: in the United States, the Federal Trade Commission (FTC); in Australia, Scamwatch (Scamwatch); in the United Kingdom, Action Fraud. Game platforms also have their own reporting tools.

Hand-off to the young reader

Lesson 1.7, Money for Young People, is written for readers under 18 and can be read together with a child aged about 11 or over. It covers pocket money, a first wage, subscriptions and messages asking for codes.

Varies by country

Accounts and rules for children differ. In the United Kingdom, Junior ISAs hold savings or investments that belong to the child and usually become available at 18. In the United States, custodial accounts under state laws (often known by the names of the uniform acts, UTMA or UGMA) hold assets for a minor until an age set by the state. In many countries, banks offer children’s accounts with a parent as co-holder and limits on cards and withdrawals, and mobile-money services often set a minimum age for opening an account.

Why people disagree

Whether pocket money should be linked to chores is a long-running debate among parents and educators. One view holds that paying for chores teaches that money is earned through work. Another holds that household tasks are a shared family responsibility, and that paying for them can make children reluctant to help without payment; in this view, pocket money is a budgeting tool, separate from chores. Many families use a mix, such as a basic allowance plus payment for extra jobs. There is no agreed best approach.
