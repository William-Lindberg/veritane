Percentages, Averages and Charts, Step by Step

The handful of number skills that unlock every financial document and news story.

Everyday·7 min (Simple) · 12 min (Detailed)·Updated 6 November 2026

Key points

  • A percentage is a share of something: the first question is what the base is.
  • A change in percentage points is different from a percentage change.
  • An average can hide how spread out the numbers are; the median is less affected by a few extreme values.
  • A chart's axis and time window change the picture it gives.
  • Very large totals make more sense per person or per year.

Simple: plain words. Detailed: exact figures, codes and methods.

In short: a few number skills (finding the base of a percentage, telling points from per cent, choosing the right average and reading a chart’s axis) make most financial documents and news stories much easier to follow.

Simple version

Percent of what?

“Per cent” means “out of 100”. 20% of ¤50 is ¤10. The number after “of” is called the base, and it changes everything.

Example

A coat costs ¤100 (example). The shop takes 20% off: it now costs ¤80. Next month the price goes up by 20%. Is it back to ¤100?

No. The second 20% is taken of ¤80, not ¤100. 20% of ¤80 is ¤16, so the new price is ¤96. The two changes have the same percentage but different bases.

When you see a percentage, ask: “a percentage of what?” A “50% increase” in something very small can still be a small amount. A “1% fall” in something very large can be a lot of money.

Points and per cent are not the same

Interest rates and inflation are themselves percentages. That makes changes in them easy to mix up.

Suppose a loan rate rises from 2% to 3% (example).

  • It rose by 1 percentage pointPercentage pointThe simple difference between two percentages; going from 2 % to 3 % is a rise of one percentage point, which is a 50 % increase.Read more about Percentage point (3 − 2 = 1).
  • It also rose by 50%, because 1 is half of 2.

Both statements are true. News reports and banks usually talk in percentage points, sometimes shortened to “points” or “pp”. A “basis point” is one hundredth of a percentage point.

Mean and median

The word “average” usually means the mean: add everything up and divide by how many there are. The medianMedianThe middle value when all values are lined up in order: half are above and half are below.Read more about Median is the middle value when everything is sorted from lowest to highest.

Five people earn ¤20,000, ¤22,000, ¤25,000, ¤30,000 and ¤200,000 a year (example).

  • The mean is ¤297,000 ÷ 5 = ¤59,400.
  • The median is ¤25,000, the middle value.

Four of the five people earn far less than the mean. One very high income pulls the mean up, but hardly moves the median. This is why statistics offices often report median income alongside mean income.

Reading a chart

Two charts of the same data can look very different.

  • The vertical axis. If it starts at 95 instead of 0, a change from 98 to 100 fills the whole chart and looks steep. Check the numbers on the axis, not just the shape.
  • The time window. A chart of the last six months can show a rise, while the last ten years show a fall. Check the dates along the bottom.
  • The units. Is it the price, the change in price, or the change in the change? Check the axis label.

Making big numbers human

Billions are hard to picture. Dividing by the number of people or years helps.

If a country of 10 million people spends ¤5 billion on a programme (example), that is ¤5,000,000,000 ÷ 10,000,000 = ¤500 per person. If the spending is spread over five years, it is ¤100 per person per year.

Detailed version

Percentage points and percentage change

Eurostat defines the percentage point as the unit for the arithmetic difference between two percentages (Eurostat). A move from 2% to 3% is +1 pp, a relative change of +50%. Both are needed: pp for policy rates and unemployment rates, relative change for most levels.

Percentage changes do not add or cancel: +10% then −10% leaves 99% of the start (1.10 × 0.90 = 0.99).

Compound annual growth rate

To summarise growth over several years:

CAGR = (end ÷ start)^(1 ÷ years) − 1

Example: 100 to 150 over 5 years gives 1.5^(1/5) − 1 = 8.45% a year, less than the simple 50 ÷ 5 = 10%. Quarterly growth is often annualised: 0.5% quarter on quarter becomes 1.005^4 − 1 = 2.02% a year, which is not the same as the year-on-year change.

Mean, median and spread

The ABS describes mean, median and mode as measures of central tendency and notes that the median is less affected by outliers and skewed data than the mean (ABS). For skewed distributions such as income and wealth, mean minus median is itself informative. Spread is described by percentiles (for example the 10th and 90th) or by ratios between them (Eurostat: median, Eurostat: average).

Index numbers and rebasing

An index expresses a series relative to a base period, which is given the value 100 (ONS):

index_t = value_t ÷ value_base × 100

If a basket costs ¤50 in the base year and ¤55 later, the index is 110: a 10% rise. Rebasing moves the base: divide every value by the new base-period value and multiply by 100. Rebasing changes the levels shown but not the percentage changes between periods. Consumer price indices, house price indices and real GDP are published this way.

Contributions to growth

When a total is made of parts, each part’s contribution in percentage points is its share of the total times its own growth:

contribution_i = weight_i × growth_i

Example: if part A is 60% of a total and grows 5%, and part B is 40% and does not grow, the total grows 0.6 × 5 + 0.4 × 0 = 3.0%, all of it contributed by A. Statistical releases use this to break down GDP or inflation.

Log scales

On a logarithmic axis, equal vertical distances mean equal percentage changes. A series growing at a steady rate appears as a straight line. Log scales suit long time spans; a linear axis on a 50-year price series makes early changes look flat.

Reading a statistical release

Before reading the headline, check: the period covered; whether data are seasonally adjusted; whether figures are preliminary and subject to revision; the unit (level, index, % change, pp); and the comparison period (month on month, quarter on quarter, year on year). Module 16 goes further.

Varies by country

Number formats differ. In the United Kingdom and the United States, 1,234.56 uses a comma for thousands and a point for decimals; in much of continental Europe, the same number is written 1.234,56 or 1 234,56. In India, large numbers are grouped in lakhs (100,000) and crores (10,000,000), written 1,00,000 and 1,00,00,000. Reading the format first avoids misreading an amount by a factor of a thousand.
