Consumer rights

Automated decisions and your data

What rights people have when a computer system, including artificial intelligence (AI), decides or helps decide something about their money: a loan, a credit limit, a fraud block or a price.

Updated 6 November 2026

Key points

  • Banks, lenders and insurers use automated systems for loan approvals, credit limits, fraud flags and pricing.
  • Automated systems learn from past data and can repeat past unfairness; this is called algorithmic bias.
  • In the EU, people have a right not to be subject to some fully automated decisions with significant effects, with safeguards that include human review; the UK has its own version of these rules.
  • Data-protection laws in many countries give a right to see the personal data a firm holds and to correct it.
  • Credit laws in several countries require specific reasons for a refusal, whether or not an algorithm was involved.

Not legal advice

This page explains general principles and gives examples. It is not legal advice. Rights over automated decisions depend on the country, the sector and the type of decision.

What an automated decision is

An algorithm is a set of rules a computer follows to make a decision. A loan algorithm might check income, credit score and existing debt against minimum thresholds before recommending yes or no. Artificial intelligence systems go further: they find patterns in large amounts of past data and use them to score new cases (Financial Literacy Framework).

These systems are fast and can be accurate. They can also be wrong, and unlike a loan officer, an algorithm does not hear an explanation. Some systems are a black box: the firm can see the inputs and the result but cannot easily say why the system decided as it did (Financial Literacy Framework).

Why bias can appear

A system trained on past lending decisions learns from a history in which some groups had less access to credit. It can reproduce those patterns without anyone intending it, for example through postcode, name or financial history. This is algorithmic bias (Financial Literacy Framework). A system can also drift: if the world changes and the model is not updated, its accuracy and fairness can fall.

The principles that hold broadly

  • A decision has reasons. Where credit law requires reasons for a refusal, the use of an algorithm does not remove that duty.
  • Data can be checked. Where data-protection law applies, people can see the personal data used and correct it.
  • A person can look again. Several legal systems give a right to human review of significant fully automated decisions.
  • Complaints have a home. Data-protection authorities handle data rights; financial regulators and ombudsmen handle lending and insurance decisions.

Varies by country: examples

  • European Union: under the General Data Protection Regulation, a person has a right not to be subject to a decision based solely on automated processing, without human involvement, that has legal effects or similarly significant effects. The European Data Protection Board lists decisions that affect people’s financial circumstances, such as eligibility for credit, and automatic refusal of online applications as examples (EDPB). Such decisions are allowed only in defined cases (authorised by law, based on explicit consent, or necessary for a contract), and then the person can obtain human intervention, express their point of view and contest the decision (GDPR Article 22, EUR-Lex). The UK kept a version of these rules after leaving the EU and has since amended it, so the details differ. The right of access includes being told about automated decision-making and given meaningful information about the logic involved (EDPB).
  • European Union, credit: a person refused credit can ask the bank’s complaints office for a written reply with the exact reasons (Your Europe).
  • United States: a lender that refuses credit has to give the applicant a statement of the specific reasons under the Equal Credit Opportunity Act and Regulation B (12 CFR 1002.9), and a notice when the decision was based on a credit report under the Fair Credit Reporting Act (15 U.S.C. 1681m). In 2022 the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau published Circular 2022-03, which said that using complex algorithms does not excuse a lender from giving specific, accurate reasons (Federal Register). The Bureau withdrew that circular, with many other guidance documents, in May 2025 (Federal Register); the reasons requirement in Regulation B itself is unchanged.

Four questions to ask

The Financial Literacy Framework suggests these questions after a decision you do not understand (Financial Literacy Framework):

  1. Was an automated system involved in this decision?
  2. Which specific factors led to this outcome?
  3. Can I speak with someone about my application?
  4. What would change the outcome in future?

What people commonly do next

  • People who receive an automated decision commonly ask whether a system was involved, which factors mattered, and how to get a person to review it.
  • A copy of the personal data used can be requested where data-protection law gives that right, and wrong data can be corrected.
  • If the firm does not answer, complaints go to the data-protection authority or the financial regulator or ombudsman.
