Consumer rights

Warranties and guarantees

The difference between the legal guarantee that comes from the law, a manufacturer's or seller's warranty, and a paid extended warranty.

Updated 6 November 2026

Key points

  • A legal (statutory) guarantee comes from consumer law and applies whether or not a warranty is offered.
  • A commercial warranty is an extra promise; it can add to the buyer's legal rights but cannot remove them.
  • A paid extended warranty is a separate product, closer to insurance, with its own price, terms and exclusions.
  • Claims under the legal guarantee generally go to the seller; claims under a manufacturer's warranty go to the manufacturer.
  • The length of legal guarantees, and who has to prove what, vary by country.

Not legal advice

This page explains general principles and gives examples. It is not legal advice. A national consumer authority or advice service can say how the rules apply to a specific purchase.

Three different things

Legal guarantee Commercial warranty Extended warranty
Where it comes from The law The seller or manufacturer, as a promise A contract you buy, often from an insurer
Price Included by law Included in the product price Paid separately
Who you claim from The seller Whoever gave the warranty The warranty provider
Can it reduce your rights? No No No; it sits on top of the legal rights

The principles that hold broadly

  • The law comes first. In many countries, consumer rights for faulty goods cannot be removed by a shop’s policy or a warranty’s small print.
  • A warranty can only add. It can offer a longer period, cover more parts or promise faster service. It cannot shorten the legal guarantee.
  • The best offer counts. Where a warranty statement is less generous than what was advertised, EU rules apply the more advantageous conditions (Your Europe).
  • Extended warranties are a purchase decision. Their value depends on the price, the likely repair cost and what the legal guarantee already covers.

Varies by country: examples

  • European Union: the legal guarantee lasts a minimum of two years from delivery, and some countries set longer periods. A commercial guarantee (“warranty”) can add protection but cannot exclude or reduce the rights under the minimum two-year guarantee (Your Europe). A producer can also offer a “commercial guarantee of durability” of more than two years at no extra cost, which applies independently of the seller’s legal guarantee (Your Europe).
  • United Kingdom: warranties and guarantees add to legal rights. Legal rights for a fault can last up to six years (five in Scotland), and using them is usually easier within the first six months; after that, a warranty claim can be simpler because the buyer might otherwise have to show the fault was not caused by them (Citizens Advice).
  • Australia: consumer guarantees apply automatically and cannot be taken away by the seller (ACCC).

What people commonly do next

  • People with a fault commonly check first whether the legal guarantee covers it, because it can be simpler than a warranty claim.
  • For a warranty claim, the warranty document, proof of purchase and a description of the fault are usually needed.
  • If a seller has stopped trading, a manufacturer's warranty can still apply, and some seller warranties are insurance-backed.
