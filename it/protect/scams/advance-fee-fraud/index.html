Scam patterns

Advance-fee fraud

A promised prize, inheritance, loan, grant or job that requires a fee to be paid first. The fee is the scam; the promised money does not exist.

Updated 6 November 2026

Key points

  • Legitimate lotteries, prize draws and inheritances do not charge the winner a fee to release the money.
  • The fees multiply: a “release fee”, then a tax, insurance, a lawyer's charge, a currency conversion.
  • Official-looking documents (court orders, bank statements, certificates) are forged to make the story credible.
  • The money sent is lost, and the target's personal details can be used for identity theft.

Warning signs

  • A message saying you won a competition you did not enter.
  • A stranger, lawyer or official saying a distant relative left you money.
  • A loan or grant offered “regardless of credit history”, with a fee first.
  • Requests for identity documents or bank details to “process” the payment.
  • Payment requested by wire transfer, gift card, voucher or crypto.
  • A new fee each time the last one is paid.

How it works, step by step

  1. The windfall. A letter, email, text or social-media message says you are owed money: a prize, an inheritance, a rebate, compensation, a grant or a loan.
  2. The paperwork. For inheritance stories, the scammer poses as a lawyer, banker or foreign official and supplies fake bank statements, certificates and other documents (Scamwatch).
  3. The fee. To release the money, a fee is needed: tax, administration, insurance or legal costs.
  4. More fees. Each payment is followed by a new obstacle and a new fee (Scamwatch).
  5. The end. Contact stops when the target stops paying. The promised money did not exist.

The loan version targets people who have applied for credit. The UK Financial Conduct Authority describes “loan fee fraud”, where fraudsters ask for an upfront payment as a deposit, administrative fee or insurance and the loan does not arrive (FCA).

Safety facts

  • Don’t pay a fee to collect winnings or a prize; legitimate lotteries don’t ask you to do this. (Scamwatch)
  • Government departments and trusted companies never ask you to pay money upfront to claim a grant or rebate. (Scamwatch)
  • Never share your bank or card details or identity documents with anyone you do not know or trust. (Scamwatch)
  • Money paid by vouchers, gift cards or a wire transfer service is unlikely to come back. (Citizens Advice)

Where to report

The general route is: the bank or payment service used, then the police or national fraud service. Examples:

  • Australia: Scamwatch (scamwatch.gov.au).
  • United Kingdom: Report Fraud in England and Wales or Police Scotland in Scotland (GOV.UK).
  • United States: the Federal Trade Commission (reportfraud.ftc.gov).
  • Sweden: the police on 114 14 (Polisen).

What people commonly do next

  • People who have paid commonly contact their bank or payment provider straight away, and stop all further payments.
  • Where identity documents were sent, the issuing authority and a credit reference agency (where they exist) are usually told.
  • Reporting to the police or national fraud service records the case and the contact details used.
