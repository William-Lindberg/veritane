Scam patterns

Subscriptions that start from “free” trials

“Free” or very cheap trials that turn into expensive recurring charges, with the terms in small print and cancellation made hard to find.

Updated 6 November 2026

Key points

  • A trial converts to a paid plan on its end date unless it is cancelled before then.
  • The recurring price, billing date and cancellation route are often shown only in the terms.
  • Some offers add extra paid items through pre-ticked boxes; in the EU a pre-ticked box is not valid consent to pay.
  • Online and phone sign-ups in many countries carry a cooling-off period for cancelling without a reason.
  • Unauthorised or unexpected recurring charges can often be disputed through the card provider.

Warning signs

  • A “free” offer that still asks for card details, often for a small “shipping” charge.
  • Countdown timers, “only today” prices or claims of limited stock.
  • Terms that are hard to find, or a total price that appears only at the last step.
  • No clear cancel button, or cancellation only by phone during short hours.
  • A charge on a statement from a company name you do not recognise.

Not every subscription that is hard to cancel is a scam: some are legal but poorly disclosed, and some break consumer law. The pattern is the same either way, and so are the routes to a refund.

How it works, step by step

  1. The offer. An advert offers a free sample, a trial of an app or service, or a product for a small delivery charge.
  2. The card. The sign-up asks for card details “to cover shipping” or “in case you continue”.
  3. The conversion. When the trial ends, the plan converts to a full price, sometimes monthly, sometimes yearly in one payment.
  4. The extras. Further products or “memberships” may have been added at checkout.
  5. The exit. Cancelling requires a phone call, a letter or a hidden page, or the account cannot be found.

What the rules say (examples)

  • European Union: traders have to show the total price, including taxes and charges, before you pay, and any additional payment needs your explicit consent: a pre-ticked box does not count, and money collected that way can be reclaimed (Your Europe). Contracts made online or by phone can generally be cancelled within 14 days without a reason (Your Europe).
  • United Kingdom: for card payments, people can ask their card provider for help through chargeback, or through a Section 75 claim for credit-card purchases of a single item over £100 and up to £30,000, after first trying the trader (Citizens Advice).
  • Elsewhere: cancellation and “click-to-cancel” rules vary by country and by state; the national consumer authority publishes the local rules.

Safety facts

  • Be careful of offers that pressure you to pay by unusual methods, like money orders, gift cards or cryptocurrency. (Scamwatch)
  • A pre-ticked box on a trader’s website is not consent to pay; you are entitled to have that payment reimbursed in the EU. (Your Europe)

Where to report

The general route is: the seller (in writing), then the card provider or bank, then the national consumer authority. Examples:

  • European Union: the European Consumer Centre in your country helps with problems involving a trader in another EU country (ECC-Net).
  • United Kingdom: Citizens Advice consumer helpline, which can refer cases to Trading Standards (GOV.UK).
  • United States: the Federal Trade Commission (reportfraud.ftc.gov).

What people commonly do next

  • People who find an unexpected recurring charge commonly cancel through the seller's own account page or by email, and keep the confirmation.
  • If the seller does not respond, the card provider or bank can be asked about a chargeback or, where available, a claim under consumer-credit rules.
  • Some banks and card providers can block future payments to a named merchant on request.
  • Misleading sign-up pages are reported to the national consumer authority.
