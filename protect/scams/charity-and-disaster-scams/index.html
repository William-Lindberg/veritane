Scam patterns

Charity and disaster scams

Fake appeals after floods, earthquakes, wars or around holidays, and fake fundraisers, that take donations meant for people in need.

Updated 6 November 2026

Key points

  • Fake appeals appear all year, and often follow real disasters and emergencies.
  • A fake charity website can look almost identical to a real one, with only the payment details changed.
  • Collectors' identification can be forged, so a badge is not proof.
  • Registered charities can be checked on a national register in many countries.
  • Pressure and guilt replace information: a genuine charity can say who it is, where it is and how the money is used.

Warning signs

  • A charity you have not heard of before, or a familiar name with an unfamiliar website or email address.
  • Pressure to give now, or being made to feel guilty for asking questions.
  • Requests for cash only, gift cards, crypto or a transfer to a personal account.
  • A cheque made out to the collector rather than the charity.
  • No receipt, or a receipt without the charity's details.
  • A crowdfunding page with no verifiable link to the people it claims to help.

How it works, step by step

  1. The moment. A disaster or emergency is in the news, or a holiday season begins. Fraudulent charity approaches happen all year and often respond to real disasters (Scamwatch).
  2. The appeal. A call, message, social-media post, door-to-door collector or crowdfunding page asks for help for victims.
  3. The copy. The name, logo or website imitates a known charity, sometimes changing only the payment details (Scamwatch).
  4. The payment. The donation goes to the scammer, by cash, card, transfer, gift card or crypto.

Besides the money lost, these scams take donations away from real charities and causes (Scamwatch).

Checking a charity (examples)

Many countries keep a public register of charities:

  • England and Wales: the Charity Commission register shows a charity’s name, address, trustees, aims and finances; Scotland and Northern Ireland keep separate registers, and very small or excepted charities may not appear (GOV.UK).
  • United States: the Internal Revenue Service offers a search for tax-exempt organisations and warns about charity impersonators (IRS).
  • Australia: the Australian Charities and Not-for-profits Commission keeps a register, which Scamwatch recommends checking (Scamwatch).

Safety facts

  • Approach charity organisations directly to make a donation. (Scamwatch)
  • Avoid any arrangement with a stranger that asks for payment by money order, wire transfer, pre-loaded card or Bitcoin; it is rare to recover money sent this way. (Scamwatch)
  • Ask a street collector for identification and details of the charity; if you have doubts about who they are, do not pay. (Scamwatch)

Where to report

The general route is: the bank or card provider (if you paid), the charity regulator, then the police or national fraud service. Examples:

  • Australia: Scamwatch (scamwatch.gov.au).
  • United States: the Federal Trade Commission (reportfraud.ftc.gov).
  • United Kingdom: Report Fraud in England and Wales or Police Scotland in Scotland (GOV.UK).

What people commonly do next

  • People who want to give commonly go to the charity's website themselves, rather than following a link or answering a call.
  • Anyone who gave to a fake appeal contacts their bank or card provider; card payments can sometimes be disputed.
  • Fake appeals are reported to the charity regulator, the platform that hosted them, and the national fraud service.
