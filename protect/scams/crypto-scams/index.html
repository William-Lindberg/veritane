Scam patterns

Crypto scams

Fake exchanges and trading apps, wallet-draining links, “giveaways”, abandoned projects and celebrity deepfake adverts that use the fact that crypto transfers cannot be reversed.

Updated 6 November 2026

Key points

  • A crypto transfer cannot be reversed by a bank or card provider once it is confirmed on the network.
  • A seed phrase (or recovery phrase) is the master key to a wallet: anyone who has it can move everything in it.
  • “Send some crypto and get double back” offers take what is sent and return nothing.
  • Fake platforms can show invented prices and profits; the problem appears when you try to withdraw.
  • In many countries most crypto activity is not covered by the compensation schemes and ombudsman services that apply to banks and regulated investments.

Warning signs

  • Adverts or videos in which a celebrity or business leader promotes a crypto platform.
  • An online contact you have not met in person who teaches you to trade on a particular app.
  • A request for your seed phrase, private key or to “connect your wallet” to an unknown site to claim a reward.
  • Returns that are promised, fixed or described as risk-free.
  • Withdrawals that need an extra payment for “tax”, “gas” or “unlocking”.
  • A new token whose team is anonymous and whose selling is restricted.

How it works, step by step

Crypto scams take several forms, but most follow the same path:

  1. The advert or contact. Fraudsters advertise on social media, often using images of celebrities, or target people searching for investments online (FCA). Scammers also use artificial intelligence to create deepfake images and voices of well-known people (Scamwatch).
  2. The platform. The advert links to a professional-looking site where prices and returns can be faked (FCA).
  3. The deposit. The target buys crypto on a real exchange and sends it to the platform’s address. From this moment it is outside any bank’s reach.
  4. The exit. The scammers close the account, or keep the pretence going until the target tries to sell or withdraw (FCA).

Other common forms:

  • Giveaways. A post promises to send back twice what you send. Nothing comes back.
  • Wallet drainers. A link asks you to “connect your wallet” or “approve” a transaction to claim a reward; the approval lets the site move your coins.
  • Seed-phrase theft. A fake support agent or app asks for the recovery phrase to “fix” or “verify” a wallet.
  • Abandoned projects (rug pulls). Promoters raise money for a new token, then sell their holdings or withdraw the funds and disappear.

Why recovery is hard

Crypto transfers have no central party that can reverse them. In the United Kingdom, most crypto activity is not regulated, so there is generally no access to the Financial Ombudsman Service and no compensation from the Financial Services Compensation Scheme if a firm fails (FCA). Rules differ elsewhere, but the absence of a reversal mechanism is common to all crypto networks.

Safety facts

  • “Send one, get two back” offers and promised returns that sound unrealistic are fraud; if an investment sounds too good to be true, it probably is. (FCA)
  • Do not pay money up front by bank transfer or cryptocurrency to anyone you have only met online; it is rare to get money back that is sent this way. (Scamwatch)
  • Never accept offers from anyone who says they can get your money back. (Scamwatch)

Where to report

The general route is: the exchange or wallet provider you used, then the police or national fraud service, then the financial regulator. Examples:

  • United Kingdom: the Financial Conduct Authority, which explains what to do after a crypto scam (FCA), and Report Fraud in England and Wales (GOV.UK).
  • United States: the FBI’s Internet Crime Complaint Center (ic3.gov) and the Federal Trade Commission (reportfraud.ftc.gov).
  • Australia: Scamwatch (scamwatch.gov.au).

What people commonly do next

  • People who sent crypto to a scammer commonly report it at once to the exchange they sent it from, which can sometimes flag the receiving address.
  • Moving any remaining funds to a new wallet with a new seed phrase is a common step when a phrase or key may have been exposed.
  • Losses are reported to the police or national fraud service and, where crypto firms are registered, to the financial regulator.
  • Offers to “trace and recover” stolen crypto for a fee are a known follow-up scam.
