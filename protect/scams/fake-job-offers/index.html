Scam patterns

Fake job offers and task scams

Jobs that require paying for training, equipment or “verification”, task apps that pay small sums and then require deposits, and “jobs” that involve receiving and forwarding money.

Updated 6 November 2026

Key points

  • Genuine employers pay workers; a job that requires the worker to pay first is a warning sign.
  • Task scams pay small amounts at first, then require deposits to unlock earnings that cannot be withdrawn.
  • Overpayment scams send a cheque or payment for more than agreed and ask for the difference back; the original payment later bounces.
  • Receiving money and passing it on for someone else can make a person a money mule, which is a crime in many countries even when the person did not know where the money came from.
  • Fake recruiters impersonate real companies and agencies, and fake adverts appear on trusted job sites.

Warning signs

  • Unexpected contact from a recruiter by text or a messaging app.
  • High pay for little effort, working from home.
  • Hiring without an interview or any check of experience.
  • A fee for training, a starter kit, a visa or a background check.
  • Being asked to top up an account with your own money or crypto to complete tasks.
  • A job that involves moving money, buying gift cards or reshipping parcels for someone else.

How it works, step by step

Upfront-fee jobs

  1. An advert or message offers well-paid, flexible work.
  2. Hiring is fast, with no real interview (Scamwatch).
  3. Before starting, the new “employee” pays for training materials, equipment, a recruitment fee or a check (Scamwatch).
  4. The job does not exist.

Task scams

  1. The worker is asked to complete simple, repetitive online tasks, such as rating products.
  2. A small payment, often in crypto, arrives after the first round (Scamwatch).
  3. Later tasks require the worker to top up an account with their own money to continue or to “unlock” earnings.
  4. The earnings shown in the app cannot be withdrawn.

Money mule “jobs”

  1. A “payment processing” or “finance assistant” role asks the worker to receive money into a personal account.
  2. The worker passes the money on to other accounts, crypto wallets or gift cards, sometimes keeping a commission (Scamwatch).
  3. The money is stolen or criminal, and the worker’s account is the visible link in the chain.

Overpayment

A buyer or “employer” sends a cheque or payment that is more than agreed and asks for the extra to be sent back. Scamwatch lists this as a warning sign (Scamwatch). The original payment is later reversed, and the money sent back is lost.

Safety facts

  • Never send your own money to get paid for a job or task; you won’t get it back. (Scamwatch)
  • Never allow anyone to use your bank account or move money through it; acting as a money mule is a serious crime, even if you do not know how the money is used. (Scamwatch)
  • Contact recruitment agencies using phone numbers you have found yourself, not ones in the message. (Scamwatch)
  • Never send your passport or identity documents to an employer or recruiter unless you are certain they are genuine. (Scamwatch)

Where to report

The general route is: the bank (especially if money passed through your account), the job site or app, then the police or national fraud service. Examples:

  • Australia: Scamwatch (scamwatch.gov.au).
  • United States: the Federal Trade Commission (reportfraud.ftc.gov).
  • United Kingdom: Report Fraud in England and Wales or Police Scotland in Scotland (GOV.UK).
  • Sweden: the police on 114 14 (Polisen).

What people commonly do next

  • People who paid a fee or deposit commonly contact their bank straight away and stop further payments.
  • Anyone who has received money into their account for a “job” usually tells their bank before moving it on.
  • Fake adverts are reported to the job site or app where they appeared, and the scam to the national fraud service.
  • Where identity documents were sent, watching for accounts opened in your name is a common next step.
