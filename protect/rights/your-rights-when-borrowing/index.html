Consumer rights

Your rights when borrowing

Rights that borrowers have in many countries: clear cost information before signing, fair treatment, reasons for a refusal, correction of credit-report errors, and limits on how debts are collected.

Updated 6 November 2026

Key points

  • Lenders in many countries are required to show the full cost of credit, including the annual percentage rate (APR) and all fees, before the contract is signed.
  • Discrimination in lending on protected characteristics, such as race, religion, sex or marital status, is illegal in many legal systems.
  • In several countries a refused applicant can ask for the reasons, and the reasons have to be specific.
  • Wrong entries on a credit report can be disputed and corrected; accurate entries stay for the period set by law.
  • Debt collectors face limits on when and how they contact people and what they can say.
  • Many credit agreements can be cancelled within a short period, and loans can often be repaid early.

Not legal advice

This page explains general principles and gives examples. It is not legal advice. Borrowers’ rights depend on the country, the type of credit and the lender; a national regulator, ombudsman or free debt-advice service can say how they apply.

Why these rights exist

Lenders know far more about credit products than most borrowers, and a loan contract is written by the lender. Borrowing rules exist to narrow that gap: borrowers see the cost before they commit, are treated on their own financial facts, and can challenge mistakes. As the Financial Literacy Framework puts it, asking for a clear explanation of a refusal is exercising a legal entitlement, not asking for a favour (Financial Literacy Framework).

The principles that hold broadly

  1. See the cost before signing. The APR, the total amount repayable, the number and size of payments and all fees are disclosed in writing before the contract is signed.
  2. Equal treatment. Credit decisions are based on financial information, not on protected characteristics.
  3. Reasons for a refusal. A refused applicant can find out why, and the reason is specific (“debt payments are above our limit for your income”), not vague (“you do not qualify”).
  4. Accurate records. Information held about a borrower’s credit history can be checked and, if wrong, corrected.
  5. Fair collection. People who owe money are not harassed, threatened or misled about what they owe.
  6. A way out. Many credit agreements carry a short cancellation right, and early repayment is often allowed, sometimes with a capped fee.

Varies by country: examples

European Union

  • Before signing a consumer credit contract, the lender gives a standard information form, the Standard European Consumer Credit Information, showing the main features, the amount and cost of credit, the APR and the payments; a borrower who did not receive it can ask for it (Your Europe).
  • The borrower can withdraw within 14 calendar days of signing without giving a reason, and can repay early; any compensation to the lender for early repayment is capped (Your Europe).
  • A person who thinks a bank refused credit unfairly can ask the bank’s complaints office for the exact reasons in writing, then contact FIN-NET, the EU network of financial dispute bodies (Your Europe).
  • The EU adopted a revised Consumer Credit Directive in 2023 (EUR-Lex); how and when each country applies it is set by national law.

United States

  • The Truth in Lending Act, through Regulation Z, requires lenders to disclose the APR, amount financed, finance charge, total of payments and payment schedule in writing before the borrower is committed (Regulation Z).
  • The Equal Credit Opportunity Act prohibits discrimination in credit based on race, colour, religion, national origin, sex, marital status, age (for those old enough to contract) or receipt of public assistance (15 U.S.C. 1691). A refused applicant is entitled to a statement of the specific reasons (Regulation B, 12 CFR 1002.9).
  • The Fair Credit Reporting Act gives the right to dispute inaccurate information on a credit report; the bureau reinvestigates, generally within 30 days (15 U.S.C. 1681i). Accurate negative information can be reported until its legal limit, which is seven years for most items and ten years for bankruptcies (15 U.S.C. 1681c).
  • The Fair Debt Collection Practices Act limits third-party collectors: generally no calls before 8 a.m. or after 9 p.m. local time (15 U.S.C. 1692c), no abusive or threatening language and no misrepresenting the amount owed (Regulation F), and a right to written verification of the debt (15 U.S.C. 1692g).

Free annual credit reports in the US are available from AnnualCreditReport.com, the only website authorised by the federal government for this (USAGov).

United Kingdom

  • Complaints about lenders go first to the lender, which generally has up to eight weeks to respond, and then to the Financial Ombudsman Service (Financial Ombudsman Service).

Questions borrowers commonly ask a lender

  • What is the APR, and what is the total amount I will repay?
  • Which fees apply if I pay late, pay early or want to leave?
  • If my application is refused, what were the specific reasons, and was a credit report or automated system used?
  • Who do I complain to if something goes wrong, and which independent body handles unresolved complaints?

What people commonly do next

  • People who are refused credit commonly ask the lender, in writing, for the specific reasons and whether a credit report was used.
  • Credit reports are checked for errors, and errors are disputed with the credit bureau and the lender that reported them.
  • Loan documents, payment records, refusal letters and messages from collectors are kept together.
  • Unresolved complaints go to the financial ombudsman, dispute body or regulator in the borrower's country.
