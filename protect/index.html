Protect

How common scams work, the warning signs, and the consumer rights that exist in many countries.

Think it’s a scam? Stop, check, ask

  1. 1

    1. Stop

    Do not pay, click a link or share a code. A real bank or agency does not rush you.

  2. 2

    2. Check

    Contact the company yourself, with a number or website you already know, not the one in the message.

  3. 3

    3. Ask

    Talk to someone you trust or your consumer authority. If you already paid, call your bank straight away.

Common scams

  • “Pig butchering”: long-con investment scams

    A stranger builds a friendship or romance over weeks or months, then introduces a “profitable” trading platform; the target deposits more and more until withdrawals are blocked.

    Read more →

  • Advance-fee fraud

    A promised prize, inheritance, loan, grant or job that requires a fee to be paid first. The fee is the scam; the promised money does not exist.

    Read more →

  • Bank impersonation and the “safe account” scam

    A caller claiming to be your bank's fraud team says your account is at risk and asks you to move money to a “safe” account that the caller controls.

    Read more →

  • Charity and disaster scams

    Fake appeals after floods, earthquakes, wars or around holidays, and fake fundraisers, that take donations meant for people in need.

    Read more →

  • Crypto scams

    Fake exchanges and trading apps, wallet-draining links, “giveaways”, abandoned projects and celebrity deepfake adverts that use the fact that crypto transfers cannot be reversed.

    Read more →

  • Fake invoices and payment redirection

    Emails or messages that look like they come from a supplier, landlord, lawyer, builder or other business you deal with, asking you to pay to “new” bank details.

    Read more →

  • Fake job offers and task scams

    Jobs that require paying for training, equipment or “verification”, task apps that pay small sums and then require deposits, and “jobs” that involve receiving and forwarding money.

    Read more →

  • Government, tax office and police impersonation

    Calls, texts or emails that claim to come from a tax office, benefits agency, court or the police and threaten arrest, deportation, fines or stopped benefits unless you pay at once, often by gift card, transfer or crypto.

    Read more →

  • Identity theft and account takeover

    Criminals use leaked or stolen personal details to open accounts in your name or take control of yours, often through password reuse, phishing or a SIM swap.

    Read more →

  • Investment scams and “guaranteed returns”

    Offers of high, steady returns with little or no risk, through fake trading platforms, copies of real firms (clone firms) or unregistered schemes.

    Read more →

  • Loan-fee and debt-relief scams

    “Guaranteed” loans that require an upfront fee, and companies that promise to erase debts or clean a credit history in return for a payment.

    Read more →

  • Phishing by email, text and phone

    Fake messages or calls that imitate a bank, delivery firm, tax office or employer to collect passwords, codes or card details.

    Read more →

  • Pump-and-dump and social-media stock tips

    Coordinated hype raises the price of a little-traded share or token so that the promoters can sell their holdings to the people who buy late.

    Read more →

  • Romance scams

    An online relationship, built on a fake profile, that leads to requests for money for emergencies, travel, customs fees or investments.

    Read more →

  • Subscriptions that start from “free” trials

    “Free” or very cheap trials that turn into expensive recurring charges, with the terms in small print and cancellation made hard to find.

    Read more →

  • Tech support and “virus” scams

    Pop-ups or calls that claim a computer or phone is infected or hacked, leading to remote access, payment for fake repairs or theft from bank accounts.

    Read more →

Your rights

  • Automated decisions and your dataWhat rights people have when a computer system, including artificial intelligence (AI), decides or helps decide something about their money: a loan, a credit limit, a fraud block or a price.
  • Chargebacks and payment protectionHow money can sometimes be recovered through a card provider or payment service when a seller does not deliver, a product is not as described, or a payment was not authorised.
  • Cooling-off periodsTime-limited rights to cancel certain contracts without giving a reason: purchases made at a distance or at the door, and some credit agreements.
  • Making a complaint and escalating itThe usual ladder for a consumer problem: the company's own complaints process, then a free ombudsman or dispute body, a regulator, and, as a last step, a small-claims court.
  • Returns and refunds: the principlesWhat buyers can generally expect when goods are faulty, not as described, or simply unwanted, and why the answer depends on where and how you bought them.
  • Unfair terms and hidden feesTerms in standard contracts that consumer law may not enforce, and the rules on showing the full price and fees before you agree.
  • Warranties and guaranteesThe difference between the legal guarantee that comes from the law, a manufacturer's or seller's warranty, and a paid extended warranty.
  • Your rights when borrowingRights that borrowers have in many countries: clear cost information before signing, fair treatment, reasons for a refusal, correction of credit-report errors, and limits on how debts are collected.

Veritane is education, not advice. Nothing here is a recommendation to buy, sell, borrow, invest or take any specific action. Rules, products and protections vary by country. For decisions about your own situation, consider a licensed professional or a free public advice service in your country. Reporting routes and legal protections vary by country.
