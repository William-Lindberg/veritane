A

Adverse action notice
A written explanation that a lender in some countries is required to give when it refuses credit or offers less favourable terms, stating the main reasons.
Adverse selection
When the people keenest to buy a product, such as insurance, are the ones most likely to claim, because they know more about their own risk than the seller does.
Amortisation
Paying off a loan in scheduled instalments, where early payments are mostly interest and later payments are mostly principal.
Anchoring
The tendency for the first number a person sees to pull their estimate or willingness to pay towards it.
Annual percentage rate (APR)
The yearly cost of borrowing as a percentage, usually including interest and most fees, with exact rules that vary by country.
Annual percentage yield (APY)
The yearly return on savings once compounding is included, so accounts that add interest at different frequencies can be compared fairly.
Annualised rate
A change over a short period scaled up as if it continued for a full year, which magnifies both trends and noise.
Annuity
A contract that turns a lump sum into a stream of regular payments, often guaranteed for life.
Appreciation
An increase in the value of an asset or a currency over time.
Arbitrage
Making a profit from a price difference for the same thing in two places at the same time.
Asset
Anything owned that has value, such as cash, property, investments or a car.
Asset allocation
How savings are split between types of assets such as cash, bonds, shares and property.
Asset class
A broad group of investments that tend to behave in similar ways, such as shares, bonds, cash or property.
Asymmetric information
A situation in which one side of a deal knows more than the other, such as a seller who knows about a car's faults.
Auto-enrolment
A system in which workers are placed into a pension scheme by default unless they choose to opt out.
Automatic stabiliser
A tax or benefit that cushions the economy without any new decision, such as unemployment benefit payments rising in a downturn.
Avalanche method
Repaying debts by putting any extra money towards the debt with the highest interest rate first, which keeps total interest as low as possible.
Average (mean)
The total divided by the number of values, which a few very large or very small values can pull far from the typical value.

B

Bail-in
Rescuing a failing bank by writing down or converting the claims of its investors and creditors instead of using public money.
Balance of payments
The full account of a country's transactions with the rest of the world: trade, income, transfers and financial flows.
Balance sheet
A snapshot of what is owned and what is owed at one point in time.
Balloon payment
A large final payment due at the end of a loan, after a series of smaller regular payments.
Bank capital
The owners' own money in a bank, which absorbs losses before depositors or other creditors lose anything.
Bank run
Many depositors withdrawing their money at once because they worry the bank will fail, which can itself cause the failure.
Bankruptcy
A legal process in which the debts of a person or company that cannot pay are dealt with under court or agency supervision, often ending in release from them.
Base effect
A change in a year-on-year rate caused by an unusual value twelve months earlier rather than by anything new.
Base rate
Another name for the policy rate: the interest rate a central bank sets.
Basis point
One hundredth of a percentage point, so 25 basis points equal 0.25 percentage points.
Bear market
A sustained fall in prices in a financial market, often defined as a drop of 20 % or more from a recent peak.
Benchmark rate
A published reference interest rate to which variable-rate loans and other contracts are tied.
Beneficiary
The person or organisation that receives the payout from an insurance policy, pension, account or will.
Bid-ask spread
The gap between the price buyers will pay and the price sellers will accept, which is a cost to whoever trades.
Bond
A loan to a government or company that pays interest and returns the original amount on a set date.
Bond yield
The yearly return a bond gives at its current price, which rises when the price falls.
Bracket creep
Inflation pushing incomes into higher tax brackets when the thresholds are not raised in line with prices.
Break-even point
The level of sales at which revenue equals total cost, so profit is zero.
Broad money
Cash plus most bank deposits: the wide measure of the money in an economy.
Broker
An intermediary who arranges a product or a trade for a client and is paid by fee, commission or both.
Bubble
A period when prices rise far above any reasonable estimate of value, sustained by expectations of further rises.
Budget
A plan for how income will be spent and saved over a period.
Budget deficit
The amount by which a government's spending exceeds its revenue in a year.
Bull market
A sustained rise in prices in a financial market.
Business cycle
The repeated but irregular pattern of expansion and contraction in an economy.
Buy now, pay later (BNPL)
Splitting a purchase into a few instalments, often without interest but with fees if payments are missed.

C

Cap-and-trade
A scheme that sets a limit on total emissions and lets firms buy and sell permits within it, so the price of a permit adjusts.
Capital
Money or assets used to produce income, or the buffer of owners' money a bank holds against losses.
Capital gain
The profit from selling something for more than it cost.
Capital gains tax
A tax on the profit made when an asset is sold, with rules that vary by country.
Carbon price
A charge per tonne of emissions, set directly as a tax or indirectly through tradeable permits, so that the cost of emissions to others shows up in prices.
Cartel
An agreement between competing sellers to fix prices or share out markets, which is illegal in most jurisdictions.
Cash-flow statement
The financial statement that shows cash received and paid in a period, split into operating, investing and financing activities.
Central bank
The institution that issues a country's or region's currency, sets the policy interest rate and often supervises banks.
Chargeback
A reversal of a card payment, requested through the card provider, when goods or services were not delivered as agreed.
Collateral
An asset a borrower pledges that the lender can take if the loan is not repaid.
Commission
A payment to a seller or adviser that depends on what the customer buys, which can create a conflict of interest.
Commodity
A raw material traded in bulk, such as oil, wheat or gold.
Comparative advantage
The idea that two trading partners can both gain when each produces what it gives up least to make.
Compound interest
Interest earned on both the original amount and the interest already added.
Consumer confidence index
A survey-based measure of how households feel about their finances and the economy, used as an early signal of spending.
Consumer price index (CPI)
A measure of the prices of a basket of goods and services that households buy, used to track inflation.
Consumer surplus
The gap between what buyers would have been willing to pay and what they actually paid.
Contactless
Paying by tapping a card or phone on a reader, usually with a limit per transaction that varies by country.
Contagion
The spread of financial trouble from one institution or market to others through shared exposures, counterparties or loss of confidence.
Contribution margin
Price minus variable cost per unit: what each sale contributes towards fixed costs and profit.
Cooling-off period
A set number of days after signing certain contracts during which the buyer can cancel without penalty.
Core inflation
Inflation excluding items with volatile prices, usually food and energy, to show the underlying trend.
Correlation
How closely two things move together; assets with low correlation can diversify each other.
Correspondent bank
A bank that holds accounts for other banks so that payments can pass between countries where they have no branch.
Cost of living
The amount of money needed to cover basic expenses in a given place and time.
Coupon
The fixed interest payment a bond makes, usually stated as a percentage of its face value.
Credit
Using money now with a promise to repay it later, usually with interest.
Credit bureau
A company or public registry that collects information about how people repay their debts.
Credit limit
The maximum amount that can be borrowed on a card or line of credit.
Credit report
The file of a person's borrowing and repayment history held by a credit bureau or registry.
Credit score
A number summarising a credit report that lenders use to judge risk; the models and scales vary by country and lender.
Credit union
A member-owned cooperative that provides savings, loans and other banking services; names and rules vary by country.
Crypto asset
A digital token recorded on a shared ledger, usually not backed by any institution and with prices that can move a great deal.
Currency peg
A policy of holding a currency's exchange rate fixed to another currency or to a basket of currencies.
Current account (banking)
An everyday account for receiving income and making payments; also called a checking or transaction account.
Current account (economy)
A country's balance of trade in goods and services plus income flows and transfers with the rest of the world.
Custody
Who holds an asset on someone's behalf, and what happens to it if that holder fails.

D

Dark pattern
A design choice in an app or website that steers or pressures users into decisions they would not otherwise make.
Deadweight loss
The value of trades that no longer happen because of a tax, price control or monopoly, which nobody receives.
Debit card
A card that pays directly from the holder's own account balance.
Debt consolidation
Combining several debts into one loan, which can lower the rate, lengthen the term, or both.
Debt-to-GDP ratio
Government debt divided by the size of the economy, used to compare debt burdens across countries and over time.
Debt-to-income ratio
Monthly debt payments divided by monthly income, used by lenders to judge affordability.
Deductible (excess)
The part of an insured loss that the policyholder pays before the insurer pays the rest.
Default
Failing to keep to the terms of a loan, above all by missing payments; in other contexts, the option that applies when no choice is made.
Default (choice)
What happens if a person does nothing, which has a large influence on what people end up with.
Default (loan)
Failing to make loan payments as agreed, which can trigger penalties, collection and damage to a credit file.
Defined benefit pension
A pension that promises a set income, usually based on salary and years of service.
Defined contribution pension
A pension in which contributions build a pot whose final value depends on investment returns.
Deflation
A general fall in prices over time.
Deflator
A price index used to convert a nominal series into a real one.
Deposit insurance
A scheme that repays depositors up to a limit if their bank fails.
Depreciation
The loss of value of an asset over time, or a fall in a currency's exchange rate.
Derivative
A contract whose value depends on something else, such as a share, an index, an interest rate or a commodity.
Direct debit
A permission an account holder gives a company to take agreed payments from their account.
Discharge (of debt)
The legal release from the obligation to repay debts at the end of a bankruptcy or similar process.
Discount rate
The rate used to convert future money into its value today.
Disinflation
Inflation slowing down while prices are still rising.
Disposable income
Income left after taxes and compulsory contributions.
Diversification
Spreading money across many investments so that no single failure causes a large loss.
Dividend
A share of profits that a company pays to its shareholders.
Dollar-cost averaging
Investing a fixed amount at regular intervals regardless of the price.
Dollarisation
Widespread use of a foreign currency instead of the national one, formally or informally, usually after a loss of confidence in the national currency.
Drawdown (investing)
The fall from a peak to a low point in the value of an investment.
Drawdown (pension)
Taking income from a retirement pot while leaving the rest invested.
Drip pricing
Showing a low price first and adding fees step by step during checkout.
Duration
A measure of how much a bond's price changes when interest rates change.

E

E-money
Value stored electronically, such as in a wallet app, which may not be protected in the same way as a bank deposit.
Elasticity
How much the quantity bought or sold changes, in percentage terms, when the price changes by one percent.
Emergency fund
Money set aside to cover essential costs during a loss of income or an unexpected bill.
Endowment effect
Valuing something more simply because one owns it.
Equilibrium price
The price at which the quantity buyers want equals the quantity sellers offer.
Equity (home)
The value of a property minus the mortgage still owed on it.
Equity (shares)
Ownership in a company through its shares.
ESG
Environmental, social and governance factors used to rate how a company manages those issues, which is not the same as its effect on the world.
Estate
Everything a person owns and owes at death, which is gathered, used to pay debts and then distributed.
Exchange rate
The price of one currency in terms of another.
Exchange-traded fund (ETF)
A fund that holds many assets and trades on an exchange like a single share.
Excise tax
A tax on specific goods such as fuel, alcohol or tobacco.
Executor
The person named in a will to carry out its instructions and administer the estate.
Expected value
The average outcome of a decision if it were repeated many times: each result weighted by its probability.
Exposure
How much of someone's money is affected by a particular risk.
Externality
A cost or benefit from an activity that falls on people who are not part of the transaction, such as pollution or vaccination.

F

Fee drag
The reduction in long-term returns caused by ongoing charges.
Fiat money
Money that has value because a government declares it legal tender and people accept it, not because it can be exchanged for a commodity such as gold.
Fiscal multiplier
How much total output changes for each unit of extra government spending or tax cut; estimates vary widely with conditions.
Fiscal policy
Government decisions on spending and taxation.
Fixed cost
An expense that stays the same each period, such as rent.
Fixed rate
An interest rate that does not change for an agreed period.
Float (currency)
An exchange rate set by market supply and demand rather than held at a fixed level.
Forward guidance
A central bank's statements about where it expects interest rates to go in future.
Framing
How a choice is worded or presented, which can change decisions without changing the facts.
Fraud
Deceiving someone to obtain money, goods or data.
Free cash flow
Cash from operations minus capital spending: the cash a business generates after maintaining and expanding its assets.
Free rider
Someone who benefits from a shared good without paying for it, which is why markets tend to under-supply public goods.
Fund
A pool of money from many investors, managed together and invested in many assets.

G

Game theory
The study of decisions where each side's best choice depends on what the others do.
GDP (gross domestic product)
The total value of everything produced in a country in a period.
GDP per capita
GDP divided by population, used to compare living standards.
Gig economy
Work arranged as short tasks or jobs, often through an app, usually on a self-employed basis.
Gini coefficient
A measure of inequality from 0 (everyone has the same) to 1, or 100 (one person has everything), most often applied to income.
Grace period
The time between a credit card statement and its due date during which paying the full balance avoids interest.
Greenwashing
Presenting a product, fund or company as more environmentally beneficial than the evidence supports.
Gross income
Income before taxes and deductions.
Gross margin
Revenue minus the direct cost of what was sold, as a share of revenue.
Guarantor
Someone who agrees to repay a loan or rent if the borrower or tenant does not.

H

Headline inflation
The overall inflation rate including all items in the price index.
Hedging
Taking a position that reduces the risk of another one, like insurance for an investment.
Herd behaviour
Copying what others do, which can push prices far from value.
Hire purchase
Paying for an item in instalments and owning it only after the last payment.
Household
One or more people living together and sharing finances: the basic unit in most economic statistics.
Hyperinflation
Extremely rapid inflation, conventionally 50 % a month or more, in which money loses its usefulness and prices are reset constantly.

I

Identity theft
Using someone's personal details to open accounts in their name or take over their existing accounts.
Income protection insurance
Insurance that pays part of a person's income if illness or injury stops them working.
Income statement
The financial statement showing revenue, costs and profit over a period; also called the profit and loss account.
Income-contingent loan
A loan repaid as a share of income above a threshold, used for education in some countries.
Index (statistics)
A series expressed relative to a base period whose value is fixed at 100.
Index fund
A fund that aims to match a market index rather than beat it, usually at low cost.
Indexation
Automatically adjusting a payment or threshold in line with inflation.
Inequality
How unevenly income or wealth is spread across people, measured with tools such as the Gini coefficient or the share held by the top 10 %.
Inflation
A general rise in prices over time, which reduces what money buys.
Inflation target
The inflation rate a central bank aims for, often around 2 % a year.
Inflation-linked bond
A bond whose payments rise with a price index.
Inheritance tax
A tax on what a person receives from an estate, or on the estate itself, which exists in some countries with thresholds and exemptions.
Insolvency
Being unable to pay debts as they fall due, or owing more than one owns; the condition that formal debt-relief processes deal with.
Instalment loan
A loan repaid in fixed regular payments over a set term.
Insurance premium
The regular price paid for insurance cover.
Interchange fee
The fee a merchant's bank pays the card issuer on each card payment.
Interest
The cost of borrowing money or the reward for lending it, expressed as a rate per period.
Interest rate
The price of borrowing or the reward for lending, written as a percentage of the amount per period, usually per year.
Interest-only loan
A loan where the regular payments cover only interest and the whole principal is due at the end.
Intestacy
Dying without a valid will, in which case fixed legal rules decide who inherits.
Investment
Putting money into something in the hope that it grows or produces income, with the chance of losing some of it.
Islamic finance
Financial products built to comply with Islamic law, which avoid interest and excessive uncertainty and share risk through asset-based contracts.

J

Joint account
An account owned by two or more people, each of whom is usually responsible for all of it.

K

Key facts document
A short standardised summary of a product's costs, terms and the ways money can be lost, which many countries require.
Keynesian economics
The school of thought holding that total demand can fall short for long periods and that government spending and monetary policy can lift output in a downturn.
KYC (know your customer)
The identity checks banks and other institutions carry out before opening an account.

L

Labour force
Everyone who is working or actively looking for work.
Lagging indicator
A statistic that changes after the economy has already turned.
Leading indicator
A statistic that tends to change before the economy as a whole does.
Lease
A contract to use something, such as a home or a car, for a period in exchange for payments.
Legal tender
Money that, by law, has to be accepted to settle a debt.
Lender of last resort
A central bank's role of lending to solvent banks that cannot borrow elsewhere during a run, against good collateral.
Leverage
Using borrowed money to increase exposure, which magnifies both gains and losses.
Liability
Money owed to someone else.
Liquidity
How quickly something can be turned into cash without losing value.
Loan-to-value (LTV)
The loan amount as a share of the property's value.
Loss aversion
Feeling losses more strongly than gains of the same size.

M

Macroprudential policy
Rules aimed at the stability of the whole financial system, such as limits on loan size relative to income or extra capital buffers in good times.
Margin (trading)
Money deposited to cover potential losses on a leveraged position.
Margin call
A demand to add money when a leveraged position has lost value, or have it closed.
Marginal cost
The cost of producing one more unit.
Marginal tax rate
The tax rate on the next unit of income earned.
Market capitalisation
A company's share price multiplied by its number of shares.
Maturity
The date on which a loan or bond has to be repaid.
Means test
A check of income or assets that decides whether someone qualifies for a benefit, and how much.
Median
The middle value when all values are lined up in order: half are above and half are below.
Mental accounting
Treating money differently depending on where it came from or what it is labelled for.
Microfinance
Small loans, savings and insurance for people without collateral or access to banks, often through group lending.
Minimum payment
The smallest amount a card issuer accepts each month, which clears the balance slowly.
Mobile money
Value stored with a phone-based provider and sent by phone, with cash paid in and taken out through local agents.
Monetarism
The school of thought holding that inflation is caused mainly by growth in the money supply and that policy works best through stable rules rather than discretion.
Monetary policy
A central bank's use of interest rates and other tools to influence inflation and the economy.
Money laundering
Disguising where illegally obtained money came from; the reason banks ask where funds come from.
Money market
The market for short-term lending between banks, governments and large firms.
Monopoly
A market with a single seller, which can set a higher price and lower output than a competitive market would.
Monopsony
A market with a single or dominant buyer, such as one large employer in a town, which can hold prices or wages down.
Moral hazard
Taking more risk because someone else bears the cost, such as after being insured or rescued.
Mortgage
A long-term loan secured on a property.
Murabaha
A cost-plus sale in Islamic finance, in which the financier buys an asset and sells it to the customer at a disclosed mark-up, paid in instalments.
Mutual fund (unit trust)
A fund priced once a day that investors buy into and sell back to the fund manager directly, rather than on an exchange.

N

Nash equilibrium
A set of choices in a game where no player can gain by changing only their own choice.
Natural experiment
A real-world event that assigns people to different conditions in a way close to random, letting researchers estimate a cause.
Negative equity
Owing more on a loan than the asset it is secured on is worth.
Net income
Income after taxes and deductions; take-home pay.
Net worth
Everything owned minus everything owed.
Nominal
Measured in current money, before adjusting for inflation.
Nudge
A change to how choices are presented that steers behaviour without removing any options.

O

Oligopoly
A market dominated by a few large sellers whose decisions depend on each other.
Ombudsman
An independent body that resolves complaints between consumers and companies, often free of charge; names vary by country.
Opportunity cost
What is given up by choosing one option over the next best one.
Option
A contract giving the right, but not the obligation, to buy or sell something at a set price by a set date.
Output gap
The difference between what an economy is producing and what it could produce at full capacity without pushing up inflation.
Overdraft
Spending more than an account holds, which a bank may allow in return for a fee or interest.

P

Participation rate
The share of the working-age population that is working or looking for work.
Pay-as-you-go pension
A public pension funded by today's workers' contributions rather than by a saved pot.
Payday loan
A small short-term loan with a very high effective interest rate, due on the next payday.
Pension
Regular income in retirement from the state, an employer scheme or personal savings.
Percentage point
The simple difference between two percentages; going from 2 % to 3 % is a rise of one percentage point, which is a 50 % increase.
Perfect competition
A model market with many small buyers and sellers of an identical product, in which no one can influence the price.
Phishing
Fake messages, calls or websites that pretend to come from a trusted organisation to get people to reveal passwords, codes or card details.
Policy rate
The interest rate a central bank sets, which influences other interest rates in the economy.
Portfolio
The whole collection of someone's investments.
Power of attorney
A legal document letting a chosen person manage someone's money or affairs if they cannot.
Present bias
Giving much more weight to the present than to the future, so plans change when the moment arrives.
Present value
What a future amount of money is worth today after discounting.
Price ceiling
A legal maximum price, which in the standard model causes a shortage when it is set below the market price.
Price discrimination
Charging different buyers different prices for the same thing according to what each will pay.
Price floor
A legal minimum price, which in the standard model causes a surplus when it is set above the market price.
Price-to-rent ratio
House prices divided by yearly rents, used to compare the cost of buying with the cost of renting.
Principal
The original amount borrowed or invested, before interest.
Probate
The legal process of proving a will and giving the executor authority to administer the estate; names vary by country.
Producer surplus
The gap between what sellers received and the lowest price they would have accepted.
Productivity
Output per hour or per worker, the main long-run driver of wages and living standards.
Progressive tax
A tax whose rate rises as income rises.
Public good
Something that one person's use does not reduce for others and that no one can be excluded from, such as street lighting.
Purchasing managers' index (PMI)
A survey index where 50 means as many firms report improvement as deterioration; above 50 signals expansion in breadth, not in size.
Purchasing power
The amount of goods and services a sum of money can buy.
Purchasing power parity (PPP)
An exchange rate that would make a basket of goods cost the same in two countries, used for fairer comparisons of living standards.

Q

Quantitative easing
A central bank buying bonds to lower long-term interest rates and add money to the financial system.
Quarter-on-quarter
The change from one three-month period to the next.

R

Rational expectations
The assumption that people form forecasts using all available information, so predictable policy is anticipated.
Real
Measured after removing the effect of inflation.
Real interest rate
The nominal interest rate minus inflation: what borrowing really costs or saving really earns.
Real wage
Pay adjusted for inflation, showing what it actually buys.
Rebalancing
Periodically returning a portfolio to its intended mix by selling what has grown and buying what has shrunk.
Recession
A broad decline in economic activity lasting more than a few months, dated differently by different countries.
Refinancing
Replacing a loan with a new one, usually to get a lower rate or a different term.
Regressive tax
A tax that takes a larger share of income from lower earners than from higher earners.
REIT (real-estate investment trust)
A listed company or fund that owns property and pays out most of its rental income to investors.
Remittance
Money sent by a migrant worker to family or others in another country.
Replacement rate
Retirement income as a share of income before retirement.
Representative APR
The rate that at least a set share of accepted applicants receive; others can be offered more.
Reserve requirement
The minimum share of deposits a bank must hold as reserves at the central bank or in cash, used in some countries as a policy tool.
Resolution (of a bank)
The managed handling of a failing bank by authorities so that essential services continue and losses fall on investors before taxpayers.
Return on equity (ROE)
Profit as a share of shareholders' equity, which rises with leverage as well as with a more profitable business.
Revision
A later change to a published statistic as more complete data arrive.
Revolving credit
Credit that can be borrowed, repaid and borrowed again up to a limit, such as a credit card.
Riba
Interest or usury, which Islamic finance prohibits.
Risk
The range of possible outcomes, including the chance of losing money.
Risk premium
The extra expected return for holding a riskier asset instead of a safe one.
Risk tolerance
How much ups and downs a person can accept emotionally, as distinct from how much loss they can afford.
Rotating savings group (ROSCA)
A group in which members contribute a fixed sum each round and take turns receiving the whole pot.
Rule of 72
A rough guide: dividing 72 by the yearly rate gives an estimate of how many years money takes to double.

S

Salary sacrifice
Giving up part of pay in exchange for a benefit such as pension contributions, with tax effects that vary by country.
Savings rate (household)
The share of disposable income that is not spent.
Scam
A scheme that deceives people into handing over money or personal information.
Seasonal adjustment
Removing regular calendar patterns from a data series so that underlying changes show.
Secured loan
A loan backed by an asset that the lender can take if the borrower does not repay.
Sequence risk
The danger that poor returns early in retirement permanently reduce how long savings last.
Share (stock)
A unit of ownership in a company.
Shrinkflation
Reducing a product's size while keeping its price, which is a hidden price rise.
Simple interest
Interest calculated only on the original amount, not on interest already added.
Sinking fund
Money set aside each month for a known future expense.
Snowball method
Repaying debts smallest balance first to build momentum.
Social insurance
Public schemes funded by contributions that pay out on defined events such as unemployment, sickness, old age or parenthood.
Social proof
Taking other people's behaviour as evidence of what is right, which marketers and scammers exploit.
Sovereign default
A government failing to make a payment on its debt as scheduled.
Stablecoin
A crypto token designed to hold a fixed value against a currency, which sometimes fails.
Stagflation
High inflation combined with low growth and high unemployment.
Standing order
An instruction to a bank to send a fixed payment regularly.
Statutory rights
Protections that the law gives buyers, whatever a seller's own policy says.
Stress test
Checking whether a budget, loan or bank survives a bad scenario such as higher interest rates.
Strong customer authentication
Requiring two independent proofs of identity, such as a password and a code on a phone, to approve a payment or log in.
Sudden stop
An abrupt halt in foreign money flowing into a country, which forces a sharp adjustment.
Sukuk
Certificates in Islamic finance that give holders a share in an asset or venture and its income, often compared with bonds.
Supply chain
The series of producers, transporters and sellers that brings a product to the buyer.
Systemic risk
The risk that trouble at one institution or market spreads to others and to the wider economy.

T

Takaful
Mutual insurance in Islamic finance, in which members pool contributions to cover each other's losses.
Tariff
A tax on imported goods, collected at the border and often passed on to buyers.
Tax avoidance
Legally arranging affairs to pay less tax.
Tax credit
An amount subtracted directly from the tax owed.
Tax deduction
An amount subtracted from income before tax is calculated.
Tax evasion
Illegally hiding income or giving false information to pay less tax.
Tax-advantaged account
An account where saving or investing is taxed less, under rules that vary by country.
Term (loan)
The length of time over which a loan is repaid.
Term deposit
Savings locked in for a fixed period in exchange for a set interest rate.
Term premium
The extra yield investors ask for lending over longer periods rather than rolling over short-term loans.
Terms of trade
The price of a country's exports relative to the price of its imports.
Time value of money
The principle that money available now is worth more than the same amount later.
Total cost of ownership
The purchase price plus all running, maintenance and disposal costs over an item's life.
Tracker rate
A variable interest rate set at a fixed margin above a benchmark.
Trade balance
Exports minus imports of goods, and of services when stated, over a period.
Tragedy of the commons
The over-use of a shared resource open to all, such as an unregulated fishery, because each user ignores the cost to others.
Transfer (bank)
Moving money electronically from one account to another.
Trimmed mean inflation
An inflation measure that drops the largest price rises and falls before averaging.
Two-factor authentication
Proving identity with two separate things, such as a password and a code on a phone.

Unbanked
Having no account at a bank or mobile-money provider.
Underemployment
Working fewer hours or at a lower level than a person wants or is qualified for.
Unemployment rate
The share of the labour force without work but available and looking for it.
Unit price
The price per standard quantity, such as per kilo or per litre.
Unsecured loan
A loan backed only by the borrower's promise to repay.

V

Value-added tax (VAT / GST)
A tax added at each stage of production and collected in the final price.
Variable cost
An expense that changes from period to period, such as groceries.
Variable rate
An interest rate that can change, often following a benchmark.
Vesting
The point at which employer pension contributions, or share awards, become fully the employee's.
Volatility
How much and how fast a price moves up and down.

W

Warranty
A seller's or maker's promise to repair or replace a product for a period.
Wealth tax

Will

Withdrawal rate

Withholding tax

Working capital

Yield

Yield curve
A chart of bond yields across maturities, read as a signal of expectations for interest rates and growth.
