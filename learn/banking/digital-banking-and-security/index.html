Digital Banking and Keeping Accounts Secure

How online and mobile banking prove who you are, and the habits and rules that limit account fraud.

Everyday·5 min (Simple) · 9 min (Detailed)·Updated 6 November 2026

Read first: Payments and Transfers

Key points

  • A second factor of proof, such as a code on your phone or a fingerprint, is the single largest protection against someone else getting into an account.
  • Genuine banks do not ask for a full password, PIN or one-time code by phone, text or email; a message that asks for one is a sign of fraud.
  • Looking through statements regularly, and reporting unknown payments quickly, limits losses.
  • Who pays for an unauthorised payment varies by country, but in almost every system reporting it quickly matters.
  • Updating phones and computers closes security gaps that criminals use.

Simple: plain words. Detailed: exact figures, codes and methods.

In short: online banking checks who you are with more than one kind of proof. Most account fraud gets around those checks by persuading the account holder to hand over the proof or to send the money themselves.

Simple version

Proving it is you

When you log in or pay online, the bank checks who you are. It can use three kinds of proof:

  • Something you know: a password or PIN.
  • Something you have: your phone or a card reader.
  • Something you are: a fingerprint or face scan.

Using two of these together is called two-factor authenticationTwo-factor authenticationProving identity with two separate things, such as a password and a code on a phone.Read more about Two-factor authentication. A thief who learns your password still cannot get in without your phone or your fingerprint. Security agencies describe it as one of the most effective ways to protect an account (NCSC).

What genuine banks do not ask for

Banks send codes so that you can confirm a login or payment. They do not need you to read those codes back to anyone. Security and consumer agencies describe requests like these as common signs of fraud (NCSC; FCA):

  • asking for your full password, PIN or a one-time code by phone, text or email;
  • asking you to move money to a “safe account” to protect it;
  • asking you to install an app that lets them see or control your screen;
  • pressing you to act within minutes.

A message can show your bank’s name and even come from a number that looks like your bank’s. Criminals can fake both. Calling the number on the back of your card, or opening the bank’s app yourself, reaches the real bank.

Looking at your statements

Unknown payments are easier to deal with when they are spotted early. Banking apps can send an alert for every payment, so unusual ones stand out. A small unknown payment can be a test by a criminal before a larger one.

The first hour after suspected fraud

People who suspect fraud commonly take these steps, in roughly this order:

  1. Contacting the bank through a number or app they already trust, and asking for the card or account to be frozen.
  2. Changing passwords, starting with email and banking, from a device they believe is safe.
  3. Writing down what happened: times, amounts, names and numbers used.
  4. Reporting it to the police or the national fraud-reporting service, where one exists.

The bank’s own fraud team can explain what happens next and whether the money can be recovered.

Keeping devices up to date

Phone and computer updates often fix security gaps that criminals use. Security agencies list updating devices and using long, unique passwords among the main steps for staying secure online (NCSC; CISA).

Authorised and unauthorised payments

There are two different kinds of fraud loss:

  • Unauthorised: someone else makes a payment from your account without your permission, for example with a stolen card.
  • Authorised: you are tricked into sending the money yourself, for example to a fake seller or to someone pretending to be the bank.

Many countries give clear legal protection for the first kind. Protection for the second kind is more limited and differs widely. The Pro layer gives examples.

Detailed version

Strong customer authentication

In the EU, PSD2 requires strong customer authentication (SCA) when a payer accesses an account online, starts an electronic payment, or does anything remotely that carries a risk of fraud (Directive (EU) 2015/2366). SCA uses at least two independent elements from knowledge, possession and inherence. For remote payments, the authentication code must be dynamically linked to the amount and payee, so that a code captured for one payment cannot be reused for another. Exemptions exist for low-value, low-risk and trusted-beneficiary payments (Delegated Regulation (EU) 2018/389). The UK retained an equivalent regime (FCA).

Liability frameworks for unauthorised transactions

Jurisdiction Rule (summary)
EU The payer’s liability for unauthorised payments from a lost or stolen instrument is capped at €50, unless the payer acted fraudulently or with gross negligence; providers refund unauthorised payments promptly (PSD2).
United States For electronic fund transfers, the consumer’s liability is limited to $50 if a lost card or code is reported within two business days, up to $500 if reported later, and can be unlimited for transfers shown on a statement and not reported within 60 days (Regulation E).
Australia The ePayments Code sets out when account holders are and are not liable for unauthorised transactions (ASIC).

Worked example (illustrative): ¤800 is taken with a stolen debit card. Under the US tiers, a report within two business days limits the holder’s share to $50 at most; a report after that but within 60 days of the statement raises the cap to $500. Under PSD2, the cap is €50 in either case unless gross negligence is shown. The difference shows why reporting time and the definition of negligence matter.

Authorised push-payment (APP) fraud

Liability rules for unauthorised payments do not, as a rule, cover payments the customer authorised, even when tricked. The United Kingdom introduced a mandatory reimbursement requirement for APP scams on Faster Payments, with sending and receiving firms sharing the cost (PSR). The EU’s instant-payment rules add a check that the payee’s name matches the account, which targets misdirected and impersonation payments. Elsewhere, recovery of authorised payments generally depends on voluntary bank policies or on tracing the funds.

Open-banking consent

Third-party providers in open-banking systems act on time-limited consent given through the customer’s bank. Consents can usually be reviewed and withdrawn in the bank’s app or with the provider. A provider’s access to account data does not include the customer’s banking password, which is entered only with the bank.

Common misconceptions

  • “A caller who knows my details must be my bank.” Personal data from leaks is widely available to criminals.
  • “Two-factor authentication makes fraud impossible.” It stops credential theft; it does not stop a customer being persuaded to approve a payment.
  • “The bank refunds every fraud.” Refund rights depend on whether the payment was authorised, the jurisdiction and the payment method.

Varies by country

Protection after fraud differs. In the EU, liability for unauthorised payments from a lost or stolen instrument is capped at €50 in most cases (PSD2). In the United States, the cap depends on how quickly the loss is reported (Regulation E). In the United Kingdom, victims of authorised push-payment scams on Faster Payments have a reimbursement right (PSR); in many other countries no equivalent rule exists. National fraud-reporting services also differ; Australia’s Scamwatch is one example (Scamwatch).

Why people disagree

Who should carry the loss when a customer is tricked into sending money? One view is that banks and payment firms are best placed to detect unusual payments and to stop money reaching criminal accounts, so making them reimburse victims gives them a reason to invest in prevention; the UK reimbursement requirement follows this reasoning (PSR). Another view is that automatic reimbursement reduces the care customers take, raises costs for all customers, and can draw in false claims, so protection is more usefully focused on prevention and on vulnerable customers. Countries are taking different positions, and the evidence is still building.
