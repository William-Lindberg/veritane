Is My Money Safe? Deposit Protection

What happens to your money if a bank fails, how deposit protection works, and where its limits are.

Everyday·4 min (Simple) · 8 min (Detailed)·Updated 6 November 2026

Read first: How Banks Work

Key points

  • Most countries protect bank deposits up to a limit per person, per bank.
  • The limit and what it covers vary by country; for example, the EU limit is €100,000 and the US standard limit is $250,000.
  • E-money, investments and crypto-assets are generally not covered by deposit protection.
  • Because the limit applies per bank, money held at more than one bank can be covered more than once.
  • Protection applies only if the institution is a member of the scheme, and brands that share one banking licence count as one bank.

Simple version

Why banks can fail

A bank lends out most of the money it holds. If many of its loans are not repaid, it can lose more than its owners put in. If many customers withdraw at once, it can run short of cash even when its loans are sound. Both can lead to a bank failing. The lesson on how banks work explains why.

Bank failures are uncommon, but they do happen.

What a protection scheme does

A deposit protection scheme (also called deposit insurance or a deposit guarantee scheme) is a fund, usually run by or for the government. Banks pay into it. If a member bank fails, the scheme pays its depositors back, up to a set limit. In many countries the payout is made within a few working days, or the accounts are moved to another bank.

These schemes have a second job. If people know their money is protected, they have less reason to rush to withdraw it. That makes bank runs less likely in the first place.

The limit

Each scheme has a maximum. The limit is usually per person, per bank.

Example

Suppose the limit is ¤100,000 (example).

  • You have ¤130,000 at one bank. If it fails, ¤100,000 is protected. The other ¤30,000 is not guaranteed; you become a creditor of the failed bank and may get some or all of it back later.
  • You have ¤70,000 at Bank A and ¤60,000 at Bank B. Each amount is under the limit at its own bank, so all ¤130,000 is protected.
  • You and a partner have ¤160,000 in a joint account. Many schemes treat each holder’s share separately: ¤80,000 each, so the whole amount is protected.

One detail matters: some banks run several brands under one licence. If two brands share a licence, the scheme counts them as one bank, and you get one limit, not two.

What is usually not covered

  • E-money held with a payment company that is not a bank. It is protected in other ways (the provider has to keep customers’ money separate), but not by deposit insurance.
  • Investments such as shares and funds. Their value can go down, and deposit protection does not cover that. Some countries have separate schemes if an investment firm fails.
  • Crypto-assets.
  • Deposits at a bank that is not a member of the scheme, such as some branches of foreign banks.

Checking membership

Banks that belong to a scheme usually say so on their website and in their account terms. Each national scheme also publishes a list of member banks.

Detailed version

Coverage examples

Jurisdiction Standard limit Source
European Union €100,000 per depositor per credit institution (harmonised) Directive 2014/49/EU
United States $250,000 per depositor, per insured bank, per ownership category FDIC
Japan ¥10 million of principal per depositor per institution, plus interest DICJ

Limits change: the United Kingdom raised its limit from £85,000 to £120,000 on 1 December 2025 (Bank of England). The scheme’s own website gives the current figure.

Joint accounts, ownership categories and temporary high balances

  • Joint accounts: the EU directive counts each depositor’s share of a joint account separately (Directive 2014/49/EU); the FDIC insures joint accounts per co-owner as a separate ownership category (FDIC).
  • Temporary high balances: the EU directive requires additional protection, for a limited period, for some balances linked to life events such as a home sale (Directive 2014/49/EU). The UK scheme also covers temporary high balances (FSCS).
  • Cross-border branches: in the EU a branch of a bank from another member state is covered by its home-country scheme; branches of banks from outside the EU are treated under national rules.

Funding

Schemes are funded by contributions from member banks, often risk-based, either before failures (ex ante) or after (ex post), usually with a government or central bank backstop. The EU directive sets a target fund size as a share of covered deposits (Directive 2014/49/EU). International good practice is set out in the IADI Core Principles (IADI).

Resolution and bail-in

Payout is one option. Authorities can also resolve a failing bank: sell it, transfer its deposits to another bank, or restructure it while it keeps operating. The FSB Key Attributes set international standards for these powers (FSB). A central tool is bail-inBail-inRescuing a failing bank by writing down or converting the claims of its investors and creditors instead of using public money.Read more about Bail-in: shareholders and some creditors absorb losses by having their claims written down or converted into shares. In the EU, covered deposits are excluded from bail-in, and deposits of individuals and small firms above the limit rank ahead of ordinary unsecured creditors (Directive 2014/59/EU; SRB).

History in brief

The United States created the FDIC in 1933, after thousands of bank failures in the early 1930s (FDIC). Theory later formalised the idea: in the Diamond–Dybvig model, deposit insurance removes the incentive for each depositor to withdraw before others, so runs do not start (Diamond and Dybvig, 1983).

Common misconceptions

  • “Two apps, two limits.” Brands under one licence share one limit.
  • “Everything at my bank is covered.” Investment products sold by a bank are not deposits.
  • “Money above the limit is lost.” It becomes a claim in the insolvency or resolution; how much is recovered depends on the value of the failed bank’s assets.

Varies by country

Limits, payout times and coverage differ. The EU sets a common limit of €100,000 (Directive 2014/49/EU). The United States covers $250,000 per depositor, per bank, per ownership category (FDIC). Japan covers ¥10 million of principal plus interest per depositor per institution (DICJ). Some countries also cover credit unions or cooperative banks under separate schemes.

Why people disagree

How generous should deposit protection be? Supporters of high limits argue that broad protection prevents runs and protects households and small firms that cannot judge a bank’s soundness, which is the logic of the Diamond–Dybvig model (Diamond and Dybvig, 1983). Critics point to moral hazardMoral hazardTaking more risk because someone else bears the cost, such as after being insured or rescued.Read more about Moral hazard: when depositors are fully protected, they have less reason to watch how much risk banks take, and banks may take more. The IADI Core Principles address this by recommending limited, credible coverage combined with supervision and risk-based contributions (IADI).
