Types of Accounts

Current (checking) accounts, savings accounts, term deposits, joint accounts and mobile wallets: what each one is for.

Everyday·5 min (Simple) · 9 min (Detailed)·Updated 6 November 2026

Read first: How Banks Work

Key points

  • Everyday accounts are built for access; savings accounts are built for interest; term deposits lock money away for a fixed time in return for a higher rate.
  • The names of accounts vary by country, but the jobs they do are much the same everywhere.
  • A joint account means joint responsibility: each holder can use the money, and in many countries each can be liable for any debt on it.
  • Mobile money and e-wallets are often not bank deposits, so they can be protected differently from a bank account.

Simple: plain words. Detailed: exact figures, codes and methods.

In short: different accounts trade access against interest. The more freely you can take money out, the less interest the account tends to pay.

Simple version

One trade-off behind every account

A bank account is a place to hold money. Every type of account balances two things:

  • Access: how quickly and easily you can take the money out or pay with it.
  • Interest: how much the bank pays you for holding it.

Banks can pay more interest when they know the money will stay put for a while. So, as a rule of thumb, more access means less interest.

Interest on savings: a quick example

Example

You put ¤10,000 (example) in a one-year term deposit at 3% a year. After a year you receive ¤300 of interest.

If you need the money after a few months, the bank may charge a penalty. A common type is a set number of days of interest. A penalty of 90 days’ interest on ¤10,000 at 3% is about ¤74.

Some savings accounts pay different rates on different slices of the balance. These are called tiered rates. For example (illustrative), 1% on the first ¤1,000 and 3% on everything above. On a ¤5,000 balance that gives ¤10 + ¤120 = ¤130 a year.

Joint accounts: shared money, shared responsibility

With a joint account, each holder can usually take money out without asking the others. If the account goes overdrawn, the bank can often ask any holder to pay the whole debt. When a relationship ends, the account does not split itself; the holders have to agree what happens to it.

Mobile money and e-wallets

In many countries, people pay and send money with a phone. Some of these services are run by banks. Others are run by phone companies or payment firms. Money held with a non-bank provider is often e-money: a stored value that the provider has to keep safe, but which is not a bank deposit. That difference matters for protection if the provider fails. The lesson on deposit protection explains why.

Detailed version

How savings interest is calculated

Most savings interest is calculated on the daily closing balance and paid monthly or yearly. With a nominal annual rate r and a 365-day year:

interest for the period = Σ (daily balance × r ÷ 365)

Example (illustrative): at 2%, a balance of ¤3,000 for 20 days and ¤5,000 for 10 days earns 3,000 × 0.02 × 20/365 + 5,000 × 0.02 × 10/365 = ¤3.29 + ¤2.74 = ¤6.03 for the 30 days.

Other conventions to look for:

  • Tiered rates: each slice of the balance earns its own rate (blended), or the whole balance earns the rate of the tier it falls in (whole-balance). The same headline rates give different results.
  • Introductory or bonus rates: a higher rate for a fixed period or conditional on no withdrawals, after which a lower standard rate applies.
  • Effective rate: an annual equivalent rate or annual percentage yieldAnnual percentage yield (APY)The yearly return on savings once compounding is included, so accounts that add interest at different frequencies can be compared fairly.Read more about Annual percentage yield (APY) includes compounding, so accounts paying interest at different frequencies can be lined up. In the United States, Regulation DD requires an annual percentage yield to be disclosed for deposit accounts (eCFR, 12 CFR 1030).

Notice periods and early-withdrawal penalties

Term deposits and notice accounts price the bank’s certainty about funding. Penalties are usually expressed as forgone interest (for example, 90 days of interest); some products do not allow early withdrawal at all. The break-even question is whether the extra rate over the term exceeds the expected cost of breaking it.

E-money versus deposits

In the European Union, e-money institutions issue electronic money against funds received, may not pay interest on it, and must safeguard customers’ funds, for example in a segregated account or by insurance (Directive 2009/110/EC). Safeguarding is not deposit insurance: the EU deposit guarantee rules cover deposits at credit institutions (Directive 2014/49/EU). Mobile-money systems elsewhere use comparable trust or safeguarding arrangements under the national payments regulator, for example the Central Bank of Kenya (CBK).

Basic-account rights

The EU Payment Accounts Directive gives consumers legally resident in the EU a right to a payment account with basic features, sets rules on fee transparency, and supports account switching (Directive 2014/92/EU). Other countries use voluntary schemes, state-backed accounts or no specific right. Account ownership varies widely worldwide; the World Bank’s Global Findex survey measures it country by country (World Bank).

Common misconceptions

  • “The higher headline rate pays more.” Effective rates, tier rules, conditions and how long the rate lasts can reverse the ranking.
  • “A wallet balance is the same as a bank balance.” Legal status and protection can differ.
  • “Joint means half each.” Rights to withdraw and liability for debts are usually joint and several, not split.

Varies by country

Account names and rules differ. A “current account” in the United Kingdom is a “checking account” in the United States and a “transaction account” in Australia. In the European Union, consumers have a legal right to a basic payment account (Directive 2014/92/EU). In Kenya and many other countries, mobile-money accounts run by non-banks are a common way to hold and send money, under rules set by the central bank (CBK). Identity documents accepted for opening an account also differ from country to country.
