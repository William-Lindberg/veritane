Pricing Techniques and How to See Them

The standard techniques sellers use to present prices, what each one does to the buyer's choice, and where rules apply.

Everyday·6 min (Simple) · 9 min (Professional)·Updated 6 November 2026

Key points

  • Common techniques include charm prices (¤9.99), “was” prices, decoy options, fees added late (drip pricing), changing and personalised prices, scarcity messages, bundles and loyalty schemes.
  • Each technique works on how people compare and notice prices, not on the product itself.
  • Knowing a technique reduces its effect but does not remove it; it works on experts too.
  • Some practices are regulated, such as rules on “was” prices and bans on hidden fees in some countries.
  • The total price, the unit price and the price over time answer most questions a technique raises.
Depth:

In short: the way a price is shown can change what people choose, even when the product is the same. Each common technique has a simple question that shows the underlying price.

Simple version

Ten common techniques

Sellers present prices in ways that affect what people buy. None of these is hidden knowledge; they are taught in marketing courses. Here is what each one does.

  1. Charm prices. ¤9.99 instead of ¤10. People read from the left, so the first digit counts most. The price feels closer to ¤9 than to ¤10.
  2. “Was” prices. “Was ¤80, now ¤50.” The first number becomes a reference point, called an anchorAnchoringThe tendency for the first number a person sees to pull their estimate or willingness to pay towards it.Read more about Anchoring. The discount feels large whatever the item is worth.
  3. Decoy options. A third option is added mainly to make another one look attractive. See the coffee example below.
  4. Drip pricing. A low price is shown first; fees are added step by step, often at the last page of checkout. See the ticket example below.
  5. Changing prices. Prices change with demand, time of day or how close an event is. Plane tickets and taxi rides often work this way.
  6. Personalised prices. Different customers see different prices or offers, based on what the seller knows about them.
  7. Scarcity and urgency messages. “Only 2 left”, “Offer ends in 10:00”. These make quick decisions more likely, and leave less time to check other shops.
  8. Bundles. Several items for one price. The price of each part becomes harder to see.
  9. Loyalty schemes. Points, levels and member prices make it more costly to switch shops.
  10. “Free” with conditions. A free item that starts a paid subscription, or free delivery above a minimum spend.

Example

Decoy (example). A café sells a small coffee for ¤2.50 and a large for ¤4.00. Then it adds a medium for ¤3.80.

Next to the medium, the large looks like a small extra step: only ¤0.20 more. More customers choose the large, even though nothing about the large has changed.

Example

Drip pricing (example). A concert ticket is shown at ¤40. At checkout, a ¤4.50 booking fee, a ¤3.00 service fee and ¤2.50 delivery are added.

The total is ¤50, which is 25% more than the price first shown. By the last page the buyer has spent time choosing seats and entering details, which makes stopping feel costly.

Knowing helps, but not fully

These techniques work on the way all human minds compare numbers. Knowing about them helps, but experts respond to them too. They are not a sign of a careless buyer.

A checkout checklist

A few questions show the price behind each technique:

  • What is the total, including every fee, tax and delivery charge?
  • What is the price per unit, for example per kg or per month?
  • What did this cost last month, here and elsewhere?
  • What happens after the offer: does a trial turn into a subscription?
  • Without the “was” price or the third option, would this still be the choice?

Professional version

Reference-dependent preferences

Prospect theory, developed by Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky, holds that people judge outcomes as gains or losses relative to a reference point, and that losses weigh more than equal gains (Nobel Prize, 2002). Several pricing techniques act on the reference point: a “was” price raises it, so the actual price registers as a gain; a decoy adds a comparison that changes which option seems reasonable; partitioned and drip pricing separate a total into parts, each judged against a lower reference.

Left-digit bias (charm pricing) reflects limited attention: readers process the leading digit more than the trailing ones.

Degrees of price discrimination

Economists classify price discrimination by how much the seller knows:

Degree Mechanism Example
First A price for each buyer, close to that buyer’s willingness to pay Personalised online offers
Second Buyers sort themselves among options Pack sizes, tiers, quantity discounts
Third Different prices for observable groups Student, senior or regional prices

Price discrimination can raise or lower total welfare: it can expand sales to buyers who would not pay a single uniform price, and it transfers surplus from buyers to sellers.

Dynamic and algorithmic pricing

Dynamic pricing changes prices with demand and supply conditions, as with airline seats. Algorithmic pricing uses software to set prices, often in response to competitors’ prices. Competition authorities study whether pricing algorithms that react to each other can sustain higher prices without explicit agreement; this remains an area of research and enforcement interest. The UK Competition and Markets Authority reviews these and other “online choice architecture” practices (CMA).

Regulatory examples

  • Reference prices: in the EU, any announcement of a price reduction is required to show the lowest price applied in the prior 30 days or more (Directive (EU) 2019/2161).
  • Drip pricing: the UK Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024 lists drip pricing among practices treated as unfair in all circumstances (legislation.gov.uk). Australian consumer law requires a single total price to be shown prominently where a component price is advertised (Federal Register of Legislation).
  • Personalised pricing disclosure: the same EU directive requires traders to inform consumers when a price has been personalised on the basis of automated decision-making (Directive (EU) 2019/2161).

Common misconceptions

  • “A discount from the ‘was’ price is a saving.” The saving is against what the item would otherwise cost, which the “was” price may not reflect.
  • “Only careless buyers are affected.” The effects come from features of attention and comparison that everyone shares.

Why people disagree

Economists differ on how far regulation of pricing techniques is useful. One view holds that disclosure rules and bans on practices such as drip pricing make prices comparable and strengthen competition. Another view holds that some techniques, such as dynamic pricing and price discrimination, also let sellers fill capacity and serve buyers who would otherwise be priced out, and that broad rules can remove those benefits. Competition authorities weigh both effects case by case (CMA).

Varies by country

In the European Union, a price reduction is required to show the lowest price of the prior 30 days (EUR-Lex). In the United Kingdom, drip pricing is listed among banned practices in a 2024 law (legislation.gov.uk). In Australia, a total price is required where a part price is advertised. In the United States, a federal rule requires the total price up front for live-event tickets and short-term lodging, and other rules differ by state.
