Wages, Real Pay and Why a Raise Can Be a Cut

Nominal and real wages, how pay is set, and the arithmetic of a pay rise and of comparing two job offers.

Everyday·5 min (Simple) · 9 min (Detailed)·Updated 6 November 2026

Read first: Prices and Inflation at Home

Key points

  • A 3% pay rise when prices rise 5% is a real pay cut: the same work buys about 2% less.
  • Wages reflect productivity, bargaining power, how scarce a skill is and local norms.
  • Minimum wages and collective bargaining differ widely between countries.
  • Total pay includes pension contributions, benefits and paid leave, not only the salary.
  • Two job offers compare more fully after tax, after work-related costs and per hour worked.

Simple: plain words. Detailed: exact figures, codes and methods.

In short: what matters about pay is what it buys. A pay rise that is smaller than the rise in prices means you can buy less than before.

Simple version

Nominal pay and real pay

Your nominal wage is the number on your payslip. Your real wageReal wagePay adjusted for inflation, showing what it actually buys.Read more about Real wage is what that number can buy, after taking price rises into account.

Example

You earn ¤2,000 a month (example). You get a 3% pay rise, so you now earn ¤2,060. Over the same year, prices rise 5%.

What used to cost ¤2,000 now costs ¤2,100. Your new pay buys 2,060 ÷ 2,100 = about 98% of what your old pay bought. Your real pay fell by about 1.9%, even though the number on your payslip went up.

A quick shortcut: real change ≈ pay rise − inflation. Here, 3% − 5% = about −2%. The exact answer is a little smaller, but the shortcut is close when the numbers are small.

What decides a wage?

Many things push pay up or down:

  • Productivity: how much value an hour of work produces. Improved tools, training and organisation raise it.
  • Bargaining power: whether the worker or the employer is in a stronger position to walk away. Unions, the number of employers nearby and how easy it is to change jobs all matter.
  • Scarcity: skills that few people have, and that many employers want, tend to be paid more.
  • Local norms and laws: minimum wages, collective agreements and what is usual in a sector or region.

What is in a pay packet

Salary is only part of the total. Other parts can be worth a lot:

  • contributions your employer pays into a pension for you,
  • paid holidays and pay during illness,
  • health cover, meals, transport or other benefits,
  • overtime rules and bonuses.

Some costs come out of pay too: tax and social contributions, and work costs such as commuting, uniforms or tools.

Comparing two job offers

Two offers that look different on salary can come out very close once everything is counted.

Example

Offer A pays ¤40,000 a year for 37.5 hours a week. The employer adds 5% to a pension (¤2,000). The commute costs ¤1,200 a year.

Offer B pays ¤42,000 a year for 40 hours a week. The employer adds 2% to a pension (¤840). The commute costs ¤3,000 a year.

Both have the same paid leave, so you work 46 weeks a year in each (example).

  • A: ¤40,000 + ¤2,000 − ¤1,200 = ¤40,800, over 1,725 hours = about ¤23.65 an hour.
  • B: ¤42,000 + ¤840 − ¤3,000 = ¤39,840, over 1,840 hours = about ¤21.65 an hour.

The offer with the higher salary pays less per hour once pension, commute and hours are counted. This is before tax; tax can change the gap again.

Detailed version

Real wage indices

Statistics offices publish average earnings as an index. The real wage index divides a nominal earnings index by a price index, both on the same base:

real wage index_t = nominal earnings index_t ÷ CPI_t × 100
real growth = (1 + nominal growth) ÷ (1 + inflation) − 1

With 3% nominal growth and 5% inflation: 1.03 ÷ 1.05 − 1 = −1.905%. The choice of deflator matters: a consumer price index measures what workers can buy, while the GDP deflator (prices of everything produced) relates pay to what firms receive. Rebasing either index changes the levels shown, not the growth rates (ONS).

Average earnings series also shift with the mix of jobs: if many low-paid jobs disappear, the average rises even if no individual’s pay changes. Analysts therefore also look at medians, fixed-composition indices and pay growth for people who stay in the same job.

Productivity and the labour share

Over long periods, real wages are linked to labour productivity, measured as output per hour or per worker (Eurostat). The labour share is the part of national income paid to workers:

labour share = compensation of employees ÷ GDP   (often adjusted for self-employed income)

Compensation of employees in national accounts includes wages and employers’ social contributions, which is why it exceeds payslip totals (Eurostat).

Wage-price dynamics

When prices rise, workers seek higher pay to restore real wages; when wages rise faster than productivity, unit labour costs rise and firms may pass that on into prices. Central banks watch wage growth minus productivity growth as a guide to inflation pressure from the labour market. Whether such a loop builds depends on expectations, bargaining structures and the strength of demand.

Compensating differentials

Jobs with unpleasant, risky or unsociable features tend to pay more for the same skill, and jobs with attractive features (security, flexibility, meaning) can pay less. This is why comparing salaries alone can mislead, and why total compensation and hours belong in any comparison.

Common misconceptions

  • “Any pay rise increases living standards.” It does so only when it exceeds the rise in the prices the worker pays.
  • “Higher salary means higher pay per hour.” Hours, leave and contributions can reverse the ranking.

Why people disagree

Economists disagree about minimum wages. One view, based on supply and demand and on studies that find fewer jobs for young and less experienced workers after some increases, stresses that a minimum set above what some jobs produce can reduce hiring of those workers. Another view, based on studies of natural experiments, finds small or no job losses from moderate minimum wages, especially where employers have power over wages because workers have few alternatives. David Card, a co-recipient of the 2021 Nobel Prize in economics, used natural experiments, including a study of a minimum-wage rise, and found that a higher minimum wage does not necessarily lead to fewer jobs (Nobel Prize). Most of the debate is about how high the minimum can go before effects appear, not about whether it has any effect.

Varies by country

Some countries set a single statutory minimum wage by law, for example Germany and the United Kingdom. Japan sets minimum wages by region. Others, such as Sweden and Denmark, have no statutory minimum and rely on collective agreements between unions and employers. The ILO and Eurostat describe the different systems (ILO, Eurostat). The share of workers covered by collective agreements also ranges from a small minority in some countries to most workers in others.
