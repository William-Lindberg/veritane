Saving for Goals

Turning "someday" into a date and a monthly number.

Everyday·5 min (Simple) · 8 min (Detailed)·Updated 6 November 2026

Read first: What a Budget Actually Is

Key points

  • A goal becomes concrete with three things: an amount, a date and a place where the money is kept.
  • Money needed soon is usually kept in cash savings; money for goals many years away is sometimes held in assets that can grow but also fall (this is education, not advice).
  • A transfer scheduled for payday moves money before it is counted as available to spend, which removes a monthly decision.
  • Several small, named goals are often easier to follow than one large, vague one.

Simple: plain words. Detailed: exact figures, codes and methods.

In short: a savings goal turns “someday” into a target amount, a date and a monthly number. Dividing the amount by the months left gives a first answer; interest and inflation adjust it.

Simple version

Three parts of a goal

“I want to save more” is a wish. A goal has three parts:

  1. An amount: how much, in ¤.
  2. A date: when the money is needed.
  3. A place: where the money is kept until then.

With an amount and a date, the monthly number follows:

monthly saving = amount ÷ months until the date

Three example goals

Example

Three goals, all with made-up numbers and no interest:

Goal Amount Time Monthly saving
A trip ¤1,200 12 months ¤1,200 ÷ 12 = ¤100
A new fridge ¤600 6 months ¤600 ÷ 6 = ¤100
A deposit on a home ¤20,000 5 years (60 months) ¤20,000 ÷ 60 ≈ ¤333

Together they need about ¤533 a month. If the budget has ¤300 free, something has to change: a later date, a smaller amount, or one goal at a time.

Seeing the monthly number early makes these choices visible before the date arrives.

Interest helps a little, or a lot

Money saved in an account that pays interest grows on its own. For short goals, the effect is small. For long goals, it adds up.

For the ¤20,000 deposit over five years, an account paying 3% a year (example, interest added monthly) brings the monthly amount down from about ¤333 to about ¤309. Interest makes up the rest: about ¤1,438 over the five years.

Prices rise too

If a goal is several years away, its price may rise. With prices rising 2% a year (example), something that costs ¤20,000 today would cost about ¤22,082 in five years. Some people raise a long-term goal each year to keep up with prices.

Naming the money

Some banks let you open several savings pots or accounts and give each a name: “Trip”, “Fridge”, “Deposit”. A named pot is easier to leave alone, and you can see progress on each goal separately.

Taking the decision out of each month

Saving whatever is left at the end of the month often means saving very little, because spending tends to fill the space. A transfer scheduled for payday moves the money before it is counted as available to spend; many people find this removes the monthly decision. The amount can still be changed when circumstances change.

Short goals and long goals

How long you have changes where people tend to keep the money:

  • Under a few years: usually cash savings, because the money is needed at a fixed date and a fall in value just before it would be a problem.
  • Many years away: some people use investments that can grow faster than savings over long periods but can also fall in value along the way. Module 6 explains how this works. This lesson does not recommend any choice.

Detailed version

The savings-goal formula

For a target future value FV, monthly rate i (nominal yearly rate ÷ 12) and n monthly deposits at the end of each month:

PMT = FV × i ÷ ((1 + i)^n − 1)
if i = 0: PMT = FV ÷ n

Example: FV = ¤20,000, yearly rate 3% (i = 0.0025), n = 60 gives PMT = 20,000 × 0.0025 ÷ (1.0025^60 − 1) = ¤309.37. Total deposits are 60 × 309.37 = ¤18,562.43, so interest contributes ¤1,437.57. With deposits at the start of each month, divide PMT by (1 + i). Any amount already saved, PV, reduces the target by PV × (1 + i)^n.

Inflation-adjusting a goal

A goal priced in today’s money grows with expected inflation π:

FV_nominal = FV_today × (1 + π)^years

At π = 2% for 5 years, ¤20,000 becomes 20,000 × 1.02^5 = ¤22,081.62, raising the monthly deposit at 3% to ¤341.57. Alternatively, the calculation can be done entirely in real terms, using a real rate of about 1% (see lesson 1.4). Central banks explain that inflation reduces what a given amount of money buys over time (ECB).

Goal-based planning and time horizons

Goal-based planning assigns each goal its own amount, date and holding. A common way to map horizon to the kind of asset:

Horizon Main concern Assets often discussed
Under about 3 years Value at a fixed date Instant-access savings, term deposits
About 3–10 years Balance of growth and stability Mixes of deposits, bonds and shares
Over about 10 years Growth after inflation Larger shares of growth assets

The boundaries are conventions, not rules. What matters is sequence: an asset that falls in price shortly before the goal date leaves less time to recover. This table is educational, not a recommendation.

Mental accounting: feature and bug

Mental accounting is the tendency to treat money as belonging to separate, labelled accounts rather than as one interchangeable pool. As a feature, labelled pots make goals concrete and make it harder to spend the trip money on something else. As a bug, the same habit can lead a household to keep savings earning 2% while carrying card debt at 20%, paying the difference in interest. Module 14 explains the behavioural research.

Common misconceptions

  • “Interest will do most of the work.” For short horizons and low rates, deposits do almost all of it.
  • “A goal is fixed once set.” Dates and amounts can move as circumstances change.

Varies by country

Many countries offer savings or investment accounts with tax advantages, each with its own rules and limits. Examples include Individual Savings Accounts (ISAs) in the United Kingdom, Tax-Free Savings Accounts (TFSAs) in Canada and the NISA scheme in Japan. Others have none, or link tax advantages to specific goals such as a first home, education or retirement. Module 9 explains the general pattern.
