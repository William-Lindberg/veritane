Herd Behaviour, FOMO and Bubbles

Why people buy because others are buying, and how that can build and then end a bubble.

Everyday·5 min (Simple) · 9 min (Detailed)·Updated 6 November 2026

Read first: Why Money Decisions Are Hard for Everyone

Key points

  • Following others (social proof) is a reasonable shortcut that stops working when everyone uses it at once.
  • Fear of missing out (FOMO) tends to bring people in late, at high prices.
  • Bubbles show recurring stages, and the end is clear only afterwards.
  • News coverage and social platforms amplify both the rise and the fall.
  • Recognising the pattern does not tell anyone when it ends; plans and limits set in advance do more.

Simple: plain words. Detailed: exact figures, codes and methods.

In short: copying other people is often sensible, but when a whole market copies at once, prices can move far from what the underlying asset earns, and then move back.

Simple version

Following the crowd is often sensible

In an unfamiliar town, a restaurant full of people is a fair sign the food is good. Copying others saves time and uses their knowledge. Psychologists call this social proofSocial proofTaking other people's behaviour as evidence of what is right, which marketers and scammers exploit.Read more about Social proof.

The shortcut stops working when everyone uses it at the same time. If each new diner chooses the busy restaurant only because it is busy, the queue tells you nothing about the food. Prices in a market can behave the same way: people buy because the price is rising, which makes it rise further. This is herd behaviourHerd behaviourCopying what others do, which can push prices far from value.Read more about Herd behaviour.

Fear of missing out

FOMO, the fear of missing out, is the feeling that others are getting rich and you are being left behind. It is strongest after prices have already risen a lot, because that is when stories of gains are everywhere. So it tends to bring people in late, at high prices.

Example

A token rises from ¤20 to ¤100 over a year (example). A friend who bought at ¤20 talks about it often. You buy at ¤100. The price then falls back to ¤40. Your loss is 60%. To get back to ¤100, the price now has to rise 150%.

The stages of a bubble

A bubbleBubbleA period when prices rise far above any reasonable estimate of value, sustained by expectations of further rises.Read more about Bubble is a period when prices rise far above what the asset’s income or use would justify, and then fall. Descriptions of bubbles often show similar stages:

  1. A real change: a new technology, cheap credit or a new market.
  2. Rising prices attract attention and early gains.
  3. Wider buying: people buying this kind of asset for the first time join in, often with borrowed money.
  4. Doubt: prices stop rising; some sell.
  5. The fall: selling feeds on itself, especially when borrowers have to sell.

The hard part is that the stages are clear only afterwards. During a rise, it is genuinely uncertain whether the price reflects a lasting change or a bubble.

Episodes from history, in two sentences each

  • Tulips, 1630s (Netherlands). Prices of rare tulip bulbs rose to very high levels and then fell sharply in early 1637. Some economists argue the episode is often exaggerated in retellings (Garber, 1989).
  • US shares, 1920s. Share prices rose through the late 1920s, with much buying on borrowed money, and fell steeply from October 1929 (Federal Reserve History).
  • US housing, 2000s. House prices and mortgage lending to riskier borrowers rose through the mid-2000s, then prices fell and many loans defaulted from 2007 (Federal Reserve History).

Media and social platforms

News and social media spread stories of gains quickly, and a rising price is itself a story. This can speed up both the rise and the fall.

A FOMO checklist

Questions people use when a “can’t miss” opportunity appears (education, not instructions):

  • Would I buy this if nobody I know had mentioned it?
  • What does it earn or produce, apart from someone else buying it later?
  • How much could I lose, in ¤, and what would that mean for me?
  • Is someone urging me to decide quickly, or promising returns?

Pressure and promised returns are also classic signals of fraud.

Detailed version

Information cascades

Bikhchandani, Hirshleifer and Welch show that when people act in sequence and observe earlier actions but not earlier private information, it can become rational to ignore one’s own information and follow the crowd. Once a cascade starts, later actions add no new information, so cascades can end abruptly: a small public signal can reverse them (Bikhchandani, Hirshleifer and Welch, 1992). Herding can therefore arise without any irrationality.

Extrapolative expectations

Greenwood and Shleifer compare six surveys of investor expectations and find that expected returns are positively correlated with past returns and with the level of the market, and negatively correlated with model-based measures of expected returns (Greenwood and Shleifer, 2014). Investors who extrapolate recent gains tend to be most optimistic when valuations are highest. Extrapolation is a building block of many behavioural models of bubbles.

Bubble literature summarised

Why people disagree

Shiller argues that asset prices can deviate substantially from fundamentals for long periods, sustained by narratives and feedback loops, and that valuation ratios have some predictive power over long horizons (Shiller, 2014). Other economists argue that “bubble” is often applied only after a fall, that large price rises and falls can reflect rational changes in expected growth or risk, and that bubbles cannot be reliably identified in real time. Garber’s work on tulips is an example of the second view applied to history (Garber, 1989). Both views accept that leverage makes the fall more damaging.

Social media and retail flows

Barber, Huang, Odean and Schwarz study users of a commission-free trading app and find that attention-driven buying concentrated in a small number of shares (“herding events”), and that these shares were followed on average by negative returns over the following weeks (Barber et al., 2022). The authors link the effect to app features that direct attention, such as lists of most-traded shares. Evidence elsewhere on the effect of social media on prices is still developing and differs by market and period.

What structure does

Because the end of a bubble cannot be timed in advance, the practical tools discussed in the literature are structural: a written plan for how much of a portfolio goes into any one asset, limits on borrowing to invest, and periodic rebalancing back to target weights. These do not predict anything; they cap exposure whatever happens.
