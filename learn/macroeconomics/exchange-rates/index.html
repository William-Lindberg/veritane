Exchange Rates

What moves a currency, and what a change in its value means for prices, travel, savings and businesses.

Intermediate·6 min (Simple) · 10 min (Detailed)·Updated 6 November 2026

Read first: Interest Rates and Central Banks

Key points

  • An exchange rate is the price of one currency in terms of another.
  • Exchange rates respond to interest-rate differences, inflation, trade flows, investors' attitude to risk and, sometimes, central bank intervention.
  • Some currencies float freely, some are managed, and some are pegged to another currency.
  • When a currency falls in value, imports become dearer and exports cheaper for foreign buyers; a “strong” currency is not simply good, nor a “weak” one simply bad.
  • Retail currency exchange includes a spread and fees; “0% commission” does not mean the exchange is free.

Simple: plain words. Detailed: exact figures, codes and methods.

In short: an exchange rate is a price, set mostly in markets, for swapping one currency for another. When it changes, some people gain and others lose, depending on whether they buy from abroad or sell to it.

Simple version

Reading a quote

An exchange rateExchange rateThe price of one currency in terms of another.Read more about Exchange rate tells you how much of one currency you get for one unit of another. A quote of “EUR/USD 1.10” means 1 euro buys 1.10 US dollars (example).

The first currency is the one being priced. If the quote goes from 1.10 to 1.20, the euro has risen in value against the dollar: each euro buys more dollars.

Why your holiday money changed

Suppose you plan a trip and change ¤500 into a foreign currency. If the rate moves by 5% before you travel, you get 5% more or less foreign money for the same ¤500. Exchange rates change every second on weekdays, because currencies are traded constantly around the world.

Things that move a currency include:

  • Interest rates: higher rates in one country can attract savers and investors, raising demand for its currency.
  • Inflation: if prices rise faster in one country, its currency tends to lose value over time.
  • Trade: a country selling a lot abroad receives foreign money that its exporters swap back into the home currency.
  • Risk: in uncertain times, investors move money into currencies they see as safer.
  • Central banks: some buy or sell their own currency to influence its value.

A rise is not a win, a fall is not a loss

News reports often call a currency “strong” or “weak”. These words do not mean good or bad.

Example

A shop imports a product priced at 100 in a foreign currency (example).

  • At a rate of 1.10 foreign units per ¤, it costs the shop 100 ÷ 1.10 = about ¤90.91.
  • If the ¤ falls to 1.00, it costs ¤100: about 10% more.

The same fall helps a local business that sells abroad: its products become cheaper for foreign buyers.

A currency that rises in value is good for importers and travellers going abroad, and harder for exporters and tourism at home. A currency that falls in value does the opposite.

The hidden cost: the spread

When you change money, the rate you get is not the rate in the news. The provider buys at one price and sells at another. The difference is the spreadBid-ask spreadThe gap between the price buyers will pay and the price sellers will accept, which is a cost to whoever trades.Read more about Bid-ask spread.

Example

The market (middle) rate is 1.10 (example). A currency booth advertises “0% commission” but sells at 1.03.

Changing ¤500 at 1.10 would give 550 foreign units. At 1.03 you get 515. The difference, 35 units, is about 6.4% of what you would get at the market rate. That is the cost, even with no commission.

Detailed version

Anchors: parity conditions

Two textbook relationships describe where exchange rates tend to go over long horizons:

  • Purchasing power parity (PPP): in the long run, exchange rates tend towards levels at which a common basket costs the same in each country. Relative PPP says the currency of a country with higher inflation depreciates by roughly the inflation difference (Eurostat). Deviations can persist for years.
  • Interest rate parity: covered interest parity links the forward exchange rate to the interest-rate difference: F ÷ S ≈ (1 + i_domestic) ÷ (1 + i_foreign). Uncovered interest parity predicts that a high-interest currency will depreciate by the interest difference; the data often show the opposite in the short run, which is the basis of the “carry trade”.

Effective exchange rates

A nominal effective exchange rate (NEER) is a trade-weighted average of a currency against many partners. A real effective exchange rate (REER) adjusts the NEER for relative prices or costs, and is used as a measure of price competitiveness (Eurostat). The BIS publishes effective exchange rates for many economies (BIS).

REER = NEER × (domestic prices ÷ trade-weighted foreign prices)

Exchange-rate regimes

  • Free float: the rate is set by the market; the central bank intervenes rarely.
  • Managed float: the market sets the rate, with intervention to smooth movements.
  • Peg or fixed rate: the central bank commits to a rate against another currency or basket, and holds reserves to defend it.
  • Currency board, dollarisation or currency union: the home currency is fully backed by, replaced by, or shared as a single currency.

The “impossible trinity” says a country cannot at the same time have a fixed exchange rate, free movement of capital, and an independent monetary policy; it can choose at most two. Pegs that become inconsistent with domestic policy or reserves can come under pressure and be abandoned, sometimes abruptly.

Pass-through

Exchange-rate pass-through is the share of a currency change that shows up in import prices and then consumer prices. It is generally incomplete and varies with the currency in which trade is invoiced, competition and the persistence of the change. Central banks include the exchange-rate channel in their analysis of how policy reaches inflation (ECB).

Market structure

The foreign exchange market is decentralised and trades mostly between dealers and institutions. The BIS Triennial Survey, conducted every three years, is the main source on its size and composition (BIS). Reference rates, such as those the ECB publishes each working day, are for information and are not the rates retail customers receive (ECB).

Common misconceptions

  • “A falling currency means the economy is failing.” Currencies move for many reasons, including interest-rate changes and shifts in global risk appetite.
  • “0% commission means no charge.” The cost sits in the rate.

Why people disagree

Economists disagree about the choice of exchange-rate regime. One view holds that a floating rate lets a country absorb external changes and run its own monetary policy. Another view holds that a fixed rate or currency union gives price stability and lowers trading costs, especially for small, open economies or for countries with a history of high inflation. Views also differ on when intervention to smooth exchange-rate movements is useful. The right choice is generally seen as depending on each country’s circumstances (BIS).

Varies by country

Regimes differ widely. The euro floats freely against other currencies. Denmark keeps its krone in a narrow band against the euro. Hong Kong links its dollar to the US dollar through a currency board system. Some countries use another country’s currency as their own. The ECB reference rates show how the euro is quoted against many currencies (ECB).
