GDP: What It Measures and What It Misses

Gross domestic product counts the value of what an economy produces; it is a useful measure and an incomplete one.

Intermediate·6 min (Simple) · 11 min (Detailed)·Updated 6 November 2026

Read first: Percentages, Averages and Charts, Step by Step

Key points

  • GDP is the total value added by everyone producing goods and services in a country over a period; it can be measured from production, income or spending, and the three should match.
  • Real GDP removes the effect of price changes, so it shows changes in the amount produced.
  • GDP per person and the growth rate are the usual ways to compare countries and years.
  • GDP leaves out unpaid work, how income is shared, damage to the environment and wellbeing.
  • GDP figures are revised as more data arrive; revisions are a normal part of the process.

Simple: plain words. Detailed: exact figures, codes and methods.

In short: GDP adds up the value of everything produced in a country in a period. It is the most used measure of the size of an economy, and it was not designed to measure wellbeing.

Simple version

What GDP counts

Gross domestic productGDP (gross domestic product)The total value of everything produced in a country in a period.Read more about GDP (gross domestic product) (GDP) is the value of all the goods and services produced in a country over a period, usually a quarter or a year.

It counts only final goods and services, or, which gives the same answer, the value added at each step. This avoids counting the same thing twice.

Example

A farmer grows wheat and sells it to a miller for ¤100 (example). The miller makes flour and sells it to a baker for ¤150. The baker makes bread and sells it to customers for ¤250.

Value added: farmer ¤100, miller ¤50 (¤150 − ¤100), baker ¤100 (¤250 − ¤150). Total: ¤250, which is the price of the final product, the bread. Adding up all the sales (¤500) would count the wheat three times.

Three ways to see the same total

GDP can be measured in three ways, and they should give the same number:

View Adds up Example from the bakery
Production Value added by every producer ¤100 + ¤50 + ¤100
Income Wages, profits and other income earned in production What the farmer, miller and baker earn
Spending Final spending by households, firms, government and foreign buyers, minus imports What customers paid for bread

Every ¤ spent on a final product is someone’s income, and it pays for value added somewhere along the chain.

Nominal and real

Nominal GDP uses the prices of the time. If prices rise, nominal GDP rises even if the same amount is produced. Real GDP removes the effect of price changes.

Example

Nominal GDP rises from ¤1,000 billion to ¤1,100 billion in a year (example): a 10% rise. Prices rose 6% over the same year. Real growth is 1.10 ÷ 1.06 − 1 = about 3.8%.

When the news says “the economy grew 2%”, it usually means real GDP.

Per person

A large country has a large GDP because it has many people. To compare living standards, economists divide by the population. A country with GDP of ¤500 billion and 10 million people has GDP per personGDP per capitaGDP divided by population, used to compare living standards.Read more about GDP per capita of ¤50,000 (example).

What GDP leaves out

  • Unpaid work: cooking, cleaning and caring for family members at home add a lot of value but are not sold, so they are not counted.
  • How income is shared: GDP per person can rise while the incomes of many households stay the same.
  • The environment: using up resources or polluting can raise GDP while reducing what is left for the future.
  • Wellbeing: health, free time and safety are not part of GDP.

Revisions

The first GDP estimate comes out quickly, based on incomplete data. As fuller data arrive, the figure is revised, sometimes by several tenths of a percentage point. A revision is the normal process, not an error.

Detailed version

The national accounts framework

GDP is defined in the System of National Accounts (SNA), the international standard maintained by the UN, IMF, World Bank, OECD and Eurostat (UNSD). The three approaches are:

Production:  GDP = Σ gross value added + taxes on products − subsidies on products
Expenditure: GDP = C + I + G + (X − M)
Income:      GDP = compensation of employees + gross operating surplus + gross mixed income
                   + taxes on production and imports − subsidies

C is household consumption, I gross capital formation (investment and changes in inventories), G government consumption, X exports and M imports (Eurostat). In practice the three measures differ because of different data sources; the published figure is a balanced estimate, and some countries publish the statistical discrepancy.

Related aggregates: GNI (gross national income) adds income earned abroad by residents and subtracts income paid to non-residents, which matters for countries with large foreign investment flows.

Real GDP and chain-linking

Real GDP is measured as a volume. Older methods valued output at the prices of a fixed base year, which overstates growth in goods whose prices fall over time. Most statistics offices now use chain-linking: growth from one year to the next is measured at the previous year’s prices, and the yearly links are multiplied together. A consequence is that chain-linked components do not add up exactly to the chain-linked total, except in the reference year (BEA).

The implicit price deflator is nominal GDP divided by real GDP, times 100.

Comparing across countries

Converting GDP at market exchange rates reflects international buying power but understates living standards where prices of non-traded services are low. Purchasing power parities (PPPs) convert at rates that equalise the price of a common basket (Eurostat). The World Bank’s International Comparison Program produces global PPPs (World Bank). Rankings by GDP per person can change substantially between the two methods.

The informal economy

Activity that is legal but unregistered, such as cash work and small trading, is part of GDP in principle. National accounts include estimates based on labour surveys and other sources. The informal share is large in many lower-income economies, so measurement error there is larger.

Alternative and complementary measures

Several measures add dimensions GDP omits. The UNDP Human Development Index combines income, education and life expectancy (UNDP). The OECD’s well-being framework reports indicators such as housing, health and environmental quality. Other approaches include satellite accounts for household production and for the environment, and distributional national accounts. These complement GDP rather than replace it in official statistics.

How professionals read a release

The first estimate (often called “flash” or “advance”) is followed by revisions. Analysts check: real or nominal; quarter-on-quarter or year-on-year; whether the quarterly rate is annualised (common in the United States, not in most of Europe); seasonal adjustment; and the size of past revisions (Bank of England).

annualised rate = (1 + quarterly rate)^4 − 1
0.5% q/q → 1.005^4 − 1 = 2.02% annualised

Why people disagree

Economists differ on how much weight GDP deserves. One view holds that GDP growth is closely linked to income, jobs and public revenue, and remains the most reliable single measure of economic activity. Another view holds that the focus on GDP neglects distribution, sustainability and wellbeing, and argues for giving equal official weight to a wider “dashboard” of indicators. A third view accepts GDP for what it measures and treats other measures as separate tools for separate questions (UNDP).

Varies by country

Publication practice differs. The United States reports quarterly growth as an annualised rate (BEA). The United Kingdom and euro-area countries report the change on the previous quarter without annualising. Some statistics offices publish monthly GDP estimates, for example the UK, while most publish quarterly. Countries also differ in how quickly they publish and how large their typical revisions are.
