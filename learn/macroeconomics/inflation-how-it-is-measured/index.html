Inflation: Causes and Measurement

What drives inflation across an economy, and how statistics offices measure it.

Intermediate·6 min (Simple) · 11 min (Detailed)·Updated 6 November 2026

Read first: Prices and Inflation at Home

Key points

  • Three broad drivers: demand rising faster than supply (demand-pull), rising costs (cost-push), and expectations of future inflation.
  • A consumer price index measures the change in the price of a basket; core measures leave out volatile items such as food and energy.
  • Headline and core, month-on-month and year-on-year are different numbers that answer different questions.
  • Deflation means prices fall; disinflation means prices still rise, but more slowly.
  • Many central banks aim for inflation of around 2% a year; some use ranges, other numbers or other measures.

Simple: plain words. Detailed: exact figures, codes and methods.

In short: inflation happens when spending grows faster than what the economy can supply, when costs rise, or when people expect prices to rise and act on it. Statistics offices measure it by pricing the same basket every month.

Simple version

Three causes in plain words

  1. Too much spending for what is available (demand-pull). If people and businesses want to buy more than the economy can produce, sellers can raise prices.
  2. Rising costs (cost-push). If energy, materials or wages cost more, businesses pass some of that on in their prices. A war, a drought or a broken supply chain can do this.
  3. Expectations. If workers expect prices to rise 5% next year, they ask for higher pay; if businesses expect their costs to rise, they raise prices sooner. Expectations can keep inflation going.

In real life the three often work together.

The basket

To measure inflationInflationA general rise in prices over time, which reduces what money buys.Read more about Inflation, a statistics office prices a basket of hundreds of goods and services every month, in many shops and regions. The result is a consumer price indexConsumer price index (CPI)A measure of the prices of a basket of goods and services that households buy, used to track inflation.Read more about Consumer price index (CPI) (CPI). The yearly inflation rate is the change in the index compared with the same month a year earlier.

Headline and core

Headline inflation is the change in the whole basket. Core inflation leaves out items whose prices jump around a lot, usually food and energy. Core shows the slower, underlying trend. Headline shows what households actually pay. Both matter.

Month and year

  • Year-on-year: this month compared with the same month last year. This is the number in most news reports.
  • Month-on-month: this month compared with last month. It reacts faster, but it is noisier and affected by seasons.

Example

Prices rise 0.3% in one month (example). If that continued for a whole year, the yearly rate would be 1.003¹² − 1 = about 3.7%. One month is not enough to say that it will continue.

Falling, or rising more slowly?

  • DeflationDeflationA general fall in prices over time.Read more about Deflation: the price level falls. The inflation rate is below zero.
  • DisinflationDisinflationInflation slowing down while prices are still rising.Read more about Disinflation: prices still rise, but more slowly, for example inflation going from 6% to 3%.

When inflation falls from 6% to 3%, prices are still rising. They do not return to where they were.

Why 2%?

Many central banks aim for inflation of around 2% a year, for example the European Central Bank and the US Federal Reserve (Federal Reserve). The reasons usually given are that a small positive rate leaves room to cut interest rates in a downturn, keeps a margin away from deflation, and allows for measurement bias in price indices. Not every central bank uses 2%, and economists debate whether it is the right number.

Detailed version

Index formulas

A CPI is a Laspeyres-type index of a fixed basket valued at current and base prices:

I_t = Σ p_t,i × q_0,i ÷ Σ p_0,i × q_0,i × 100

Example: a basket of 10 loaves at ¤2.00 and 5 bus trips at ¤3.00 costs ¤35. If bread rises to ¤2.20 and bus fares stay the same, the basket costs ¤37: the index is 105.7, a 5.7% rise. In practice, elementary aggregates (single products in single regions) are computed with unweighted formulas such as the geometric mean (Jevons), and aggregated with expenditure weights. Most offices update weights each year and chain-link the annual indices, which limits substitution bias. The EU’s Harmonised Index of Consumer Prices (HICP) follows common rules across member states so that rates can be compared (Eurostat).

Owner-occupied housing

The treatment of housing costs for people who own their home differs most between countries:

  • Excluded: the HICP has so far excluded owner-occupied housing; the ECB’s 2021 strategy review recommended including it, and work towards that is under way (ECB).
  • Rental equivalence: the cost of housing services is estimated from the rent of comparable homes (used, for example, in the US CPI and the UK CPIH).
  • Net acquisitions or user cost: prices of newly bought homes, or a cost based on interest and depreciation, are used in some countries.

Because housing is a large share of spending, the choice can change the measured rate by a meaningful amount.

Core measures

Core measures exclude volatile items (ex food and energy) or use statistical trimming. A trimmed mean removes the items with the largest and smallest price changes each month; a weighted median takes the middle price change. Central banks track several measures, since each one excludes different information.

Base effects

The year-on-year rate depends on the change twelve months ago as well as this month. If energy prices rose sharply a year ago, the year-on-year rate falls this month when that rise drops out of the comparison, even if this month’s prices are unchanged. Analysts decompose the change in the annual rate into the current monthly change and the base effect.

Inflation expectations

Expectations are measured with household surveys (ECB Consumer Expectations Survey, New York Fed Survey of Consumer Expectations), surveys of firms and professional forecasters, and market measures. The market measure most often cited is breakeven inflation: the yield on a conventional government bond minus the yield on an inflation-linked bond of the same maturity. It includes risk and liquidity premiums, so it is not a pure expectation.

Inflation targets compared

  • European Central Bank: 2% over the medium term, symmetric (deviations above and below are equally undesirable) (ECB).
  • US Federal Reserve: 2% over the longer run, measured by the price index for personal consumption expenditures (Federal Reserve).
  • Reserve Bank of India: a target set by the central government in terms of CPI inflation, with a tolerance band, under a flexible inflation-targeting framework written into law (RBI).
  • Sveriges Riksbank: 2% for CPIF, a consumer price index with a fixed interest rate (Riksbank).

Why people disagree

Economists disagree about the right target. Some argue for a higher target, such as 3% or 4%, because it would leave more room to cut interest rates before they reach zero. Others argue that a higher target would erode the credibility built around 2% and impose larger costs of inflation on households. Some propose targeting the price level or nominal GDP instead of the inflation rate, so that past misses are made up later (Federal Reserve). Economists also disagree about how much of a given inflation episode came from demand, from supply costs or from expectations, which shapes views on the right policy response.

Varies by country

Targets and measures differ. The euro area and the United States target 2%, measured with different indices. India’s target is set by the government in terms of CPI with a tolerance band (RBI). Sweden targets 2% for a CPI measure that excludes the direct effect of interest-rate changes on mortgage costs (Riksbank). The treatment of owner-occupied housing in the main index also differs between countries.
