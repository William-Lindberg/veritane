The Economics of Climate and Carbon Pricing

Why economists treat greenhouse-gas emissions as an externality, and what the main policy tools do, described without taking a side.

Intermediate·6 min (Simple) · 12 min (Detailed)·Updated 6 November 2026

Read first: Fiscal Policy: Budgets, Deficits and Public Debt

Key points

  • Emissions impose costs on people who are not part of the purchase that caused them; economists call this an externality.
  • A carbon tax sets the price of emissions and lets the quantity adjust; cap-and-trade sets the quantity and lets the price adjust. Both aim to make the external cost visible in prices.
  • What happens to the revenue (rebates, cuts in other taxes, public investment) largely decides who pays on balance.
  • Measuring emissions and “leakage” (activity moving to places without a carbon price) are the main design problems.
  • Economists disagree on the right price, the discount rate and the pace; this lesson sets out the positions without a verdict.

Simple: plain words. Detailed: exact figures, codes and methods.

In short: when a product’s price leaves out the damage its emissions cause to others, people buy more of it than they would if they paid the full cost. Carbon pricing tries to put that cost into the price; how high, how fast and who pays are open questions.

Simple version

The factory and its neighbours

A factory makes a product and sells it. Its chimney releases smoke that harms people nearby, who are not buying or selling anything. The factory does not pay for that harm, so its price is lower than the full cost to society.

When a cost falls on people outside a deal, economists call it an externalityExternalityA cost or benefit from an activity that falls on people who are not part of the transaction, such as pollution or vaccination.Read more about Externality. Greenhouse-gas emissions are an externality on a global scale: they come from many sources and affect people everywhere, including people not yet born (Eurostat).

Example

A factory makes 1,000 units a year (example). Each unit releases 0.5 tonnes of carbon dioxide (CO₂): 500 tonnes in total. Suppose each tonne causes ¤50 of damage to others.

The external cost is 500 × ¤50 = ¤25,000 a year, or ¤25 per unit. If the product sells for ¤100, the full cost to society is about ¤125.

In cap-and-tradeCap-and-tradeA scheme that sets a limit on total emissions and lets firms buy and sell permits within it, so the price of a permit adjusts.Read more about Cap-and-trade, the government issues a limited number of permits. Firms that can cut emissions cheaply sell spare permits to firms for which cutting is costly. Either way, emitting costs money, so the cheapest cuts are made first.

Who ends up paying?

A carbon priceCarbon priceA charge per tonne of emissions, set directly as a tax or indirectly through tradeable permits, so that the cost of emissions to others shows up in prices.Read more about Carbon price raises the price of things that cause emissions, such as fuel and electricity. Part of the cost reaches households. But the government also collects money, and what it does with it changes who pays on balance.

Example

A carbon tax of ¤50 per tonne (example). Household A’s spending causes 10 tonnes of emissions a year and pays ¤500 more. Household B’s causes 4 tonnes and pays ¤200 more.

If the government returns the money as an equal rebate of ¤350 to each household, A pays ¤150 more on balance and B receives ¤150 more. If instead the money funds other spending, both pay more.

Three open questions

  1. How high should the price be? It depends on estimates of future damage, which are uncertain.
  2. How much weight does the future get? Much of the damage happens decades from now. How to compare it with costs today is partly a question of values.
  3. What about other countries? If one country prices emissions and another does not, production may move rather than shrink.

Detailed version

The social cost of carbon and the discount rate

The social cost of carbon (SCC) is the present value of the damage caused by emitting one additional tonne of CO₂. It is estimated with integrated assessment models that link emissions, climate and the economy. William Nordhaus received the 2018 Nobel Prize for integrating climate change into long-run macroeconomic analysis (Nobel Prize).

The discount rate has a large effect. The present value of ¤1,000 of damage in 100 years:

PV = 1,000 ÷ (1 + r)^100
r = 1.4%  →  about ¤249
r = 3.0%  →  about ¤52
r = 4.5%  →  about ¤12

A low discount rate gives future damage far more weight today. The rate combines a judgement about how much less future wellbeing should count (pure time preference) with expectations about growth (richer future generations value an extra ¤ less). The first is a question of ethics as much as economics, which is why estimates differ widely.

Governments use different approaches. Some use SCC estimates; others use a target-consistent value, the cost of meeting a legislated emissions target. The UK publishes carbon values for policy appraisal on a target-consistent basis (UK Government). Official values in several countries have been revised over time as methods and policy changed.

Price versus quantity under uncertainty

When the damage from each extra tonne changes little with the total (a flat marginal-damage curve) and abatement costs are uncertain, a tax limits the risk of very high costs. When damage rises steeply near a threshold, a quantity limit limits the risk of exceeding it. Hybrid schemes add price floors, ceilings or reserves to cap-and-trade. Existing schemes

The World Bank tracks carbon taxes and emissions trading systems worldwide in its annual State and Trends of Carbon Pricing report, including prices, coverage and revenue (World Bank). The EU Emissions Trading System, launched in 2005, is a cap-and-trade system covering power, industry and other sectors, with a cap that falls over time (European Commission). Sweden has levied a carbon tax since 1991. China launched a national emissions trading system in 2021, starting with the power sector.

Leakage and border adjustment

Carbon leakage occurs when production moves to jurisdictions with lower carbon prices, leaving global emissions less changed. Responses include free allocation of permits to exposed industries and border carbon adjustments, which charge importers for the carbon embedded in goods. The EU’s Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism applies to imports of goods such as cement, iron and steel, aluminium, fertilisers, electricity and hydrogen (European Commission). Trading partners have raised questions about its compatibility with trade rules and its effects on exporters.

Distribution and data

Carbon pricing is often regressive before revenue is used: lower-income households spend a larger share of income on energy. Revenue recycling through lump-sum rebates can make the net effect progressive when income groups are compared as a whole, while some households within each group still pay more. Cross-country comparisons of emissions per person use series such as the World Bank indicator EN.GHG.CO2.PC.CE.AR5 (World Bank); production-based and consumption-based measures give different rankings, because traded goods carry embedded emissions. Why people disagree

Economists disagree on several points. On the price level, estimates of the social cost of carbon range widely, depending on the climate model, the damage function and the treatment of uncertainty and extreme outcomes. On the discount rate, one view uses market interest rates to reflect how societies trade off present and future, giving lower weight to distant damage; another uses a near-zero rate of pure time preference on ethical grounds, giving distant damage much more weight (Nobel Prize). On the pace, some favour gradual price increases to limit adjustment costs, others faster action to reduce the chance of severe outcomes. On instruments, some favour carbon pricing as the lowest-cost tool, while others argue that regulation, standards and public investment are needed alongside or instead of prices, for reasons of politics, fairness or market failures that prices do not address. This lesson does not take a position.

Varies by country

Approaches differ. The European Union uses cap-and-trade for power and industry and adds a border mechanism for some imports (European Commission). Sweden uses a carbon tax on fuels. China operates a national emissions trading system that began with its power sector. Many other countries have no explicit carbon price and rely on regulation, standards or subsidies (World Bank). How revenue is used also differs from scheme to scheme.
