Business Cycles and Recessions

Why economies expand and contract, how recessions are dated, and what tends to happen to jobs, prices and interest rates.

Intermediate·6 min (Simple) · 11 min (Detailed)·Updated 6 November 2026

Read first: GDP: What It Measures and What It Misses, Unemployment and the Labour Market

Key points

  • Economies grow in waves around a long-run trend: periods of expansion are followed by contractions.
  • A recession is a broad fall in economic activity; “two quarters of falling GDP” is a shorthand, and official dating methods vary by country.
  • Leading indicators tend to move before the economy, coincident indicators with it, and lagging indicators after it.
  • Governments and central banks respond with budgets and interest rates, and their effects arrive with delays.
  • Recessions end, but nobody can predict their timing reliably.

Simple: plain words. Detailed: exact figures, codes and methods.

In short: economic activity rises and falls in waves around a long-run upward path. A recession is a broad and lasting fall in activity; how it is identified, and when it ends, is decided after the fact.

Simple version

The wave picture

Over decades, most economies produce more and more. But the path is not smooth. There are periods when output, jobs and incomes grow quickly (expansions), and periods when they fall (contractions). The full wave, from one high point to the next, is called the business cycleBusiness cycleThe repeated but irregular pattern of expansion and contraction in an economy.Read more about Business cycle.

The high point is the peak. The low point is the trough. A recessionRecessionA broad decline in economic activity lasting more than a few months, dated differently by different countries.Read more about Recession is the period from a peak to a trough.

The cycles are not regular. Some expansions last a decade; some recessions last a few months.

Why economies move in waves

There is no single cause. Some common ones:

  • Spending changes. If households and businesses become cautious at the same time, they spend less, so other businesses sell less and cut jobs, which reduces spending again.
  • Credit. When borrowing is easy, spending and asset prices can rise quickly; when lenders pull back, spending falls.
  • Events outside the economy. A pandemic, a war or a sudden rise in energy prices can reduce output.
  • Policy. Higher interest rates aimed at lowering inflation also slow spending.

How do you know it is a recession?

A common shorthand is “two quarters in a row of falling real GDP”.

Example

Real GDP changes over four quarters (example): +0.4%, −0.1%, −0.2%, +0.1%.

The second and third quarters both fell, so the shorthand calls this a recession. Over the whole year, output still grew by about 0.2%.

The shorthand is easy to check, but it can mislead. A small fall in GDP in two quarters might come with rising employment. A sharp fall in one quarter might be a deep recession that the rule misses. This is why some countries use a committee that looks at many indicators.

What a recession means in ordinary life

In a typical recession:

  • some businesses cut hours, stop hiring or lay people off, so unemployment rises,
  • people who keep their jobs see smaller pay rises,
  • businesses invest less, and some close,
  • inflation tends to slow,
  • central banks often cut interest rates,
  • government deficits grow, because tax revenue falls and benefit spending rises.

The effects are uneven. Some sectors, regions and groups are hit much harder than others.

Indicators in plain words

  • Leading indicators tend to change before the economy turns: new orders, building permits, business and consumer surveys, and the gap between long and short interest rates.
  • Coincident indicators move with the economy: output, employment, retail sales.
  • Lagging indicators change after: the unemployment rate often keeps rising for a while after a recession ends.

Detailed version

Dating recessions

In the United States, the National Bureau of Economic Research’s Business Cycle Dating Committee identifies peaks and troughs. It defines a recession as a significant decline in economic activity that is spread across the economy and lasts more than a few months, looking at depth, diffusion and duration across measures such as real personal income less transfers, payroll employment, household employment, real consumer spending and industrial production (NBER). The committee announces dates months after the turning point, because it waits for enough data (NBER FAQ).

In the euro area, a committee convened by the Centre for Economic Policy Research performs a similar role. In many other countries, including the United Kingdom, the two-quarter rule is the common convention in public reporting. Japan’s Cabinet Office sets reference dates using a set of indicators.

The output gap

The output gap compares actual output with potential output, the level consistent with stable inflation:

output gap = (actual GDP − potential GDP) ÷ potential GDP

With actual output of 980 and potential of 1,000: (980 − 1,000) ÷ 1,000 = −2%. Potential output cannot be observed; it is estimated with statistical filters or production-function models, and estimates are revised substantially as new data arrive. A negative gap signals spare capacity; a positive gap, overheating.

Indicator composites

  • Purchasing managers’ indices (PMIs): monthly surveys of firms; a reading above 50 indicates more firms reporting expansion than contraction.
  • Confidence indicators: surveys of households and firms on expectations and plans.
  • Initial claims for unemployment benefits: a timely signal where available weekly.
  • Composite leading indicators: combinations of such series, published by statistical agencies and international organisations.
  • Yield curve slope: the difference between long- and short-term government bond yields; an inverted curve (short above long) has preceded several past US recessions, with variable lead times and false signals.

Eurostat’s Business Cycle Clock shows the position of several indicators relative to their trend (Eurostat).

Stylised facts

Across many cycles: investment and spending on durable goods vary more than consumption of services; employment lags output at turning points; productivity often falls early in a recession, because firms keep workers while output falls; inflation tends to fall with a lag. Recoveries differ: some regain the previous peak quickly, others leave a lasting gap below the earlier trend.

Forecasting limits

Turning points are hard to forecast. Data arrive late and are revised; GDP is published weeks after the quarter ends (Bank of England). Policy acts with lags of many months (ECB). Forecasters therefore express outlooks as ranges and probabilities rather than single numbers.

Why people disagree

Economists disagree about what causes business cycles. One view, associated with Keynesian economics, holds that cycles come mainly from swings in total spending, so that output can stay below potential for long periods and policy can help close the gap. Another view, associated with real business cycle models, holds that much of the variation reflects changes in productivity and supply, to which the economy adjusts efficiently. Other views stress the role of credit and financial conditions in expansions and contractions. Modern models used by central banks combine elements of several of these views (ECB).

Varies by country

Recessions are identified differently. In the United States, a committee of the NBER dates them using many indicators (NBER). In the euro area, a committee of economists convened by the Centre for Economic Policy Research dates them for the whole euro area. In the United Kingdom, public reporting usually uses two consecutive quarters of falling real GDP. Japan’s Cabinet Office sets official reference dates.
