Unemployment and the Labour Market

How unemployment is defined and measured, and what the figures do and do not show.

Intermediate·5 min (Simple) · 10 min (Detailed)·Updated 6 November 2026

Read first: GDP: What It Measures and What It Misses

Key points

  • A person counts as unemployed when they have no work, are available to start, and are actively looking; people who have stopped looking are not counted.
  • The participation rate and the employment rate give a fuller picture than the unemployment rate alone.
  • Frictional, structural and cyclical unemployment have different causes.
  • Underemployment (wanting more hours) and informal work matter a lot in many countries.
  • Survey-based figures and counts of people registered for benefits measure different things.

Simple: plain words. Detailed: exact figures, codes and methods.

In short: the unemployment rate counts people who want work, can start and are looking for it. To see the whole labour market, it helps to look at how many people are in work and how many have stopped looking.

Simple version

Who counts as unemployed?

In the international definition, a person is unemployed if all three are true:

  1. they have no paid work, not even one hour in the reference week,
  2. they are available to start work soon,
  3. they have looked for work recently.

People with jobs are employed. The employed and the unemployed together are the labour forceLabour forceEveryone who is working or actively looking for work.Read more about Labour force. Everyone else of working age is outside the labour force: students, retired people, people caring for family full-time, and people who want a job but have stopped looking. The last group are called discouraged workers.

The unemployment rateUnemployment rateThe share of the labour force without work but available and looking for it.Read more about Unemployment rate is the unemployed as a share of the labour force, not of the whole population.

A worked example

Example

A town has 1,000 people of working age (example): 600 have jobs, 40 have no job and are looking, and 360 are not looking (students, retired, carers and others).

  • Labour force: 600 + 40 = 640.
  • Unemployment rate: 40 ÷ 640 = 6.25%.
  • Participation rate: 640 ÷ 1,000 = 64%.
  • Employment rate: 600 ÷ 1,000 = 60%.

Why the rate can fall without anyone finding a job

Example

In the same town, 10 of the 40 job seekers give up looking. Nobody finds a job.

  • Unemployed: 30. Labour force: 630.
  • Unemployment rate: 30 ÷ 630 = about 4.8%.

The rate fell from 6.25% to 4.8%, yet the number of people in work did not change. The employment rate (still 60%) shows this.

This is why economists look at the participation rateParticipation rateThe share of the working-age population that is working or looking for work.Read more about Participation rate and the employment rate alongside unemployment.

Three kinds of unemployment

  • Frictional: people between jobs, or new to the job market. It takes time to find a good match, even when jobs exist.
  • Structural: the skills or locations of job seekers do not match the jobs available, for example when an industry shrinks in one region.
  • Cyclical: fewer jobs because the whole economy is slowing, as in a recession.

Some frictional and structural unemployment exists at all times. Cyclical unemployment rises and falls with the economy.

People who want more work

Some people have a job but want more hours: for example a part-time worker who wants full-time work. This is underemploymentUnderemploymentWorking fewer hours or at a lower level than a person wants or is qualified for.Read more about Underemployment. In many countries, a large share of work is also informal: unregistered, without a contract or social protection. The unemployment rate captures neither.

Detailed version

Definitions and survey design

The International Labour Organization’s standards define employment and unemployment for use in labour-force surveys, and statistical agencies such as Eurostat apply them: an unemployed person is without work during the reference week, available to start within two weeks, and has actively sought work in the past four weeks or found a job starting within three months (Eurostat). Labour-force surveys interview a sample of households each quarter or month, often in a rotating panel, and weight the results to population estimates (Eurostat).

Key ratios:

unemployment rate  = unemployed ÷ labour force
activity (participation) rate = labour force ÷ working-age population
employment rate    = employed ÷ working-age population

Age bands matter: Eurostat’s headline employment rate covers ages 20–64, while other measures use 15–64 or 15+ (Eurostat, Eurostat).

Because the figures are based on samples, monthly changes come with sampling error; a change of 0.1 percentage point is often within the margin of error (ONS).

Survey counts and registry counts

Registered unemployment counts people who sign on with a public employment service or claim unemployment benefits. It depends on benefit rules: tightening eligibility reduces the count without changing joblessness. Survey-based unemployment follows the international definition and is comparable across countries. The two series can move in different directions.

Broader measures of slack

Statistics offices publish wider measures that add discouraged workers, people available but not seeking, and the time-related underemployed. Comparing these with the headline rate shows how much slack the headline misses.

The natural rate and the Beveridge curve

The natural rate of unemployment (related to the NAIRU, the non-accelerating-inflation rate of unemployment) is the rate consistent with stable inflation, made up of frictional and structural unemployment. It is not observed and is estimated with uncertainty.

The Beveridge curve plots the unemployment rate against the job vacancy rate. Movements along the curve reflect the business cycle (more vacancies, less unemployment). Shifts of the curve outwards reflect a less efficient matching process, for example more skills mismatch. Search-and-matching theory, which explains why vacancies and unemployment coexist, was recognised by the 2010 Nobel Prize (Nobel Prize).

Okun’s relationship

Arthur Okun observed that changes in unemployment move inversely with output growth relative to trend. The relationship is a rule of thumb whose size differs across countries and periods, depending on labour laws and how firms adjust hours versus jobs.

Why people disagree

Economists disagree about how low unemployment can go without raising inflation. One view holds that the natural rate can be estimated well enough to guide policy, and that keeping unemployment below it leads to rising inflation. Another view holds that estimates of the natural rate are too uncertain to rely on and have been revised substantially in the past, so observed inflation and wages are a more reliable guide for policy. They also disagree on how much structural unemployment responds to policies such as training, benefits and employment protection (Nobel Prize).

Varies by country

Most statistics offices publish a survey-based rate using the international definition, for example the UK’s Labour Force Survey (ONS) and Australia’s monthly Labour Force survey (ABS). Some countries also publish a registered count that is widely reported, such as Germany’s figure from its Federal Employment Agency. Where informal work is common, the unemployment rate can be low while underemployment and informality are high.
