How Housing Markets Work

Why house prices rise and fall: supply, demand, credit, interest rates and expectations.

Intermediate·6 min (Simple) · 11 min (Detailed)·Updated 6 November 2026

Read first: Rent vs Buy: The Real Comparison

Key points

  • The supply of homes changes slowly, because building takes years; demand can change much faster.
  • Interest rates change how much buyers can borrow for the same monthly payment, and so what they can offer.
  • How easy it is to get credit, and changes in population and incomes, move housing markets through cycles.
  • A home is both a place to live and an asset, which creates tension in housing policy.
  • A price bubble can be identified with confidence only after the fact.

Simple: plain words. Detailed: exact figures, codes and methods.

In short: house prices are set by how many homes there are, how many people want them, and how much those people can borrow. Each of these moves at a different speed.

Simple version

Supply and demand for homes

The number of homes in a town changes slowly. Finding land, getting permission and building can take years, and only a small share of homes is new each year. The number of people who want to live there can change faster: a new employer opens, families grow, people move in from elsewhere.

When demand rises faster than the supply of homes, prices and rents tend to rise. When demand falls, or many new homes are finished at once, they tend to fall or rise more slowly.

Interest rates and prices

Most buyers borrow. What they can pay for a home depends on how much a monthly payment can borrow, and that depends on the interest rateInterest rateThe price of borrowing or the reward for lending, written as a percentage of the amount per period, usually per year.Read more about Interest rate.

Example

A buyer can afford ¤1,000 a month over 25 years (example).

Mortgage rate Amount ¤1,000 a month can borrow
3% about ¤210,900
6% about ¤155,200

At 6%, the same payment borrows about 26% less. If many buyers face the same change, what they can offer for homes falls too.

This link works in both directions. Falling rates let buyers borrow more, which tends to push prices up; rising rates do the opposite. Credit and cycles

Prices also depend on how easy it is to get a loan. When lenders accept smaller deposits or higher income multiples, more buyers can borrow more. When they tighten their rules, fewer can. Changes in population, incomes and lending rules together create cycles: periods when prices rise faster than incomes, and periods when they fall or stay flat.

Home and asset

A home is somewhere to live. It is also, for many households, their largest asset. Owners tend to gain when prices rise; people hoping to buy tend to face higher costs. Renters’ costs are linked to the same market through rents. This is one reason housing policy is often contested.

Do house prices only go up?

Over some long periods, house prices in many places have risen faster than general prices. But there are also long periods when they fell. In Japan, land prices fell for many years after the early 1990s. In the United States, national house prices fell between 2006 and 2012. Price data for many countries, published by the Bank for International Settlements, show both rises and falls (BIS). To compare prices across years, the Inflation: What Is Money Worth?Work out what an amount of money in one year is worth in another, using an inflation rate you enter or official consumer price index (CPI) levels. adjusts for changes in general prices.

Bubbles

A bubbleBubbleA period when prices rise far above any reasonable estimate of value, sustained by expectations of further rises.Read more about Bubble is a period when prices rise far above what rents, incomes and interest rates can explain, carried by the expectation of further rises. Prices that later fall look like a bubble afterwards. At the time, people disagree about whether high prices reflect real changes, such as low interest rates, or a bubble.

Detailed version

Supply elasticity

The price elasticity of housing supply measures how much new construction responds to a rise in prices. Where land is plentiful and building rules permit construction, extra demand mostly results in more homes; where land is scarce or regulation restricts building, it mostly results in higher prices. Glaeser and Gyourko review evidence that supply is restricted in some high-cost US areas and that this affects prices, mobility and output (Journal of Economic Perspectives, 2018); an earlier paper by Glaeser, Gyourko and Saks relates rising prices to the difficulty of obtaining permission to build (NBER, 2005).

Valuation metrics

Two ratios are standard:

price-to-income = average house price / average household income
price-to-rent   = house price / annual rent for an equivalent home

Analysts compare each with its own long-run average. The user cost approach (previous lessons) shows why: a fall in real interest rates lowers the user cost, so a higher price-to-rent ratio can be consistent with fundamentals. Himmelberg, Mayer and Sinai argue that price-to-rent ratios read without interest rates can mislead (Journal of Economic Perspectives, 2005). The BIS and Eurostat publish house price indices (BIS; Eurostat); indices show changes, so level comparisons across countries need care.

Credit cycles

Credit supply and house prices interact. Easier lending raises prices, which raises the collateral value of homes, which supports further lending. When the process reverses, falling prices reduce collateral and tighten lending. Mian and Sufi studied US zip codes and found that areas with a larger share of borrowers previously denied credit saw faster mortgage credit growth before 2007 and higher default rates afterwards (NBER, 2008).

Policy tools

  • Planning and zoning: rules on land use and density, which shape the supply response.
  • Taxes: recurrent property taxes, transaction taxes and the tax treatment of mortgage interest and capital gains on homes all change the cost of owning.
  • Macroprudential measures: limits on LTV, LTI, DTI or DSTI. The European Systemic Risk Board lists the measures EU countries apply (ESRB).
  • Public and social housing: direct provision of homes outside the market.

Episodes

  • Japan, 1990s onward: land and property prices fell for an extended period after a rise in the late 1980s.
  • United States, 2006–2012: national house prices fell after a period of rapid credit growth, followed by a wave of mortgage defaults (NBER, 2008).
  • Ireland, 2007 onward: prices fell sharply after a period of rapid lending growth; the Central Bank of Ireland later introduced mortgage measures limiting LTV and LTI.

Common misconceptions

  • “Prices are high because of one cause.” Supply, rates, credit and incomes interact, and their weights differ by place.
  • “New supply has no effect on prices.” In the standard model, extra supply lowers prices relative to what they would otherwise have been; it can coexist with rising prices when demand rises faster.

Why people disagree

Economists disagree about what matters most for house prices. One view stresses supply: where planning rules and land limits restrict building, demand turns into higher prices rather than more homes (Glaeser and Gyourko, 2018). Another view stresses credit and demand: easier lending and falling interest rates raise what buyers can pay, and expansions of credit to new borrowers have been followed by price rises and later defaults (Mian and Sufi, 2008). Many economists see both at work, with weights that differ between places and periods; the policy responses each view suggests, such as changing planning rules or tightening lending limits, are also debated.

Varies by country

Housing markets differ in structure. The share of households who own rather than rent differs widely across countries. In some, most mortgages have rates fixed for decades, so rate changes affect mainly new buyers; in others, most have variable or short fixed rates, so rate changes reach existing borrowers within a few years. Planning systems also differ: some allocate land through detailed local plans, others through zoning codes, and others through national rules.
