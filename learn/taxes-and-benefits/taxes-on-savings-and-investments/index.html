Taxes on Savings and Investments

Interest, dividends and capital gains are often taxed differently, and the timing of tax and the type of account change the result.

Intermediate·6 min (Simple) · 11 min (Detailed)·Updated 6 November 2026

Read first: Income Tax: Brackets, Marginal and Average Rates, Fees and Costs

Key points

  • Investments produce three main kinds of income: interest, dividends and capital gains.
  • Gains are usually taxed when they are realised (when the asset is sold), not while it is held.
  • Losses can offset gains under rules that vary by country.
  • Tax-advantaged accounts change when tax applies, and sometimes whether it applies at all.
  • Tax paid every year reduces the amount left to grow, so its effect builds over time in the same way that fees do.
  • Income from another country can have tax withheld at source; tax treaties can reduce it.

Simple: plain words. Detailed: exact figures, codes and methods.

In short: money that earns money can be taxed too. What kind of income it is, when it is taxed and which account holds it all change how much is left.

Simple version

Three kinds of investment income

  1. InterestInterestThe cost of borrowing money or the reward for lending it, expressed as a rate per period.Read more about Interest. A bank or borrower pays you for the use of your money. ¤5,000 in a savings account at 3% (example) earns ¤150 in a year.
  2. DividendsDividendA share of profits that a company pays to its shareholders.Read more about Dividend. A company pays part of its profit to its shareholders. Owning shares that pay ¤2 per share a year, 100 shares bring ¤200 (example).
  3. Capital gainsCapital gainThe profit from selling something for more than it cost.Read more about Capital gain. You sell something for more than you paid. Shares bought for ¤1,000 and sold for ¤1,400 give a gain of ¤400 (example).

Many countries tax these three in different ways, at different rates and with different allowances.

Gains on paper and gains in hand

If your shares rise from ¤1,000 to ¤1,400 and you keep them, the ¤400 gain is unrealised. It exists only on paper and can disappear if prices fall. When you sell, it becomes realised. In most systems, capital gains taxCapital gains taxA tax on the profit made when an asset is sold, with rules that vary by country.Read more about Capital gains tax applies when the gain is realised, not while you hold the asset.

If you sell at a loss, many systems let you subtract the loss from gains, sometimes in later years too. The rules on which losses count, and for how long, vary by country.

Accounts as wrappers

Many countries offer tax-advantaged accountsTax-advantaged accountAn account where saving or investing is taxed less, under rules that vary by country.Read more about Tax-advantaged account. They work like a wrapper around ordinary savings or investments. The wrapper changes the tax, not the investment inside. Common patterns:

  • no tax on income and gains inside the account,
  • tax relief on money paid in, with tax on money taken out later,
  • limits on how much can be paid in each year, and rules on when money can be taken out.

Why timing matters

Tax paid every year comes out of the money that would have kept growing. Tax paid once, at the end, leaves more to grow in the meantime.

Example

¤10,000 is invested for 20 years and earns 6% a year (example). The tax rate on investment income is 30% (example).

Case Value after 20 years
No tax about ¤32,071
Tax charged on the return every year (6% becomes 4.2%) about ¤22,770
Tax charged once, on the whole gain, at the end about ¤25,450

The tax rate is the same in each taxed case. Only the timing changes, and the difference is about ¤2,680.

This effect, often called tax drag, builds up in the same way as fee dragFee dragThe reduction in long-term returns caused by ongoing charges.Read more about Fee drag. The Investment Fee ImpactSee how much two yearly investment fees, for example 0.1 % and 1 %, take from the final balance over many years. shows how a yearly percentage taken from a balance adds up over decades.

Income from abroad

If you own shares in a company based in another country, that country may take tax from the dividend before you receive it. This is a withholding taxWithholding taxTax deducted at source before income is paid out.Read more about Withholding tax. Agreements between countries, called tax treaties, often reduce it, and your own country may give credit for tax already paid abroad.

Detailed version

After-tax return

annual taxation:   r_after = r × (1 − t)
                   FV = PV × (1 + r(1 − t))^n

deferred taxation: FV = PV × [ (1 + r)^n × (1 − t) + t ]
                   (tax t on the whole gain at the end)

With r = 6%, t = 30%, n = 20 and PV = ¤10,000: annual taxation gives 10,000 × 1.042^20 ≈ ¤22,770; deferral gives 10,000 × (1.06^20 × 0.7 + 0.3) ≈ ¤25,450. The gap is the value of deferral and grows with n, r and t. Inflation adds a further effect: tax on nominal returns can exceed the real return, so the effective tax rate on the real return is higher than t.

Lock-in

Because gains are taxed on realisation, selling an asset with a large unrealised gain brings forward a tax bill. Holders then keep assets longer than they otherwise would; economists call this the lock-in effect. Rules that reset the cost basis at death, or that tax gains at lower rates after a holding period, change the strength of lock-in. The United States, for example, distinguishes short-term from long-term gains by holding period (IRS).

Dividends: classical and imputation systems

Company profits can be taxed twice: once as corporate profit and again as a dividend to the shareholder.

  • Classical system: both taxes apply in full, sometimes with a reduced rate on dividends. The United States taxes qualified dividends at rates set for that category (IRS).
  • Imputation system: shareholders receive a credit for corporate tax already paid on the profit behind the dividend. Australia and New Zealand use imputation.
  • Partial relief: a dividend allowance or lower rate. The United Kingdom has a dividend allowance and separate dividend rates (GOV.UK).

Wrappers

Wrappers are of two broad types: “taxed in, untaxed out” (contributions from after-tax income; returns and withdrawals free of tax, as in the United Kingdom’s ISA (GOV.UK)) and “untaxed in, taxed out” (relief on contributions; withdrawals taxed as income, common for pensions). If the tax rate is the same at both ends, the two give the same after-tax result; they differ when rates change between contribution and withdrawal.

Wealth taxes

A few countries, for example Norway, Spain and Switzerland, levy a tax on net wealth above a threshold each year, in addition to taxes on income from it. Valuation of unlisted assets and mobility of wealthy taxpayers are the main practical issues discussed in the literature.

Cross-border reporting

Withholding on cross-border dividends and interest can be reduced under a treaty, often by filing a form with the payer (IRS). Financial institutions in many countries report foreign account holders to tax authorities: the US FATCA regime requires foreign institutions to report on US account holders (IRS), and the OECD’s Common Reporting Standard organises similar automatic exchange among participating countries.

Why people disagree

Whether income from saving should be taxed, and how heavily, is debated. One view holds that taxing returns to saving taxes the same income twice (once when earned, again when its savings earn a return) and discourages investment, so the tax base should be consumption; the flat tax proposed by Hall and Rabushka taxes wages and business cash flow but not interest, dividends or gains at the household level (Hoover Institution). Another view holds that capital income is concentrated among the wealthiest households and that differences in inheritance and returns justify taxing it; Saez and Stantcheva develop a model in which taxing capital income is part of an optimal system (NBER, 2016). The answer depends on how saving responds to taxes and on how fairness between people with the same earnings but different wealth is weighed.

Varies by country

In the United Kingdom, Capital Gains Tax is charged on gains above an annual exempt amount, and ISAs shelter income and gains (GOV.UK). In the United States, long-term and short-term gains are taxed at different rates, and losses can offset gains with a limit on how much can offset other income each year (IRS). Australia credits shareholders for company tax already paid on dividends through franking credits. Allowances and rates change over time; each tax authority publishes the current figures.
