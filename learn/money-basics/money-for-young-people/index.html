Money for Young People

A first lesson written for readers under 18: pocket money, a first account, a first job and a first phone contract.

Beginner·4 min (Simple) · 8 min (Detailed)·Updated 6 November 2026

Key points

  • Money is limited, so every choice to spend on one thing means giving up something else.
  • A bank or mobile-money account for someone under 18 usually needs a parent or guardian, and its limits vary by country.
  • A first pay slip shows gross pay, deductions and net pay.
  • A phone, game or app subscription is a contract that renews and charges until it is cancelled; an adult is usually the one responsible for it.
  • Nobody honest asks a young person online for money, codes or access to an account.

Simple: plain words. Detailed: exact figures, codes and methods.

In short: money is limited, so every spending choice gives something else up. This lesson follows four young people through four common money moments.

Simple version

Story 1: saving for something

Amira gets ¤5 of pocket money a week (example). She wants headphones that cost ¤60. If she saves ¤3 a week and spends ¤2, she reaches ¤60 in 60 ÷ 3 = 20 weeks.

She could save all ¤5 and get there in 12 weeks, but then she has nothing to spend for three months. There is no right answer. This is the first idea in economics: money is limited, so choosing one thing means giving up another. Economists call what you give up the opportunity costOpportunity costWhat is given up by choosing one option over the next best one.Read more about Opportunity cost.

Story 2: a first wage

Leo gets a weekend job. His first pay slip shows three lines (example):

Line Amount What it means
Gross pay ¤400 Pay before anything is taken off
Deductions ¤40 Tax or contributions taken off, if any apply
Net pay ¤360 What actually arrives in his account

Whether young workers pay tax or contributions, and how much, varies by country and by how much they earn. The pay slip shows what was taken off and why.

To get paid, Leo needs an account. In many countries, an account for someone under 18 needs a parent or guardian to sign, and it may have limits on spending or borrowing.

Story 3: a subscription that renews

Sam signs up for a game subscription at ¤4.99 a month (example). It is easy to forget. Over a year it costs 12 × ¤4.99 = ¤59.88. Many subscriptions start with a free trial and then charge automatically each month until someone cancels.

A subscription is a contract: an agreement that has rules both sides agree to. For young people, the account holder or card owner, usually a parent, is the person responsible for paying. That is why many app stores and phone plans ask for an adult’s approval.

Story 4: a message asking for a code

Jo gets a message in a game chat: “Send me your login code and I’ll give you free coins.” Another friend-of-a-friend offers ¤50 to “receive some money in your account and pass it on”.

Both are scams. A one-time code lets someone else get into an account. Passing money through your account for a stranger is called being a “money mule”, and it is linked to crime. Banks, games and real friends do not ask for codes or for the use of an account. The person to talk to when a message like this arrives is a parent, carer, teacher or other adult you trust.

Ten words in one line each

  1. Budget: a plan for money you have not spent yet.
  2. Income: money that comes in.
  3. Expense: money that goes out.
  4. Saving: money kept for later.
  5. Interest: a payment for borrowing or lending money.
  6. Gross pay: pay before anything is taken off.
  7. Net pay: pay after deductions.
  8. Subscription: a payment that repeats until it is cancelled.
  9. Contract: an agreement with rules that both sides accept.
  10. Scam: a dishonest scheme to take money or information.

Detailed version

Evidence on young people’s financial literacy

The OECD’s PISA programme, best known for testing reading, mathematics and science, includes an optional financial literacy assessment for 15-year-old students in participating countries and economies. Results differ widely between and within countries, and the assessment also asks about students’ access to accounts, payment cards and earnings. Survey results like these are one reason many education systems now include money topics in the curriculum.

Legal capacity and account rules by age

The age at which a person can enter a binding contract, and open accounts alone, is set by national law.

  • In England and Wales, a person under 18 is a minor. Most contracts with minors cannot be enforced against them, with exceptions for necessities. The Minors’ Contracts Act 1987 deals with some of these rules (legislation.gov.uk).
  • In Scotland, the Age of Legal Capacity (Scotland) Act 1991 sets general legal capacity to enter transactions at 16 (legislation.gov.uk).
  • In Japan, the age of adulthood was lowered from 20 to 18 in 2022, so 18- and 19-year-olds can now sign contracts such as phone or credit agreements without a parent’s consent.

Banks and mobile-money providers add their own rules on top: minimum ages, a parent or guardian as joint holder, spending caps and no overdraft or credit.

Fraud patterns aimed at young people

  • Money muling. Europol describes criminal groups recruiting people to move illicit money through their accounts, often with fake job offers or offers of easy money, and reports recruitment of people aged 12 to 21 (Europol). Account holders who do this can face account closure and criminal investigation.
  • Gaming-item and account scams. Offers of free in-game currency, skins or items in exchange for login details or a one-time code, or fake trading sites for items.
  • Social-media marketplace scams. Goods that do not arrive after payment, or payments outside the platform’s protection.

Security agencies recommend unique passwords and two-step verification on important accounts (NCSC). Reporting routes vary: in the United States, fraud can be reported to the Federal Trade Commission (FTC); in Australia, to Scamwatch (Scamwatch); in the United Kingdom, to Action Fraud, the national reporting centre. Banks and game platforms also have their own reporting channels.

Notes for parents and teachers

  • The Simple layer is written to be read with or by a young person; each story ends with an open question rather than a rule.
  • The four stories map onto later lessons: budgeting (Module 2), accounts (Module 3), pay slips (lesson 1.2) and scams (Module 15).
  • The savings-goal and subscription-audit tools work with made-up numbers, so a class can use them without real data.
  • Teaching children about money (lesson 2.7) covers age bands and family approaches.

Varies by country

Ages and account rules differ. In Scotland, general legal capacity starts at 16; in England and Wales and in Japan, adulthood starts at 18; in the United States, the age of majority is set by each state and is 18 in most of them. Whether a 16-year-old can open an account alone, get a payment card or sign a phone contract depends on both the law and the provider’s own rules.
