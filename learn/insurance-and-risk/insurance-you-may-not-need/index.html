Insurance That Often Pays the Seller More Than You

Add-on cover such as extended warranties, payment protection and gadget plans, and how its value can be checked.

Everyday·5 min (Simple) · 8 min (Detailed)·Updated 6 November 2026

Read first: Premiums, Deductibles and Excess

Key points

  • Add-on insurance sold at the point of sale has, in several regulator reviews, paid out a small share of premiums in claims while paying high commissions.
  • Legal consumer rights may already cover some of the same faults.
  • Expected value compares the price with the chance of a claim times the likely payout.
  • Add-on cover often overlaps with policies a person already has.
  • Cooling-off periods in many countries allow insurance to be cancelled within a short time after purchase.

Simple: plain words. Detailed: exact figures, codes and methods.

In short: some insurance is offered at the moment you buy something else: a phone, a car, a loan, a flight. Its value can be checked with the same tools as any other insurance.

Simple version

What add-on insurance is

Add-on insurance is sold together with another product. Common examples:

  • Extended warranty: pays for repairs after the manufacturer’s warranty ends.
  • Gadget or phone cover: pays for damage, loss or theft of a device.
  • Payment protection: pays loan or card repayments if you lose your job or fall ill.
  • Car add-ons: cover for the gap between a car’s value and the loan, tyres, or small dents.

The offer comes at checkout, when attention is on the main purchase and there is little time to look at alternatives.

Rights you may already have

In many countries the law already gives buyers rights when goods are faulty. These are called statutory rightsStatutory rightsProtections that the law gives buyers, whatever a seller's own policy says.Read more about Statutory rights. A warrantyWarrantyA seller's or maker's promise to repair or replace a product for a period.Read more about Warranty sold for extra money adds to those rights; it does not replace them. Part of what an extended warranty covers may already be covered by law for a period.

Varies by country

  • European Union: sellers are responsible for faults present when goods are delivered that appear within at least two years.
  • United Kingdom: the Consumer Rights Act 2015 says goods must be of satisfactory quality, fit for purpose and as described.
  • Australia: consumer guarantees apply automatically, and the competition regulator states that businesses must not pressure or mislead consumers into buying an extended warranty.

The expected-value check

The expected valueExpected valueThe average outcome of a decision if it were repeated many times: each result weighted by its probability.Read more about Expected value compares what you pay with the average amount you would get back if the same situation were repeated many times.

Example

An extended warranty costs ¤90 for three years on a ¤400 appliance (examples). Suppose there is a 10 % chance of a fault that legal rights do not already cover, and the average repair costs ¤150.

Expected payout = 10 % × ¤150 = ¤15. The price is ¤90. On these numbers, the buyer pays ¤90 for cover with an expected payout of about ¤15.

If the payout were large enough to cause real hardship, paying more than the average could still make sense, as with any insurance. A ¤150 repair is a loss many households could absorb from an emergency fundEmergency fundMoney set aside to cover essential costs during a loss of income or an unexpected bill.Read more about Emergency fund.

Expected ValueMultiply each outcome by its probability to see the average result of a choice: a general table, an extended warranty, an insurance deductible choice or a lottery ticket.

The overlap check

Add-on cover often duplicates something already in place:

  • Home contents insurance may cover gadgets, including away from home.
  • Some bank accounts and credit cards include travel or purchase protection.
  • Income protection or employer benefits may already cover loan repayments during illness.

The cooling-off period

In many countries, insurance can be cancelled within a short cooling-off periodCooling-off periodA set number of days after signing certain contracts during which the buyer can cancel without penalty.Read more about Cooling-off period after purchase, often with a full or partial refund. This gives time to check cover away from the checkout. The length and conditions differ by country and product.

Detailed version

Claims ratios and commissions

The claims ratio (loss ratio) is claims paid divided by premiums earned. Products with low claims ratios return a small share of each premium to customers; the rest goes to commissions, costs and profit.

claims ratio = claims paid ÷ premiums earned
example: ¤15 expected payout ÷ ¤90 premium ≈ 0.17

Regulator reviews have examined add-on products specifically. The Australian Securities and Investments Commission reviewed add-on insurance sold through car dealers and concluded that it represented poor value for consumers (ASIC REP 492). In the United Kingdom, the Financial Conduct Authority states that payment protection insurance was often mis-sold, which led to a large redress programme (FCA). The FCA also publishes value measures data on claims frequencies and acceptance rates across general insurance products (FCA).

Point-of-sale conduct rules

Responses to these findings include:

  • Information duties: in the European Union, the Insurance Distribution Directive requires a demands-and-needs test and a standard product information document, and covers cross-selling with other products (EUR-Lex).
  • Deferred sales: some regimes require a gap between the main purchase and the sale of the add-on.
  • Product governance: rules that require firms to show a product offers fair value to its target market.

Behavioural framing at checkout

Several features of the checkout setting are documented in behavioural research: the add-on price looks small next to the main price (relative framing); the decision is made under time pressure; recent attention to the product’s possible faults raises their perceived probability; and a pre-ticked or default option shifts choices. These are features of how people decide, not failings of particular buyers.

Statutory rights versus warranties

Statutory rights attach to the sale and are against the seller; warranties are contracts, often with a third party. The ACCC notes that warranties apply in addition to the consumer guarantees under Australian Consumer Law (ACCC). The EU legal guarantee runs for at least two years (Your Europe). The UK statutory framework is set out in the Consumer Rights Act 2015 (legislation.gov.uk).

Common misconceptions

  • “The warranty is the only protection.” Statutory rights exist alongside it in many countries.
  • “A low price means low value.” Value depends on the claims ratio and the size of the loss covered, not the absolute price.
