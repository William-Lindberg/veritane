When Debt Becomes a Problem

Warning signs, what lenders and debt collectors can and cannot do, and where free help exists.

Everyday·6 min (Simple) · 10 min (Detailed)·Updated 6 November 2026

Read first: Paying Down Debt

Key points

  • Common warning signs are borrowing to repay other debts, going without essentials to make payments, and avoiding letters or calls from lenders.
  • Contacting lenders early usually opens more options, such as reduced payments or a pause, than waiting until accounts are in default.
  • Free, non-profit debt advice exists in many countries; some paid debt-management firms charge fees for services that free advisers provide.
  • Debt collection is regulated, and harassment by collectors is unlawful in many countries.
  • Formal insolvency options, such as repayment plans and bankruptcy, exist in many countries and differ in how they work.

Simple: plain words. Detailed: exact figures, codes and methods.

In short: debt becomes a problem when repayments crowd out essentials or need new borrowing. At that point there are more options than many people expect, and free help exists in many countries.

Simple version

Signs that debt is becoming hard to manage

Debt problems often build slowly. Common signs include:

  • using a credit card, overdraft or new loan to make payments on other debts;
  • paying only the minimum, month after month, while the balance stays about the same;
  • going without food, heating or other essentials to keep up with payments;
  • missing payments, or not opening letters and answering calls from lenders;
  • feeling worried about money most of the time.

Debt problems can happen to anyone, and they often start with something outside a person’s control: illness, a job loss, a relationship ending or a rise in prices. They are a circumstance, not a personal failing.

Talking to lenders early

Lenders in many countries have set processes for borrowers in difficulty, and in some countries the rules require them. When a borrower gets in touch early, options may include:

  • a lower payment for a few months;
  • a short pause in payments;
  • freezing interest or fees;
  • a longer term, so each payment is smaller.

These options are easier to arrange before an account goes into defaultDefaultFailing to keep to the terms of a loan, above all by missing payments; in other contexts, the option that applies when no choice is made.Read more about Default (when the lender treats the debt as not being repaid under the contract).

Priority and non-priority debts

Some debts have more serious consequences than others if they are not paid. Debt advisers often call these priority debts: rent or mortgage payments, energy bills, taxes and court fines. Missing them can lead to losing a home, being disconnected or legal action. Credit cards and personal loans are usually non-priority: missing them costs fees and harms your credit history, but the consequences are less immediate.

Example

Someone has ¤1,800 (example) coming in each month. Rent, food, energy and transport take ¤1,500. That leaves ¤300, but the minimum payments on their three non-priority debts add up to ¤600.

A common approach used by debt advisers is a pro-rata offer: the ¤300 is split in proportion to what each creditor is owed. With debts of ¤3,000, ¤5,000 and ¤600 (¤8,600 in total), the offers would be about ¤104.65, ¤174.42 and ¤20.93 a month.

Free help

Many countries have free, independent debt advice, run by charities, non-profits or public bodies. Advisers can help draw up a budget, talk to creditors, and explain formal options. Examples include the National Debt Helpline in Australia (NDH) and the free debt advice services listed by the UK government (GOV.UK).

Some companies charge fees to manage debts or to negotiate with lenders. Some of these services are available free from non-profit advisers. Asking whether a service charges, and how much, shows the difference.

What debt collectors may not do

Debt collection is regulated in many countries. Rules commonly forbid collectors from:

  • threatening violence or arrest;
  • lying about the amount owed or about what will happen;
  • calling at unreasonable hours or contacting someone too often;
  • telling employers, friends or neighbours about the debt.

When there is no way to repay

If debts cannot be repaid in a reasonable time, most countries have formal options. These include repayment plans over several years and, in some cases, bankruptcy, after which most remaining debts are written off. They have serious effects, including on credit files, and the rules differ by country. A later lesson on insolvency covers them in more detail.

Detailed version

Forbearance and hardship frameworks

Forbearance is any concession a lender grants to a borrower in financial difficulty: reduced or deferred payments, interest freezes, term extensions or capitalisation of arrears. In the United Kingdom, the Financial Conduct Authority’s consumer credit rules require firms to treat customers in default or arrears with forbearance and due consideration (FCA, CONC). Other jurisdictions use hardship-variation rights in credit law or rely on lenders’ own policies. Forbearance is usually reported on the credit file, and arrears often continue to accrue unless interest is frozen.

Collection regulation examples

  • United States: the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act and Regulation F prohibit harassment, false or misleading statements and unfair practices by debt collectors, and set rules on communications and validation notices (Regulation F).
  • Australia: the regulators publish a debt collection guideline for collectors and creditors (ASIC).
  • United Kingdom: collection by credit firms and debt collectors falls under the FCA’s consumer credit rules (FCA, CONC).

Limitation periods

Limitation (statute of limitations) rules set how long a creditor has to take court action. In England and Wales, the Limitation Act 1980 sets six years for actions on simple contracts, which covers most unsecured consumer debts (Limitation Act 1980). In the United States, periods are set by each state. In many systems, a payment or written acknowledgement restarts the clock, and an expired debt may still exist and appear on a credit file even though it cannot be enforced in court.

Insolvency regimes at a high level

Feature Repayment-plan route Liquidation / discharge route
Mechanism Pay what is affordable for a set period; the rest is written off Non-exempt assets are realised; remaining debts are discharged
US example Chapter 13 plan, typically three to five years Chapter 7 liquidation (US Courts)
UK example Individual voluntary arrangement Bankruptcy, usually ending after 12 months (GOV.UK)
Canada example Consumer proposal Bankruptcy, both administered by licensed insolvency trustees (OSB)

In the EU, the restructuring and insolvency directive requires access to a full discharge of debt for over-indebted entrepreneurs, within a period of no more than three years in most cases, and allows member states to extend this to consumers (Directive (EU) 2019/1023). US filers must receive credit counselling from an approved agency before filing (US Courts).

Credit-report impact

Defaults, judgements and insolvencies are typically recorded on credit files for several years, with periods set by national law. During that time, access to mainstream credit is limited and prices are higher. Analysts reading household-debt data look at arrears rates, insolvency statistics and debt-service ratios together, since each captures a different stage of distress.

Common misconceptions

  • “Ignoring letters makes the debt go away.” Interest, fees and legal steps usually continue.
  • “Paid debt help is the only option.” Free non-profit and public services exist in many countries.
  • “Bankruptcy clears every debt.” Some debts, such as certain fines or secured debts, are often excluded or treated differently.

Varies by country

Help and formal options differ. In the United Kingdom, the government lists free debt advice and formal options such as debt relief, individual voluntary arrangements and bankruptcy (GOV.UK). In Australia, the National Debt Helpline offers free financial counselling (NDH). In Canada, consumer proposals and bankruptcies are administered by licensed insolvency trustees under federal rules (OSB). In the United States, bankruptcy is handled by federal courts, and debt collection is regulated at federal and state level (US Courts).

Why people disagree

How easy should it be to have debts written off? One view stresses a “fresh start”: people who cannot repay are more productive, healthier and more likely to work or start businesses again if debts are discharged quickly, which is part of the reasoning behind the EU’s three-year discharge rule for entrepreneurs (Directive (EU) 2019/1023). Another view stresses repayment: easy discharge can raise the cost of credit for all borrowers, reduce lending to people with less income, and weaken the expectation that contracts are honoured. Countries balance these views differently, for example in how long repayment plans last and which debts can be discharged.
