The Real Cost of Borrowing

How to turn an interest rate, fees and a loan term into numbers that can be compared from one offer to another.

Everyday·6 min (Simple) · 11 min (Professional)·Updated 6 November 2026

Key points

  • The total cost of a loan is the clearest single comparison; the monthly payment alone can hide it.
  • The annual percentage rate (APR) combines the interest rate and most fees into one yearly figure, and its exact legal definition varies by country.
  • A longer term lowers each payment but raises the total interest paid.
  • A fixed rate stays the same for an agreed period; a variable rate follows a benchmark rate and can go up or down.
  • Fees for repaying early change the calculation if a loan might be paid off ahead of schedule.
Depth:

In short: a loan’s real cost is everything you pay back minus what you borrowed. The APRAnnual percentage rate (APR)The yearly cost of borrowing as a percentage, usually including interest and most fees, with exact rules that vary by country.Read more about Annual percentage rate (APR) (the yearly cost of borrowing including most fees) turns that into a rate, so offers with different fees and terms can be lined up.

Simple version

Two offers, side by side

Lenders often advertise the monthly payment. It is the number that has to fit your budget, but on its own it does not show the cost.

Example

Two offers for a ¤10,000 loan (example):

Offer A Offer B
Interest rate 7% a year 6% a year
Term 3 years 5 years
Arrangement fee none ¤300, taken from the loan
Monthly payment ¤308.77 ¤193.33
Total repaid ¤11,115.75 ¤11,599.68
Total cost (interest + fees) ¤1,115.75 ¤1,899.68
APR about 7.0% about 7.3%

Offer B has the lower interest rate and the lower monthly payment. It also costs about ¤784 more in total, because the money is borrowed for two extra years and there is a fee. Its APR is higher than Offer A’s, even though its interest rate is lower.

The pattern is general: a lower monthly payment can mean a higher total cost. A longer term spreads the debt thinner, but interest is charged for longer.

What the APR adds

The interest rate is the price of the money. Many loans also carry fees: arrangement fees, account fees or compulsory insurance. The APR rolls the rate and most of these fees into one yearly percentage. Two loans with the same APR cost about the same per year, whatever their mix of rate and fees.

The APR has limits. It does not include every possible fee (for example, late-payment fees), and its exact rules vary by country. It also compares cost per year, not total cost, so a long loan at a low APR can still cost more in money than a short loan at a higher APR.

Fixed and variable rates

  • Fixed rateFixed rateAn interest rate that does not change for an agreed period.Read more about Fixed rate: the interest rate stays the same for an agreed period. Payments are predictable.
  • Variable rateVariable rateAn interest rate that can change, often following a benchmark.Read more about Variable rate: the rate moves with a benchmark rateBenchmark rateA published reference interest rate to which variable-rate loans and other contracts are tied.Read more about Benchmark rate, such as the central bank’s rate or a market rate. Payments can go up or down.

A fixed rate is sometimes a little higher at the start, because the lender carries the chance that rates rise. A variable rate passes that chance to the borrower.

Repaying early

Some loans charge a fee if you repay early, because the lender loses interest it planned to earn. Others allow early repayment free of charge, or up to a set amount each year. This matters if you might sell a car, receive a lump sum, or move the loan to a cheaper lender.

Reading the key facts

Before signing, lenders in many countries have to give a standard summary. It is often called a key facts documentKey facts documentA short standardised summary of a product's costs, terms and the ways money can be lost, which many countries require.Read more about Key facts document or a pre-contract information sheet. It typically shows the APR, the total amount to repay, the number and size of payments, and the fees for late or early repayment. Putting two of these side by side is a direct way to see the difference between offers.

Professional version

Calculating an APR

An APR is the internal rate of return of the loan’s cash flows from the borrower’s side: the rate at which the money actually received equals the present value of everything paid.

cash received = Σ payments_k ÷ (1 + x)^k        (x = monthly rate, solved numerically)
US-style nominal APR      = 12 × x
EU-style APRC (effective) = (1 + x)^12 − 1

For Offer B, cash received is ¤9,700 (the ¤300 fee is deducted) against 60 payments of ¤193.33. Solving gives x ≈ 0.606% a month: a nominal APR of 7.27% and an effective APR of 7.52%. For Offer A, with no fees, the nominal APR equals the 7% contract rate and the effective figure is 7.23%. The EU annual percentage rate of charge is defined on the effective basis, with a standard formula and assumptions set out in the consumer credit directive (Directive (EU) 2023/2225); the US APR under the Truth in Lending rules is a nominal annual rate (Regulation Z).

Representative and personal APR

Advertised rates are often representative: in the United Kingdom, a representative APR is one that at least 51% of customers taking the product receive (FCA, CONC). The personal APR offered after an application can be higher, depending on the applicant’s risk.

Total cost of credit

total cost of credit = Σ payments + Σ fees paid separately − amount borrowed

EU and US disclosure rules require this figure (as the “total cost of credit” or the “finance charge”) alongside the APR.

Benchmarks and margins

A variable rate is typically benchmark + margin. The benchmark (a policy rate or a market reference rate) moves with monetary conditions (Bank of England); the margin reflects the lender’s costs, risk and profit and is usually fixed in the contract.

Worked example (illustrative): ¤10,000 over 60 months at benchmark 3% + margin 3% = 6% gives ¤193.33 a month. If the benchmark rises 2 percentage points after 24 months, the remaining balance of ¤6,354.89 is repaid over 36 months at 8%, and the payment rises to ¤199.14. Had the rate been 8% from the start, the payment would have been ¤202.76.

Prepayment charges

Early-repayment charges are often a percentage of the amount repaid (1% of ¤6,354.89 is ¤63.55) or a number of months’ interest. EU law caps compensation for early repayment of consumer credit and sets conditions on when it can be charged (Directive 2008/48/EC). A refinancing decision compares the interest saved with the charge plus any new fees.

Common misconceptions

  • “The lowest rate is the cheapest loan.” Fees and term can reverse the ranking.
  • “APR is total cost.” It is a yearly rate; total cost also depends on how long the money is borrowed.
  • “Variable rates follow the central bank exactly.” Pass-through depends on the benchmark and the contract.

Varies by country

APR rules differ. In the European Union, lenders show an annual percentage rate of charge (APRC), calculated on an effective basis with a standard formula (Directive (EU) 2023/2225). In the United States, the Truth in Lending rules require an APR and a finance charge, and the APR is a nominal rate (Regulation Z). In the United Kingdom, adverts show a representative APR that at least 51% of customers taking the product receive (FCA). Which fees are included differs between these definitions.
