Revisions, Uncertainty and Data Quality

Official numbers are estimates that change; how to read them with the right degree of confidence.

Professional·5 min (Simple) · 9 min (Detailed)·Updated 6 November 2026

Read first: Reading a Statistical Release

Key points

  • First estimates are built from partial data and are revised, sometimes by a noticeable amount.
  • Statistics offices publish revision histories and, for surveys, margins of error.
  • Seasonal factors are re-estimated as new data arrive, which also revises past figures.
  • Comparisons across countries or over time need matching definitions and the same data vintage.
  • A single month is one data point, not a trend.

Simple: plain words. Detailed: exact figures, codes and methods.

In short: the first number published for a month or a quarter is a careful first draft. Reading it well means knowing how much drafts usually change.

Simple version

The first number is a draft

Statistics offices face a trade-off. People want numbers quickly, but complete information takes months to arrive: company accounts, tax records, late survey replies. So offices publish an early estimate from the data they have, then update it as more arrives. Each update is a revisionRevisionA later change to a published statistic as more complete data arrive.Read more about Revision.

Revisions are not mistakes. They are the planned result of publishing early. Codes of practice for official statistics ask offices to explain revisions openly (OSR).

Example

A first estimate says the economy grew 0.2% in a quarter (example). Three months later it is revised to 0.4%. The revision is 0.2 percentage points. In words, growth was twice as fast as first thought; in points, the change is small. Both descriptions are accurate, and the difference in wording changes how a reader hears it.

Other reasons numbers change

  • Seasonal patterns are re-estimated every year, which nudges past adjusted figures.
  • Methods improve. An office may change how it measures something and rebuild the whole history.
  • Population estimates used to scale survey results are updated, for example after a census.

Margins of error

Many figures come from surveys of a sample of households or firms. A survey result has a margin of error. If an unemployment rate is 5.0% with a margin of ±0.2 points (example), a move to 5.1% next month is within the range that sampling alone could produce.

Three questions before quoting a statistic

  1. Is it preliminary? Words like “flash”, “advance”, “provisional” or “first estimate” mean revisions are expected.
  2. How large are revisions or margins of error, usually? Many releases say so in their notes.
  3. Is the comparison like for like? Same definition, same period, same adjustment, and ideally numbers published at the same time.

Why early numbers are still useful

Revisions do not make first estimates useless. Statistics offices publish analyses of their own revisions, which show how large they tend to be for each series (ONS). Central banks, governments and firms act on first estimates every month, because waiting a year for final data would mean acting too late. The skill is to hold the first number with the right amount of confidence: as the best current estimate, not as a final fact.

One month is not a trend

A single month can move for reasons that do not last: weather, a strike, a holiday falling in a different week, or sampling noise. A run of months in the same direction, confirmed by other indicators, says more.

Detailed version

Release stages

Most national accounts follow staged estimates. Eurostat publishes flash estimates of euro-area GDP and inflation ahead of fuller releases (Eurostat). The US Bureau of Economic Analysis publishes successive quarterly GDP estimates and later annual and comprehensive updates (BEA). The UK’s ONS publishes a revisions policy and analyses of revisions to its main series (ONS). Annual and benchmark revisions can change several years of history at once.

Vintages and real-time data

Each published version of a series is a vintage. Research on how policymakers or forecasters acted has to use the data available at the time, not today’s revised history. The Federal Reserve Bank of Philadelphia maintains a real-time data set of US macroeconomic vintages for this purpose (Philadelphia Fed); other central banks and the OECD keep similar archives.

Revision analysis

Two summary statistics are standard:

mean revision (bias)           = average of (later − earlier)
mean absolute revision (size)  = average of |later − earlier|

Example revisions to five quarters (example): +0.2, −0.1, +0.3, 0.0, +0.1 percentage points. Mean revision = 0.5 ÷ 5 = +0.10 (first estimates tended to be low); mean absolute revision = 0.7 ÷ 5 = 0.14. A mean revision that differs significantly from zero suggests systematic bias in early estimates; the absolute measure shows how far a single first estimate can be trusted. Turning points are where early estimates are often least reliable.

Sampling and non-sampling error

  • Sampling error comes from observing a sample; it is quantifiable and published as confidence intervals or standard errors.
  • Non-sampling error comes from non-response, misreporting, coverage gaps, imputation and model assumptions (including seasonal adjustment). It is often larger and harder to quantify. Falling survey response rates have made it a growing concern for several statistics offices.

Quality frameworks

The UK Code of Practice for Statistics is built on trustworthiness, quality and value (OSR). The European Statistics Code of Practice sets principles on professional independence, accuracy and reliability, timeliness and punctuality, and coherence and comparability (Eurostat). The IMF’s data standards initiatives play a similar role internationally. These frameworks treat the trade-off between timeliness and accuracy as a design choice to be documented, not hidden.

How Veritane labels it

In the News section, data items carry a flag when the publisher marks a figure as preliminary (for example a Eurostat flash estimate or an ONS provisional figure), and the item names the release as its source. Projections are labelled separately from outturns, so a forecast is not read as a measured figure.
