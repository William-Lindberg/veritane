Seasonally Adjusted, Annualised, and Other Labels

What SA, SAAR, NSA, y/y and 3m/3m mean, and when each one can mislead.

Professional·5 min (Simple) · 9 min (Detailed)·Updated 6 November 2026

Read first: Reading a Statistical Release

Key points

  • Seasonal adjustment removes regular calendar patterns from a series; it is an estimate, and it is revised.
  • An annualised rate compounds a short-period change as if it continued for a whole year, which magnifies noise as well as trend.
  • Year-on-year changes are smoother but react later to turning points.
  • Calendar and working-day adjustment is a separate step from seasonal adjustment.
  • Countries report growth on different bases, so an annualised figure and a quarterly figure are not comparable without conversion.

Simple: plain words. Detailed: exact figures, codes and methods.

In short: the small labels next to an economic number (SA, SAAR, y/y) change what the number means. The same economy can show 0.5% growth and 2.0% growth in the same quarter, depending on the label.

Simple version

Why seasons get in the way

Many things follow the calendar. Ice-cream sales are higher in summer. Shops sell more before major holidays. Building work slows in winter in cold countries. If you compare July ice-cream sales with June, you mostly learn that July is warmer.

Seasonal adjustmentSeasonal adjustmentRemoving regular calendar patterns from a data series so that underlying changes show.Read more about Seasonal adjustment removes these regular patterns, so that a change from one month to the next shows what is new. The adjusted figure is labelled SA (seasonally adjusted). The raw figure is NSA (not seasonally adjusted).

Seasonal adjustment is an estimate. Statistics offices work out the “normal” pattern from past years, and they update it as new years arrive. That is one reason past figures get revised.

Year on year as a cross-check

Year-on-year figures compare a month with the same month a year earlier, so summer is compared with summer and winter with winter. Seasons cancel out without any adjustment. The cost is speed: a year-on-year figure includes everything that happened over the past twelve months, so it turns later when the economy changes direction. Many readers look at both: the short comparison for direction, the yearly comparison for the bigger picture.

The annualisation pitfall

An annualised rateAnnualised rateA change over a short period scaled up as if it continued for a full year, which magnifies both trends and noise.Read more about Annualised rate answers: “if this quarter’s pace continued for a year, what would the yearly rise be?”

Example

An economy grows 0.5% in a quarter (example).

  • Reported quarter on quarter: 0.5%.
  • Reported annualised: 1.005 × 1.005 × 1.005 × 1.005 − 1 = 2.02%.

Both describe the same quarter. A headline that compares one country’s 2.0% with another country’s 0.5% may be comparing identical growth.

Monthly figures stretch even further. A 0.3% monthly rise in prices, annualised, is 3.66%.

Varies by country

Varies by country

  • United States: quarterly GDP growth is reported as a seasonally adjusted annual rate (BEA).
  • Euro area and United Kingdom: quarterly GDP growth is reported quarter on quarter, not annualised, alongside year-on-year growth.
  • Japan: official releases show both the quarter-on-quarter change and its annualised equivalent.

Detailed version

How seasonal adjustment works

A series is modelled as components: trend-cycle, seasonal, calendar effects and an irregular residual. In a multiplicative model, Y = T × S × C × I; in an additive model, Y = T + S + C + I. The seasonally adjusted series is Y with S (and usually C) removed; it still contains the irregular, so it is not a trend (Eurostat; ABS).

Calendar adjustment is a separate step that corrects for the number of working days, leap years and moving holidays such as Easter (Eurostat). A series can be calendar adjusted without being seasonally adjusted.

Methods in one paragraph

Two families dominate. X-13ARIMA-SEATS, maintained by the US Census Bureau, extends the series with an ARIMA forecast and then applies moving-average filters (the X-11 approach) or model-based signal extraction (SEATS) (US Census Bureau). TRAMO-SEATS fits a regression-ARIMA model (TRAMO) to handle outliers and calendar effects, then decomposes it (SEATS). The European Statistical System’s guidelines and its JDemetra+ software support both (Eurostat Handbook). Known problems

  • End-point revisions. Filters are symmetric in the middle of a series and must rely on forecasts at the end, so the latest seasonal factors are the least certain and are revised as data arrive (ONS).
  • Residual seasonality. A seasonal pattern left in the adjusted series, often visible as a quarter that is repeatedly higher or lower; it can be tested for.
  • Large one-off movements. When a single period moves far outside the normal range, as happened in many series in 2020, the method can misread it as a change in the seasonal pattern. Statistics offices treat such periods as outliers or fix seasonal factors temporarily, and document the choice.
  • Aggregation. Adjusting components separately and adding them (indirect adjustment) gives a slightly different total from adjusting the total directly.

Conversions

annualised from quarterly:  a = (1 + q)^4 − 1
annualised from monthly:    a = (1 + m)^12 − 1
quarterly from annualised:  q = (1 + a)^(1/4) − 1
y/y from four quarters:     y = (1 + q1)(1 + q2)(1 + q3)(1 + q4) − 1
3m/3m:                      (average of last 3 months ÷ average of previous 3) − 1

Examples: 2.0% SAAR ⇔ 0.496% q/q; 3.0% SAAR ⇔ 0.742% q/q. Multiplying by four (0.5% × 4 = 2.0%) is the simple approximation and slightly understates the compounded figure.

Reading guidance

The annualised rate of one quarter carries the full noise of that quarter, scaled up roughly fourfold. Year-on-year growth equals the latest four quarterly rates compounded together, so it lags turning points by construction. Analysts often read both: q/q or 3m/3m for direction, y/y for level.
