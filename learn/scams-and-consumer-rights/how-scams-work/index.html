How Scams Work: The Anatomy of Fraud

Every scam combines a hook, a story, urgency, isolation and a payment that is hard to reverse.

Everyday·6 min (Simple) · 10 min (Detailed)·Updated 6 November 2026

Read first: Digital Banking and Keeping Accounts Secure

Key points

  • Most scams follow the same five steps: a hook, a story, urgency, isolation and a payment that is hard to reverse.
  • The hook works on ordinary feelings: hope of gain, worry, affection or a sense of duty.
  • Scammers often work in organised groups, with scripts and personal data taken from leaks.
  • Anyone can be targeted. Victims come from every age group and every level of education.
  • A fixed habit (pause, check through a channel you already trust, then decide) protects people more than being clever does.

Simple: plain words. Detailed: exact figures, codes and methods.

In short: a scam is a story built to move your money before you have time to check it. The story changes; the structure underneath it stays the same.

Simple version

What a scam is

A scamScamA scheme that deceives people into handing over money or personal information.Read more about Scam is a lie told to get your money or your personal details. When the lie is used to take money, the law in most countries calls it fraudFraudDeceiving someone to obtain money, goods or data.Read more about Fraud. Scams arrive by phone, text, email, social media, dating apps, websites and sometimes at the door.

The people behind them are often organised. Many work in teams, follow written scripts and use personal data stolen in earlier leaks. That is why a scam message can know your name, your bank or your recent online order.

The five parts of almost every scam

Once you know the parts, you can spot them in a new story.

  1. The hook. Something that grabs a feeling. It can be hope (“you have won”), fear (“your account is under attack”), affection (“I think I am falling for you”) or duty (“your grandson needs help”).
  2. The story. A believable reason for what happens next. It often uses a real company’s name, a real logo or a real news event.
  3. Urgency. A deadline that leaves no time to think: “within the hour”, “before the police arrive”, “only three places left”.
  4. Isolation. A request to tell no one. Your bank, your family and your friends are the people most likely to spot the lie.
  5. The payment. Money moved in a way that is hard to undo: a bank transfer you approve yourself, gift cards, crypto, cash handed to a courier.

Two examples, taken apart

Example

A call “from your bank”. A caller says your account has been hacked (hook). They know your name and the last digits of your card (story, using leaked data). They say thieves will empty the account tonight (urgency). They ask you not to tell branch staff, because “staff may be involved” (isolation). They ask you to transfer your savings of ¤6,000 (example) to a “safe account” (payment). Real banks do not ask customers to move money to protect it.

Example

An investment chat group. A friendly stranger adds you to a group where members post screenshots of profits (hook and story). The platform shows 2% growth a week (example). Repeated for a year, that would be about 180% growth, which no ordinary investment offers. A “bonus” ends on Friday (urgency). You are told outsiders “would not understand” (isolation). Deposits go in as crypto (payment). Withdrawals then need new “fees”, and the money does not come back.

Why clever people get caught

Scams do not target a lack of intelligence. They target timing. A message that lands when you are tired, worried, lonely or busy works on anyone. Fraud agencies stress that victims come from every age and every background (Scamwatch).

A habit that protects people

Many fraud agencies teach the same three-step habit. Australia’s Scamwatch calls it “Stop. Check. Protect.” (Scamwatch).

  • Stop. A pause breaks the urgency, which is the part the scam depends on.
  • Check. Checking means ending the contact and reaching the organisation through a number or website you already know, such as the number on the back of your card. A number given in the message itself may lead back to the scammer.
  • Protect. If money or details have already gone, the bank and the reporting body can act faster the sooner they hear.

Detailed version

Fraud typologies

Regulators and police group scams by the story they use. Common categories in national reporting include (Scamwatch; FCA):

Type Core story Usual payment
Impersonation Bank, police, tax office or a delivery company Transfer to a “safe account”, card details, remote access
Investment Platform or adviser with high, steady returns Transfers, crypto
Romance Online relationship, then a sudden need for money Transfers, gift cards, crypto
Purchase Goods or rentals that do not exist Transfer outside the platform’s payment system
Invoice or payment redirection A supplier’s “new bank details” Business transfer
Advance fee A prize, loan or inheritance released after a fee Transfers, vouchers
Employment Paid “tasks” or a job that needs an upfront payment Crypto, transfers

A useful distinction is authorised versus unauthorised fraud. In unauthorised fraud a criminal moves money without the account holder’s consent (a stolen card, an account takeover). In authorised fraud the victim makes the payment, having been deceived. The UK’s Payment Systems Regulator uses the term “authorised push payment (APP) scams” for the second group (PSR). The difference matters because many legal protections were written for unauthorised payments.

Social engineering

The techniques map closely onto persuasion principles described in social psychology: authority (a uniform, a logo, a title), scarcity (limited places, a deadline), social proof (fake reviews, group chats full of “winners”), reciprocity (a small favour or gift first) and commitment (small early steps that make later, larger steps feel consistent). Scripts also manage the victim’s emotions over time: an investment scam may pay out a small “profit” early to build trust before larger deposits.

Personal data from breaches makes the story credible. Knowing a name, address, bank and partial card number is enough to sound like the bank itself (NCSC).

How reversible is each payment?

Reversibility explains why scammers steer victims towards certain methods.

Method Can it be reversed?
Card payment Often disputable through the card scheme’s chargeback process, within time limits set by the scheme
Authorised bank transfer Final in principle; recovery depends on the receiving bank freezing funds quickly and on national reimbursement rules
Gift cards and vouchers Rarely recoverable once the code is shared
Crypto assets Transfers are final on the network; recovery depends on an exchange freezing an account
Cash given to a courier Practically unrecoverable

Varies by country

Reimbursement for authorised scams differs widely. In the United Kingdom, a mandatory reimbursement requirement for most authorised push payment scams over the main domestic payment systems began in October 2024 (PSR). In the United States, federal rules on electronic transfers focus on payments the account holder did not authorise, so a deceived customer who approved a transfer is often outside them. Australia and Canada run national reporting centres that collect reports and publish warnings (Scamwatch; Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre).

Reading fraud statistics

National bodies such as the US Federal Trade Commission (ReportFraud) and Australia’s Scamwatch publish yearly figures. Three cautions apply when reading them. Fraud is under-reported, partly because of embarrassment, so totals are lower bounds. Category definitions differ, so cross-country comparisons are rough. A rise in reports can reflect a new reporting channel rather than more fraud.
