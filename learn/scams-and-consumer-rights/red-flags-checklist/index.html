The Red Flags Checklist

A short list of warning signs that catches most scam patterns before any money moves.

Everyday·4 min (Simple) · 7 min (Detailed)·Updated 6 November 2026

Read first: How Scams Work: The Anatomy of Fraud

Key points

  • Eight warning signs cover the common scam patterns: guaranteed returns, pressure to act now, unexpected contact, secrecy, unusual payment methods, a 'safe account', changed bank details and requests for codes or passwords.
  • One red flag is a reason to pause and check through a channel you already trust.
  • Two or more red flags together match the standard pattern of a scam.
  • Legitimate organisations sometimes create urgency too, which is why the check matters more than the feeling.
  • Businesses use the same idea as written controls: call-back checks and a second person approving payments.

Simple: plain words. Detailed: exact figures, codes and methods.

In short: most scams show at least one of eight warning signs. Spotting one is a reason to pause; spotting two is the typical shape of fraud.

Simple version

The checklist

The eight signs below appear again and again in the warnings that fraud agencies publish (Scamwatch; FCA).

# Red flag Why it matters
1 Guaranteed or very high returns Real investments can lose value. A promise of no loss is not how investing works.
2 Pressure to act now A deadline removes the time you would use to check.
3 Contact you did not expect A call, text or message that starts the conversation is the usual first step of a scam.
4 A request for secrecy Your bank, family and friends are the people most able to spot a lie.
5 Payment by gift card, crypto, wire transfer or cash courier These methods are hard or impossible to reverse. Public bodies do not ask for payment this way.
6 “Move your money to a safe account” Banks, police and tax offices do not ask anyone to move money to protect it.
7 New or changed bank details on an invoice Criminals copy real invoices and change only the account number.
8 A request for a code, password or PIN No legitimate organisation needs your one-time code. Sharing it hands over the account.

How to read it

  • One flag is a reason to pause. The check means ending the contact and reaching the organisation through a number or website you already had.
  • Two or more flags together match the standard pattern of a scam from the previous lesson.
  • No flags does not prove an offer is safe. New scam stories appear all the time.

Three practice scenarios

Example

1. The parcel text. “Your parcel is held. Pay ¤1.99 (example) within 24 hours or it will be returned.” It is unexpected (flag 3) and urgent (flag 2). The link asks for card details. Two flags. The delivery company’s own app or website, opened separately, shows whether a parcel exists.

Example

2. The supplier email. A small business receives an invoice for ¤4,800 (example) from a regular supplier. The email says the supplier “has changed banks” (flag 7) and asks for payment today (flag 2). Two flags. A phone call to the supplier, on the number already in the business’s records, confirms or rules out the change.

Example

3. The friendly investor. Someone met on a social app shares a trading site where “nobody loses” (flag 1). They ask you to keep it between the two of you (flag 4) and to deposit in crypto (flag 5). Three flags.

Why a fixed list helps

Under pressure, people decide with feelings first. A short written list, checked the same way every time, does not depend on how you feel in that moment. That is why many people print a card like the table above and keep it near the phone.

Detailed version

Mapping flags to fraud types

Each flag is a footprint of one of the five stages (hook, story, urgency, isolation, payment) described in the previous lesson. The mapping helps when a story is new.

Flag Stage Typologies where it is common
Guaranteed returns Hook Investment, crypto, Ponzi-type schemes
Pressure to act now Urgency Nearly all
Unexpected contact Hook, story Impersonation, investment, romance
Secrecy Isolation Romance, impersonation (especially “police” and “bank” stories)
Irreversible payment method Payment Advance fee, romance, impersonation of public bodies
“Safe account” Story, payment Bank and police impersonation
Changed payment details Story, payment Invoice and payment-redirection fraud against firms and home buyers
Codes or passwords Payment (account takeover) Phishing, remote-access and SIM-swap fraud

Sources for the typologies: national reporting centres such as Scamwatch, the Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre and the US Internet Crime Complaint Center (IC3).

False positives: legitimate urgency exists

A checklist that flags everything stops being used. Real organisations do sometimes call without warning (a bank’s fraud team checking a card payment) and real deadlines exist (a tax return, a court date). The distinguishing feature is not urgency itself but which channel the check runs through. A genuine call survives being ended and returned on a number from an independent source, such as a card, a statement or an official website. A scam does not, because the scammer controls the channel they gave you.

This is why fraud guidance focuses on verification through an independent channel rather than on judging whether a message “feels” real (NCSC).

Organisational controls

Firms apply the same logic as written procedures, because invoice and payment-redirection fraud targets accounts departments:

  • Call-back verification. Any change of a supplier’s bank details is confirmed by calling a contact number already on file, not one in the request.
  • Dual authorisation. Payments above a threshold need approval from a second person, so one deceived employee cannot release funds alone.
  • Segregation of duties. The person who sets up a new payee is not the person who approves payments to it.
  • Payee name checks. Some payment systems compare the name on the receiving account with the name the payer typed. The United Kingdom and the euro area both run services of this kind; coverage and rules vary by country.

Varies by country

Warning lists differ in emphasis because the common scams differ. Australia’s Scamwatch organises its guidance around the “Stop. Check. Protect.” habit (Scamwatch). The UK’s Financial Conduct Authority focuses on investment and pension offers and lets the public check whether a firm is authorised (FCA). Canada’s Anti-Fraud Centre publishes alerts on current scam stories (CAFC). The underlying flags are the same across all three.
