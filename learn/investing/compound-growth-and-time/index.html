Compound Growth and Time

Why starting early can matter more than starting big, and what the averages leave out.

Intermediate·5 min (Simple) · 9 min (Detailed)·Updated 6 November 2026

Read first: Interest: The Price of Time

Key points

  • Returns earn returns: growth is added to the amount, and the next period's growth is calculated on the larger amount.
  • At an assumed 7% a year, ¤2,000 grows to about ¤29,949 in 40 years, and half of that growth comes in the last ten years.
  • In an example at 7%, someone who invests for ten years from age 25 and stops ends with more than someone who invests for 30 years from age 35.
  • Time is one of the inputs a person can often influence; the return is not.
  • Real returns are not smooth; averages hide uneven paths.

Simple: plain words. Detailed: exact figures, codes and methods.

In short: with compoundCompound interestInterest earned on both the original amount and the interest already added.Read more about Compound interest growth, each year’s gain is added to the pot and earns its own gain the next year. Over long periods this makes time a powerful input, but the rates used in examples are assumptions, not promises.

Simple version

Returns earn returns

Suppose an investment grows by 7% a year (an assumption for this example). After one year, ¤2,000 becomes ¤2,140. The next year, the 7% is calculated on ¤2,140, not on ¤2,000. That is compounding.

Years Value of ¤2,000 at 7% a year (example)
0 ¤2,000
10 ¤3,934
20 ¤7,739
30 ¤15,225
40 ¤29,949

Look at the last row. Between year 30 and year 40, the value grows by about ¤14,700. That is more than the whole growth of the first 30 years. Compounding is slow at first and fast later.

The Rule of 72Rule of 72A rough guide: dividing 72 by the yearly rate gives an estimate of how many years money takes to double.Read more about Rule of 72 gives a quick estimate: divide 72 by the yearly rate to find roughly how many years it takes to double. At 7%, 72 ÷ 7 ≈ 10 years.

Two people, two starting ages

Example

Both invest ¤2,000 at the start of each year and earn 7% a year (an assumption). Both stop at age 65.

  • Person A invests from age 25 to 34 (ten payments, ¤20,000 in total), then stops adding money. At 65, the pot is about ¤225,073.
  • Person B starts at 35 and invests every year until 64 (30 payments, ¤60,000 in total). At 65, the pot is about ¤202,146.

Person A paid in a third as much but ended with more, because A’s money had ten extra years to compound.

There is an honest caveat. This result depends on the rate. At 5% a year, the same plan gives Person A about ¤114,158 and Person B about ¤139,522, so B ends with more. Compounding rewards time, and the size of the reward depends on a return nobody knows in advance.

What if I can only put in a little?

Small regular amounts also compound. ¤50 a month at an assumed 7% a year for 30 years adds up to about ¤61,000, from ¤18,000 paid in. The amount you put in, the time and the return all matter. Of those three, the return is the one you control least.

Real life is bumpy

The table uses the same 7% every year. Real investments do not move like that. Some years they rise 20%, some years they fall 15%. The average over many years can be close to the figure in a table, but the path is uneven, and the end result depends on the order of the good and bad years when money goes in or out.

Detailed version

The formulas

Lump sum: FV = PV × (1 + r)^n. Regular contributions C at the end of each period: FV = C × ((1 + r)^n − 1) / r; at the start of each period, multiply by (1 + r). The SEC’s calculator implements the same relationships (Investor.gov).

The Rule of 72 approximates the exact doubling time ln 2 / ln(1 + r). At 7%, the rule gives 10.29 years and the exact figure is 10.24 years; the approximation is closest for rates around 6–10% (Investor.gov).

Arithmetic and geometric means

The arithmetic mean averages yearly returns; the geometric mean is the constant rate that gives the same end value. They differ whenever returns vary:

+50% then −50%:  arithmetic mean = 0%
                  ¤100 → ¤150 → ¤75
                  geometric mean = √(1.5 × 0.5) − 1 = −13.4%

Compounding follows the geometric mean. A projection that applies an arithmetic average as if it were a constant rate overstates the median outcome.

Volatility drag

A common approximation links the two: g ≈ a − σ² / 2, where σ is the standard deviation of yearly returns. With an arithmetic mean of 8% and a volatilityVolatilityHow much and how fast a price moves up and down.Read more about Volatility of 20%, g ≈ 8% − 2% = 6%. Higher volatility at the same arithmetic mean lowers long-run compound growth.

Real versus nominal growth

Projections in nominal terms mix growth with inflation. Real growth is (1 + nominal) / (1 + inflation) − 1. A 30-year projection at 7% nominal with 2.5% inflation grows at about 4.4% in real terms, and the real end value is a little under half of the nominal figure (1.025^−30 ≈ 0.48).

Contribution timing

With regular contributions, the order of returns matters. On a path where returns are low early and high late, contributions bought cheaply early benefit most; on the reverse path, they benefit least. The dca-vs-lump-sum tool shows how regular investing and a single lump sum end on a path the user enters. On a path that rises more often than it falls, the lump sum ends higher; on a path that falls first, regular investing ends higher.

Common misconceptions

  • “A 7% average means 7% every year.” It means a long-run figure around which actual years vary widely.
  • “Starting late cannot be made up.” It can be offset partly with larger contributions; the arithmetic shows how much larger.

Varies by country

Taxes affect compounding, and the rules differ. Many countries offer accounts where growth is not taxed each year, such as ISAs in the United Kingdom, IRAs and 401(k) plans in the United States, and TFSAs in Canada. Outside such accounts, yearly taxes on interest, dividends or gains reduce the amount left to compound. The Taxes module explains the general pattern without country rates.
