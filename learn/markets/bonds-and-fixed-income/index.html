Bonds and Fixed Income

Lending to governments and companies: how bonds pay, why prices move opposite to yields, and what can go wrong.

Intermediate·6 min (Simple) · 11 min (Detailed)·Updated 6 November 2026

Read first: Interest: The Price of Time

Key points

  • A bond is a loan: the issuer pays interest (the coupon) and repays the amount borrowed on a set date (the maturity).
  • When market interest rates rise, the prices of existing bonds fall, and the other way round.
  • The longer a bond has left to run, the more its price moves when rates change; this sensitivity is called duration.
  • Credit ratings estimate the chance that the issuer does not pay; a higher yield is the compensation for that risk.
  • Inflation-linked bonds adjust their payments with a price index.
  • A bond fund has no maturity date, so it does not return a fixed amount on a fixed day the way a single bond does.

Simple: plain words. Detailed: exact figures, codes and methods.

In short: a bond is a loan you can buy and sell, and its price moves in the opposite direction to interest rates.

Simple version

A bond is a loan

When a government or a company needs to borrow, it can sell bondsBondA loan to a government or company that pays interest and returns the original amount on a set date.Read more about Bond. Each bond is a promise:

  • to pay a fixed amount of interest each year, called the couponCouponThe fixed interest payment a bond makes, usually stated as a percentage of its face value.Read more about Coupon, and
  • to repay the original amount (the face value) on a set date, called the maturityMaturityThe date on which a loan or bond has to be repaid.Read more about Maturity.

Example

A bond has a face value of ¤1,000, a 5% coupon and 10 years to maturity (example). The holder receives ¤50 a year for ten years and ¤1,000 at the end.

The see-saw

Bonds can be sold before they mature, so they have a price that changes. Picture a see-saw. On one side is the bond’s price; on the other is the interest rate available on new bonds.

Suppose rates on new bonds rise to 6%. Nobody pays the full ¤1,000 for an old bond paying ¤50 when a new one pays ¤60. So the old bond’s price falls until its total return matches the new rate. In this example it falls to about ¤926.

If rates fall to 4%, the old ¤50 coupon becomes attractive, and the price rises to about ¤1,081.

If you hold the bond to maturity and the issuer pays in full, these price changes do not change what you receive. They matter if you sell early.

Longer bonds move more

A bond with two years left is close to repaying its ¤1,000. A rise in rates from 5% to 6% lowers its price only a little, to about ¤982. A bond with ten years left falls to about ¤926. More years at the old rate means a bigger difference, so a bigger price change.

Government and company bonds

  • Government bonds are loans to a national government. Governments that borrow in their own currency have rarely failed to pay, though some have. Their yields are often used as a reference for other rates.
  • Corporate bonds are loans to companies. A company can fail. To make up for that chance, corporate bonds generally pay a higher yield than government bonds of the same length.

Rating agencies give bonds grades such as AAA or BB. A rating is an opinion about the chance of default, not a guarantee.

Why a bond fund can lose money

A bond fund holds many bonds and keeps replacing those that mature. It has no single maturity date. When rates rise, the fund’s price falls, and there is no fixed day when it returns to a set amount. Over time, the fund buys new bonds at the higher rates, which raises its income.

Bonds that follow inflation

Some governments issue inflation-linked bondsInflation-linked bondA bond whose payments rise with a price index.Read more about Inflation-linked bond. The amount repaid, and so each payment, rises with a price index. They protect the buying power of the money lent rather than the number of ¤.

Varies by country

Government bonds have local names and rules. The United Kingdom calls them gilts (UK DMO); the United States issues Treasury notes and bonds, and inflation-protected securities called TIPS (TreasuryDirect). Euro-area governments each issue their own bonds in euros. Whether private savers can buy them directly, and how the interest is taxed, varies by country.

Detailed version

Price and yield

A fixed-coupon bond’s price is the present value of its cash flows, discounted at the market yield y:

P = Σ (C / (1 + y)^t) for t = 1..n  +  F / (1 + y)^n

with coupon C, face value F and n years. Yield to maturity (YTM) is the single y that makes this equal to the market price. It assumes all payments are made and coupons are reinvested at y.

Worked example (annual coupons, F = ¤1,000, C = ¤50):

Years left Price at 4% Price at 5% Price at 6%
2 ¤1,018.86 ¤1,000.00 ¤981.67
10 ¤1,081.11 ¤1,000.00 ¤926.40

The inverse relation is mechanical: the cash flows are fixed, so a higher discount rate means a lower present value (SEC).

Duration and convexity

Macaulay duration is the present-value-weighted average time to the cash flows. Modified duration = Macaulay duration / (1 + y) and gives the approximate percentage price change for a 1-point change in yield:

ΔP / P ≈ −D_mod × Δy

For the 10-year bond above at 5%, Macaulay duration ≈ 8.11 years and modified duration ≈ 7.72. A 1-point rise predicts about −7.7%; the exact change is −7.36% (to ¤926.40). The gap is convexity: the price-yield curve bends, so the linear estimate overstates losses and understates gains for large moves. For the 2-year bond, modified duration ≈ 1.86 and the exact change is −1.83%.

Professionals often express rate changes in basis pointsBasis pointOne hundredth of a percentage point, so 25 basis points equal 0.25 percentage points.Read more about Basis point (one hundredth of a percentage point) and quote risk as DV01: the money change in price for a 1 basis point move.

Credit spreads

A corporate bond’s yield is commonly read as a government yield of the same maturity plus a credit spread. The spread compensates for expected default losses, uncertainty about them, and lower liquidity. Spreads widen when investors demand more compensation, which lowers corporate bond prices even when government yields are unchanged.

Sovereign bonds carry credit risk too, especially when a government borrows in a currency it does not issue. Ratings are opinions; agencies publish their methods and default histories.

Inflation-linked bonds

In common designs the principal is indexed to a consumer price index and the fixed coupon rate applies to the indexed principal (TreasuryDirect). Their yield is a real yield. The gap between a nominal and an inflation-linked yield of the same maturity (the breakeven inflation rate) is often read as a market-based measure of inflation compensation, which also contains risk and liquidity premiums.

The yield curve

Plotting yields against maturity for one issuer gives the yield curveYield curveA chart of bond yields across maturities, read as a signal of expectations for interest rates and growth.Read more about Yield curve. Central banks publish estimated curves; the ECB, for example, publishes daily euro-area curves (ECB). Shapes and their interpretation are covered in the professional lesson on the yield curve.

Common misconceptions

  • “Bonds are safe, full stop.” Default risk, inflation and price changes before maturity all apply.
  • “A fund’s yield is what I will earn.” A fund’s quoted yield is a snapshot; its price changes with rates and it has no maturity.
  • “Higher coupon means higher return.” Return depends on the price paid; a high-coupon bond bought above face value can yield less than a low-coupon one.
