Funds, ETFs and Index Investing

Pooled investments: how they work, what they cost, and the difference between tracking an index and picking investments.

Intermediate·6 min (Simple) · 11 min (Detailed)·Updated 6 November 2026

Read first: Diversification, Fees and Costs

Key points

  • A fund pools money from many people to buy many assets; each investor owns units of the pool.
  • Exchange-traded funds (ETFs) trade on an exchange during the day like shares; traditional funds are bought and sold at one price per day.
  • Index funds aim to copy an index; active funds try to beat one and charge more for trying.
  • How active funds perform after fees, compared with index funds, has been studied extensively, and researchers read the evidence differently.
  • A fund's fact sheet shows what it holds, what it costs, where it is based and how it is set up.

Simple: plain words. Detailed: exact figures, codes and methods.

In short: a fund lets many people share one large basket of investments, and the main differences between funds are what is in the basket, how it is chosen and what it costs.

Simple version

The pool

Imagine 1,000 people each put ¤100 into one pot. The pot now holds ¤100,000. A manager uses it to buy shares in 200 companies. Each person owns one thousandth of the pot. That is a fundFundA pool of money from many investors, managed together and invested in many assets.Read more about Fund.

The pieces of the pot are called units or shares of the fund. If the investments in the pot rise in value by 10%, each unit rises by about 10%, minus costs.

A fund gives each person a slice of many investments at once. This spreads risk, an idea called diversificationDiversificationSpreading money across many investments so that no single failure causes a large loss.Read more about Diversification.

Two ways to buy a fund

  • Traditional funds (called mutual fundsMutual fund (unit trust)A fund priced once a day that investors buy into and sell back to the fund manager directly, rather than on an exchange.Read more about Mutual fund (unit trust), unit trusts or similar names) are bought from and sold back to the fund company. There is one price per day, worked out after markets close.
  • Exchange-traded funds (ETFsExchange-traded fund (ETF)A fund that holds many assets and trades on an exchange like a single share.Read more about Exchange-traded fund (ETF)) are traded on a stock exchange during the day, like a share. The price moves through the day and there is a small gap between buying and selling prices.

Why costs matter

Fees are taken every year, whether the fund rises or falls. Small differences add up over long periods.

Example

¤10,000 is invested for 30 years. The investments grow 5% a year before fees (example). With a 0.2% yearly fee the pot grows to about ¤40,800. With a 1.5% yearly fee it grows to about ¤28,100. The difference, about ¤12,700, is the effect of the higher fee.

Reading a fact sheet

Funds publish a short fact sheet or key information document. Five things it answers:

  1. What does it hold? Shares, bonds or both; which countries; how many holdings; the largest ones.
  2. What does it cost? The yearly charge, plus any entry, exit or performance fees.
  3. What is it trying to do? Track an index (which one?) or beat one.
  4. Where is it based? A fund’s home country (its domicile) affects investor protection and tax.
  5. How has it moved? Past returns, and a risk indicator. Past returns describe the past only.

Varies by country

Fund rules are national or regional. In the European Union, many funds sold to the public follow the UCITS rules, and investors receive a standard key information document (EUR-Lex, PRIIPs). In the United States, most funds sold to the public are registered under the Investment Company Act of 1940. How fund gains and income are taxed differs from country to country, and a fund based abroad can be taxed differently from a local one.

Detailed version

Structures and regulation

Funds are legal structures: companies, trusts or contractual arrangements, with a depositary or custodian holding the assets separately from the manager. EU UCITS rules set diversification limits, liquidity requirements and a depositary regime for funds sold to the public across member states (EUR-Lex, 2009/65/EC). The US equivalent for most retail funds is the Investment Company Act of 1940. Other regions have their own regimes, and many recognise each other’s structures.

Tracking difference and tracking error

  • Tracking difference = fund return − index return over a period. It is mostly the fund’s costs, offset partly by income from activities such as securities lending.
  • Tracking error = the standard deviation of the periodic return differences. A fund can have a small tracking error but a persistent negative tracking difference.

How index funds replicate

  • Full physical replication: hold every index constituent at its weight.
  • Sampling: hold a representative subset; cheaper for indices with thousands of bonds or small companies, at the cost of higher tracking error.
  • Synthetic replication: hold a basket of other assets and enter a swap with a bank that pays the index return. This introduces counterparty exposure to the swap provider, limited by collateral rules (FSB, 2011).

Securities lending (lending holdings to other market participants for a fee, against collateral) can lower net costs and adds a small counterparty and collateral exposure.

ETF liquidity and closure

An ETF’s price stays close to the value of its holdings because authorised participants can create or redeem units in exchange for the underlying assets. When the underlying assets are hard to trade, the ETF can trade at a discount or premium to its net asset value (BIS, 2011). Funds can also close; investors then receive the value of the holdings, which crystallises any gain or loss at that date.

The active versus passive evidence

Arithmetic gives a starting point: before costs, the average invested currency unit earns the market return, so after costs the average actively managed unit trails the market by its extra costs. The debate is about whether some managers reliably beat the market and whether investors can identify them in advance.

Why people disagree

Fama and French found that, after costs, few US equity funds produced returns that could be distinguished from luck in their sample (Fama and French, 2010). Berk and Green argue that skilled managers exist, but money flows into funds that have done well until their extra returns are competed away, so lack of persistence does not prove lack of skill (Berk and Green, 2004). Others point to market segments, such as smaller companies or less-traded bonds, where they see more room for active management. Results depend on the period, market, benchmark and whether closed funds are included.

Common misconceptions

  • “An index fund has no risk.” It carries the full risk of its index.
  • “ETFs are a type of investment.” ETF describes how the fund is traded, not what it holds.
  • “A cheap fund is the same as a good fund.” Cost is one factor; holdings, tracking, structure and tax treatment also differ.
