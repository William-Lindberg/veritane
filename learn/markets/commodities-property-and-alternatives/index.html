Commodities, Property and Alternative Assets

Gold, oil, property funds, private assets and collectibles: how they behave and what they cost to hold.

Intermediate·5 min (Simple) · 10 min (Detailed)·Updated 6 November 2026

Read first: Diversification

Key points

  • Commodities pay no income; returns come from price changes, minus the costs of holding them.
  • Gold is held by many as a store of value, and its price moves a lot too.
  • Listed property funds (REITs and similar) give exposure to property, with price swings similar to shares.
  • Private assets are hard to sell quickly and hard to value.
  • Collectibles have high buying and selling costs and pay nothing while held.
  • How much these assets help diversify a portfolio has differed from one period to another.

Simple: plain words. Detailed: exact figures, codes and methods.

In short: before looking at returns, a useful first question for any asset is “what does it pay me while I hold it?”, because the answer explains much of how it behaves.

Simple version

The key question

Shares can pay dividends. Bonds pay interest. A rented flat pays rent. Some assets pay nothing at all while you hold them. For those, the whole return comes from selling for more than you paid, and holding them often costs money.

Asset Pays while held? Main costs of holding
Gold bars or coins No Storage, insurance, dealer margins
Oil, wheat, metals (through funds) No Rolling futures contracts (see below), fund fees
Listed property funds Yes, rent passed on as dividends Fund fees
Private equity, private credit Some, irregular High fees, long lock-ups
Art, wine, watches, cards No (wine can even spoil) Auction fees, storage, insurance

Commodities

A commodityCommodityA raw material traded in bulk, such as oil, wheat or gold.Read more about Commodity is a raw material traded in standard units, such as a barrel of oil or a tonne of copper. Most investors do not store barrels. Funds hold contracts to buy the commodity in the future, and replace them before they expire. That replacement can cost money, so a commodity fund can fall even when the commodity’s price stays the same.

Gold

Gold has been used as money and as a store of value for thousands of years. It pays no interest. Its price can move by large amounts over months and years. People hold it for different reasons: as a hedge against inflation or currency problems, or as an asset that might behave differently from shares. How well it has done any of these depends on the period studied.

Property funds

A REITREIT (real-estate investment trust)A listed company or fund that owns property and pays out most of its rental income to investors.Read more about REIT (real-estate investment trust) (real-estate investment trust) or similar fund owns buildings and passes most of the rent to investors. It is listed on an exchange, so it can be sold any day. The price of the fund moves with the stock market as well as with property values, so it can swing much more than the valuation of a house.

Private assets

Private equity (stakes in companies not listed on an exchange), private credit and infrastructure funds are not traded daily. Your money is often locked in for years. Values are estimated by the manager, perhaps a few times a year. A calm-looking value does not mean the asset is calm; it means it is valued rarely.

Collectibles

Art, wine, watches and similar items pay nothing and cost a lot to buy and sell.

Example

A painting is bought at auction for ¤10,000 plus a 15% buyer’s fee: ¤11,500 in total (example). When it is sold, the seller pays a 10% fee. To get ¤11,500 back, it must sell for ¤11,500 ÷ 0.90 ≈ ¤12,778. The price has to rise about 28% just to break even, before storage and insurance.

Varies by country

Property fund rules are national. Several countries, including the United States, Japan, Singapore and the United Kingdom, have REIT regimes that give tax treatment in exchange for paying out most income, with different conditions in each. Whether private assets can be sold to ordinary savers, and how gold and collectibles are taxed, also differs between countries.

Detailed version

Futures roll, contango and backwardation

Commodity exposure is usually obtained through futures. Holding exposure means selling the expiring contract and buying a later one: the roll. When later contracts are priced above nearer ones, the curve is in contango; when below, backwardation (CFTC Glossary).

Worked example: the front contract is ¤80 and the next is ¤82. If the spot price stays at ¤80, the ¤82 contract converges towards ¤80 as it nears expiry, a loss of 2 / 82 ≈ 2.44% per roll period, before collateral interest. A fully collateralised futures position’s return can be split as:

total return ≈ spot price change + roll yield + interest on collateral

Gorton and Rouwenhorst constructed a long-run index of commodity futures and reported returns and correlations with shares, bonds and inflation over their sample (Gorton and Rouwenhorst, 2006). Later research using different periods has reached different results, so period dependence is a central caveat.

REIT structures

REIT-type regimes typically require most assets to be property, most income to come from rent, and most taxable income to be distributed; in exchange, tax is largely paid by investors rather than at fund level. Listed vehicles add leverage and stock-market pricing on top of the property itself, which is why their prices move more than appraisal-based property indices.

Valuation smoothing in private assets

Infrequent, appraisal-based valuations make reported returns smoother than underlying economic values. Smoothed returns show positive serial correlation and understate volatility and correlation with listed markets. Getmansky, Lo and Makarov model this effect and show how it can be adjusted for (Getmansky, Lo and Makarov, 2003). An unsmoothing step (for example, assuming reported return = weighted average of current and past true returns) is a common professional adjustment.

Correlation and diversification

Diversification depends on correlationCorrelationHow closely two things move together; assets with low correlation can diversify each other.Read more about Correlation, which is not constant. Correlations between asset classes measured in calm periods can differ from those measured when prices fall broadly. Any claim that an alternative asset “diversifies” is a statement about a sample period.

Why people disagree

Some investors and researchers treat commodities and gold as a distinct source of return and inflation protection. Others argue that, without income, long-run returns rest on price changes and roll yields that vary by period, and that the diversification benefit seen in one sample has not held in others. Both sides cite data; the disagreement is largely about which periods and measures are representative.

Common misconceptions

  • “A commodity fund tracks the commodity price.” It tracks futures returns, including roll.
  • “Private assets are less volatile.” Reported volatility is lower partly because values are smoothed.
  • “Collectibles keep up with inflation.” Individual items vary widely, and transaction costs are high.
