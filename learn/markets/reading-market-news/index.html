Reading Market News Without Being Misled

What daily market reports actually say, and the common ways they can mislead.

Intermediate·5 min (Simple) · 8 min (Detailed)·Updated 6 November 2026

Read first: How Stock Markets Work

Key points

  • A one-day move in an index is small noise for anyone thinking in years.
  • In a market that grows over time, "all-time highs" happen often and are not a signal on their own.
  • Explanations for a move are usually written after it happened.
  • A change in points means nothing without the starting level; percentages can be compared.
  • "Markets expect" means prices imply something, not that anyone knows.
  • Checking the source and the time window answers most questions about a headline.

Simple: plain words. Detailed: exact figures, codes and methods.

In short: most daily market reports describe a short price move and attach a story to it; a few questions separate the facts from the story.

Simple version

A sample headline, taken apart

“Shares fall 400 points as investors worry about rates” (example)

This sentence contains three different things:

  1. A fact: an index fell 400 points.
  2. A missing fact: the starting level. If the index was at 40,000, the fall is 1%. If it was at 4,000, the fall is 10%. These are very different days.
  3. A story: “investors worry about rates”. Nobody surveyed the buyers and sellers. Every share sold was also bought by someone. The reason is the writer’s interpretation, added after the move.

Points versus percent

Index points are like centimetres on a map with no scale. Percentages can be compared across indices and across time. A rewritten version of the headline: “An index fell 1% today” (example).

All-time highs are common

If a price grows over time, each new peak is an “all-time high”. In a growing market this can happen many times in a year. The phrase says the level is the highest so far. It says nothing on its own about what comes next.

Stories come after the move

A report written at the end of the day looks for a reason. On a day with mixed news, the same move can be explained by whichever item fits. Two reports can give opposite reasons for the same move. The price change is the observation; the reason is a guess.

“Markets expect”

When a report says “markets expect interest rates to rise”, it usually means that prices of some contracts are consistent with that path. It does not mean anyone knows. Those prices change as news arrives, sometimes within hours.

Five questions to ask

  1. What exactly moved, and by how much in percent?
  2. Over what time window? A day, a month, a year and ten years can tell different stories.
  3. Who is the source? A statistics office, a central bank, a company, or a commentator?
  4. Is this a fact or an interpretation? Look for words like “because”, “as” and “amid” that join a fact to a story.
  5. What is the comparison? “Highest since…” depends on which date is chosen as the start.

Words to read carefully

Market reports use colourful words for ordinary movements. Definitions vary between publications, so the number is more informative than the label.

Detailed version

Signal and noise

For an index with annual volatility σ, the typical daily move is roughly σ / √252 (about 252 trading days a year). At σ = 16% (example) that is about 1% a day, so a 1% move is an ordinary day. Annual returns accumulate small daily drifts that are invisible within the daily noise.

Market-implied expectations

Prices of interest-rate futures, overnight index swaps and government bonds imply a path for the policy rate. For a short-term interest-rate future quoted as 100 minus the rate, a price of 96.50 implies a rate of 3.50% over the contract period (example). These implied rates contain risk premiums, so they are not pure forecasts. Central banks distinguish them from surveys: the Federal Reserve Bank of New York, for instance, publishes a survey of market participants’ expectations alongside market prices (New York Fed). Inflation compensation derived from inflation-linked bonds has the same caveat. Volatility measures

Realised volatility is the standard deviation of past returns over a window. Implied volatility is backed out of option prices. Volatility indices summarise implied volatility for an index; the VIX, for example, is calculated from S&P 500 index option prices and reflects expected 30-day volatility (Cboe). A higher reading means option buyers are paying more for protection, not that a fall is forecast.

Selection and survivorship in reporting

  • Survivorship bias: performance tables that include only funds still operating omit those that closed, which tend to have lower returns. Elton, Gruber and Blake measured this effect in US mutual fund data (Elton, Gruber and Blake, 1996).
  • Selection of start dates: “up 40% since the low” and “down 10% since the peak” can describe the same series.
  • Selection of stories: forecasters who were right are interviewed; those who were wrong are not, so reported forecasting skill looks higher than it is.

Attribution after the fact

Short-term price changes are close to unpredictable from public information, which is common ground across the views recognised by the 2013 economics prize (Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences, 2013). Narrative attributions of a daily move are therefore hypotheses. Event studies, which compare prices just before and after a timed announcement, give stronger evidence about which news moved prices.

A checklist professionals use

Check Question
Units Points, percent, basis points, annualised or not?
Window Intraday, close-to-close, year to date?
Base Nominal or real; local currency or converted?
Source Primary (exchange, central bank, statistics office) or secondary?
Status Preliminary, revised, estimated?
