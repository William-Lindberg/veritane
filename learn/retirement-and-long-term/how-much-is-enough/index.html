How Much Is Enough?

The methods used to estimate a retirement target, and the assumptions each one rests on.

Intermediate·6 min (Simple) · 11 min (Detailed)·Updated 6 November 2026

Read first: Why Retirement Planning Exists

Key points

  • The replacement-rate method aims for a share of income before retirement.
  • The expense method starts from expected spending and subtracts guaranteed income such as a public pension.
  • Rules based on multiples of salary are rough shortcuts.
  • Every estimate rests on assumptions about returns, inflation and lifespan; showing them makes the number easier to judge.
  • Estimates move as life and assumptions change, so they are usually revisited every few years.

Simple: plain words. Detailed: exact figures, codes and methods.

In short: there is no single right number. There are a few methods, and each one gives an answer that depends on its assumptions.

Simple version

Two questions

Any retirement estimate tries to answer two questions:

  1. How much income will be needed each year?
  2. How much of that will come from savings, and how big do the savings need to be?

Method 1: a share of your current income

The replacement rateReplacement rateRetirement income as a share of income before retirement.Read more about Replacement rate is retirement income as a share of income before retirement. A target is picked as a percentage, such as 70 % (example). The idea is that some costs fall in retirement (commuting, work clothes, pension contributions, sometimes a mortgage).

Example

A worker earns ¤40,000 a year (example) and uses a 70 % target. That is ¤28,000 a year. A public pension of ¤12,000 a year would leave ¤16,000 a year to come from workplace pensions and savings.

This method is quick. Its weakness is that it says nothing about how a particular person actually spends.

Method 2: start from spending

The expense method builds a retirement budget: housing, food, energy, transport, health, leisure, gifts. Then it subtracts income that is guaranteed, such as a public pension or a defined benefit pension.

Example

A couple expect to spend ¤2,000 a month, or ¤24,000 a year (example). Their guaranteed pensions are ¤10,000 a year. The gap is ¤24,000 − ¤10,000 = ¤14,000 a year.

From a yearly gap to a total pot

How big a pot pays ¤14,000 a year? It depends on how long the money must last and how much it earns along the way. In today’s money:

Years of income Real return 0 % Real return 2 % Real return 4 %
25 years ¤350,000 about ¤273,300 about ¤218,700

A “real” return is growth after inflation. All three columns are examples. The same gap needs a pot about 60 % larger at 0 % than at 4 %.

Multiples of salary

Some guides use rules like “aim for a certain multiple of salary by a certain age”. These rules are simple to remember. They hide many assumptions about returns, retirement age and spending, so they give a rough first look rather than a plan.

Why the number moves

Small changes in assumptions change the result a great deal:

  • Lifespan: planning for 30 years instead of 25 raises the pot needed.
  • Returns: lower real returns raise it.
  • Inflation: higher inflation than assumed reduces what a fixed income buys.
  • Retirement age: retiring later means more years of saving and fewer years to fund.
  • Health and care costs: these are hard to predict and depend a lot on the country.

Because of this, an estimate is usually treated as a range, and revisited every few years.

Detailed version

The present-value calculation

For a level real income C for n years at real return r:

pot = C × (1 − (1 + r)^−n) ÷ r        (r ≠ 0)
pot = C × n                           (r = 0)

With C = 14,000 and n = 25: r = 0 → 350,000; r = 0.02 → 273,328; r = 0.04 → 218,709. At r = 0.02, n = 20 gives 228,920 and n = 30 gives 313,550. The sensitivity to n is why the planning horizon is set from survival probabilities rather than average life expectancy; the UK Office for National Statistics, for example, publishes the chance of reaching each age (ONS).

Deterministic versus stochastic projections

A deterministic projection uses one return and one inflation rate every year. It is transparent but hides variation. A stochastic (Monte Carlo) projection simulates thousands of return paths from an assumed distribution and reports a range, often as the probability that the money lasts to a given age. Its output is only as good as the assumed distribution, correlations and fees; a model with optimistic inputs gives an optimistic “probability of success”.

Sensitivity analysis

Professionals typically vary one input at a time (return, inflation, retirement age, horizon, spending) and show how the target moves. A short table of these changes often explains more than a single figure.

Health and long-term care

Health and care costs in later life vary widely by country, depending on what public health systems and social care cover, means tests and co-payments. Projections often treat them as a separate reserve because the timing and size are uncertain.

Annuity prices as a benchmark

The price of a lifetime annuityAnnuityA contract that turns a lump sum into a stream of regular payments, often guaranteed for life.Read more about Annuity gives a market-based answer to “what does ¤1 of guaranteed income for life cost at this age?” It includes the insurer’s mortality assumptions, interest rates and margins. Comparing a target pot with the cost of buying the same income from an insurer checks whether the assumed returns and horizon are consistent with market prices.

Common misconceptions

  • “The replacement rate is the same for everyone.” People with low incomes often spend almost all of their income and need a higher rate; people with high savings rates need a lower one.
  • “Average life expectancy is the right horizon.” By definition, half of a group lives beyond its median age at death, so a plan that ends at that age leaves half the group without savings for their final years.
  • “Nominal returns can be compared with spending in today’s money.” Targets in today’s money need real returns.

Why people disagree

Planners disagree on how much spending falls in retirement. One view uses a fairly constant real spending level for the whole retirement. Another view holds that spending often falls with age after an active early retirement, which lowers the target, while a third stresses that late-life care costs can raise spending again. Which pattern applies varies between households and countries, and estimates differ accordingly.
