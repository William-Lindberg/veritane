Mini economy simulator

Move the interest rate, government spending, taxes, oil prices and money growth in a small textbook model and see inflation, unemployment, growth and government debt over 10 simulated years.

A simplified teaching model, not a forecast. The numbers show what this model’s equations give, not what any real economy will do.

What this means

In year 1 the policy rate is 4.0%. With last year’s inflation at 2.0%, the real interest rate is +2.0%, against a neutral real rate of 2% in this model. Together with a spending change of 0.00 and a tax change of 0.00 points of GDP, the output gap moves from 0.0% to 0.0%. Unemployment follows the gap the other way, at 5.0%, and inflation is 2.0%.

By year 10, inflation is 2.0%, unemployment 5.0% and GDP growth 2.0%. The rate stayed at 4.0% the whole time. In this model a fixed rate does not react when inflation moves, so a gap that opens up keeps feeding into inflation; turning on the Taylor rule shows the reacting version.

The government runs a primary deficit of 0.00% of GDP each year and pays an average 4.0% on its debt by year 10. Debt goes from 60.0% to 59.8% of GDP. Debt as a share of GDP falls when nominal GDP grows faster than the interest on the debt plus the deficit, and rises when it grows slower.

These are the model’s own equations with teaching values. Real economies have many more moving parts, and economists disagree on the size of each effect.

Show the maths

Each year t (1 to 10) is computed from year t − 1. Rates are in percent.

1. Policy rate
   fixed:        i_t = 4.00
   Taylor rule:  i_t = max(0, r* + π_{t−1} + 0.5(π_{t−1} − π*) + 0.5·y_{t−1})

2. IS curve (demand; output gap y in % of potential GDP)
   y_t = 0.5·y_{t−1} − 0.5·(i_t − π_{t−1} − r*) + 0.8·ΔG − 0.5·ΔT − 0.05·oil_t

3. Phillips curve with partly anchored expectations
   πᵉ_t = 0.7·π_{t−1} + 0.3·(μ − g*)          (μ − g* = 2.00)
   π_t  = πᵉ_t + 0.3·y_t + 0.1·oil_t

4. Okun's law
   u_t = max(0, u* − y_t / 2)

5. Real GDP growth
   growth_t = g* + (y_t − y_{t−1})

6. Government debt (b in % of GDP)
   r^debt_t = 0.8·r^debt_{t−1} + 0.2·i_t
   d        = d₀ + ΔG − ΔT = 0.00
   b_t = b_{t−1} · (1 + r^debt_t) / ((1 + growth_t)(1 + π_t)) + d

Parameters: r* = 2, π* = 2, u* = 5, g* = 2,
ρ = 0.5, a = 0.5, spending multiplier 0.8, tax multiplier 0.5,
κ = 0.3, θ = 0.3, oil pass-through φ = 0.1, oil output effect ψ = 0.05,
Okun coefficient 2, debt refinanced per year 20%.
Year 0: i₀ = r^debt₀ = r* + π₀ = 4.00; oil_t is 0 in year 1 and 0 afterwards.

The values are illustrative teaching choices picked so the model is easy to follow, not estimates for any country. The structure follows standard textbook models:

  • IS curve, Phillips curve and a policy rule: the “three-equation model” in W. Carlin and D. Soskice, Macroeconomics: Institutions, Instability, and the Financial System (Oxford University Press, 2015).
  • Policy rule with weights 0.5 and 0.5 and a 2 % real rate and target: J. B. Taylor, “Discretion versus policy rules in practice”, Carnegie-Rochester Conference Series on Public Policy 39 (1993), 195–214.
  • Phillips curve with expected inflation: M. Friedman, “The Role of Monetary Policy”, American Economic Review 58(1) (1968), 1–17; and the chapters on the Phillips curve and on aggregate supply in O. Blanchard, Macroeconomics (Pearson) and N. G. Mankiw, Macroeconomics (Worth).
  • Unemployment and the output gap: A. M. Okun, “Potential GNP: Its Measurement and Significance”, Proceedings of the Business and Economic Statistics Section, American Statistical Association (1962).
  • Money growth and inflation (quantity theory with constant velocity, π = μ − g) and the debt-to-GDP equation: the chapters on money and inflation and on government debt in the same textbooks.

Assumptions and limits

  • A simplified teaching model, not a forecast. It describes no real country, and its numbers are not predictions.
  • Every parameter is an illustrative textbook value chosen for teaching, not an estimate. Real economies differ from country to country and over time.
  • One closed economy, one year per step, no exchange rate, no financial markets, no banks, no trade.
  • Expected inflation is last year’s inflation partly pulled towards the inflation implied by money growth (money growth minus potential growth). People in the model do not look ahead.
  • Changes in spending and taxes start in year 1 and stay in place for all 10 years. The oil price change happens once, in year 1.
  • The interest rate on government debt moves a fifth of the way towards the policy rate each year, as a stand-in for debt being refinanced over about five years.
  • Unemployment cannot fall below 0 % and the Taylor-rule rate cannot fall below 0 %; the results say when either limit is reached.
